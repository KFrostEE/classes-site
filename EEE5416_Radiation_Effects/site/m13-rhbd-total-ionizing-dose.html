Module 13

Rad-Hard Circuit Design: Total Ionizing Dose

How a designer working in a commercial process keeps a circuit working while its transistors degrade under total dose: by closing feedback loops around gain that is falling, by matching transistors so that their shifts cancel, by averaging out offsets with clocked switches, by supplying from a helper transistor the extra base current that degraded bipolar transistors draw, by budgeting voltage headroom for thresholds that move, and by drawing transistors whose leakage paths do not exist. Each technique masks degradation up to a limit, and the module sets out where each limit lies.

Prerequisites M06, M08, M09 (M15 for the design margin) Read time ~60 min Sources Deck 09; lecture on Deck 09; Holman, Nowlin et al. and Faccio in the course textbook; IEEE NSREC 2001 Short Course §§II–III

M08 followed total dose from trapped charge to the device parameters it moves: n-channel thresholds fall, p-channel threshold magnitudes rise, bipolar current gain falls, and charge in the isolation oxides opens leakage paths. It ended on the step from device to circuit, which is not a scaling: a circuit can hold its specification through most of its transistors' degradation and then fail abruptly. This module is about designing for that step. Radiation hardening by design (RHBD) is the practice of building radiation-tolerant circuits in a commercial process, where the designer cannot change the oxides or the doping and must work with the topology, the transistor sizes and the layout instead (Deck 09 p. 9). Its single-event counterpart is M14.

§1 explains why hardening moved from the process to the design and states the design philosophy the course follows. §§2–3 treat the operational amplifier: what feedback does with an open-loop gain that falls, and why it cannot do the same for an input offset. §4 treats bipolar current mirrors whose current gain degrades, and §5 CMOS mirrors and the voltage headroom a threshold shift consumes. §6 covers matched gate–source voltages, periodic reconfiguration and chopper stabilization, which average a radiation-induced offset away. §7 turns to layout, the enclosed transistor and the guard ring, and to the FinFET technologies where neither can be drawn. §8 covers silicon on insulator, where a bias on the substrate can compensate charge trapped in the buried oxide, and §9 sets the techniques side by side with what each costs and how a design is checked against the end of the mission.

1 · Hardening by process and by design

A circuit can be made tolerant of total dose in two places. Radiation hardening by process (RHBP) changes the fabrication itself, the oxide growth, the anneals and the hydrogen content, so that less charge is trapped per unit dose (M10). Hardening by design leaves the process alone and changes what is drawn on it. This section explains why the second approach now dominates, and what the course's design philosophy asks of a circuit.

Why design replaced process

Radiation-hardened processes were developed from the 1960s through the 1980s, typically with defence funding (Deck 09 p. 5). The market that sustained them did not grow with the industry: by Deck 09's figure only 0.05 % of the integrated circuits manufactured in 2000 were radiation-hardened, and most foundries have long since left the radiation-hardened market. Commercial process development is driven by smaller features, higher speed, lower supply voltage and lower power, and radiation tolerance is a side effect when it appears at all (Deck 09 p. 8). Silicon on insulator (SOI) is Deck 09's example: it was developed for speed, for removing bulk effects and for lower power, and it happens to suit single-event-tolerant design.

Scaling has in one respect helped. The total-dose threshold shift falls steeply with gate-oxide thickness (M08 §8), and Deck 09 p. 7 records that thinner oxides and better oxide–silicon interfaces have reduced total-dose effects while smaller devices at lower voltages have made single-event effects worse. Holman, in the course textbook, draws the practical consequence: gate oxides in deep-submicron CMOS are about 1 nm thick and trap little charge, so a hardened digital cell library for a bulk process can tolerate several hundred krad(Si) by drawing its n-channel transistors without edges and surrounding them with guard rings (§7), while ignoring the small threshold shifts. The thick isolation oxides, not the gate oxide, now carry most of the trapped charge, and the analog circuits, which depend on precise bias points, carry most of the sensitivity.

The course's design philosophy

Deck 09 p. 11 lists the four total-dose effects a CMOS or BiCMOS designer must expect, each traced to its mechanism in an earlier module:

  • p-channel threshold magnitudes increase, so p-channel transistors become harder to turn on. Trapped holes and, in p-channel devices, interface traps both drive the threshold negative (M08 §1).
  • n-channel threshold magnitudes decrease, so n-channel transistors become harder to turn off: trapped holes drive the threshold negative, while interface traps drive it back positive.
  • Bipolar current gain β falls, because the base current rises through recombination at the oxide over the emitter–base junction (M08 §9).
  • Leakage between adjacent n-type diffusions rises, because positive charge in the field or trench oxide inverts the p-type surface beneath it (M08 §5).

The same slide states the philosophy in one sentence: design the circuit as if it were to be made in a technology with very poor process control. Radiation moves the transistor parameters after the circuit is built, by amounts that differ from transistor to transistor because each transistor's shift depends on its own bias during irradiation (M09 §3). A circuit that already tolerates wide process spread tolerates much of that movement too. The tools the slide names are conservative design, matching, negative feedback, special layout and the right choice of components, and §§2–8 take them in turn.

Choosing the process and the components

Deck 09 p. 9 names the commercial processes a hardened design can use: submicron bulk CMOS, which is inexpensive; BiCMOS, which combines CMOS with bipolar transistors and suits mixed-signal design; SOI; and three-dimensional transistors, FinFETs and gate-all-around devices. Holman weighs them for mixed-signal work. Bulk CMOS has good inherent total-dose tolerance once the leakage paths are removed. SOI is intrinsically harder against single events and prompt dose but still leaks under total dose. BiCMOS adds low-noise, high-frequency bipolar transistors and with them the bipolar gain degradation of §4. Within any process, Holman lists components to avoid as inherently vulnerable to radiation:

  • pinched and diffused resistors;
  • MOS capacitors, replaced by poly–poly or poly–metal capacitors;
  • surface Zener diodes;
  • lateral p-n-p transistors, replaced where needed by a gated lateral p-n-p.

Holman also notes that no design method is optimal for every case: each new design and each new process generation may require the hardening techniques to be reassessed. He states a limit of the compensation circuits he describes (switched-capacitor offset cancellation, charge pumps and bulk–source biasing): they usually compensate for the symptoms of degradation at the circuit level rather than reversing or preventing the degradation in the transistor, so an analog circuit may work normally until a critical dose and then fail catastrophically when the voltage headroom or the gain falls below what it needs. The same reasoning extends to the other circuit-level techniques of §§2–6, which also act on the symptoms; the layout of §7 is different, because it removes a leakage path rather than compensating for it. That behaviour is the threshold effect of M08 §10, produced deliberately, and it is why §9 checks every design against the parameter values expected at the end of the mission.

A mixed-signal chip for a long interplanetary mission is to be built in a commercial 65 nm bulk CMOS process. Which of Deck 09 p. 11's four effects would you expect to dominate the digital logic, and which the analog front end, and why does that difference arise?

In the digital logic, leakage between n-type diffusions and along transistor edges dominates. The gate oxide at that node is very thin (Holman puts deep-submicron gate oxides at about 1 nm), so it traps little charge and the threshold shifts are small; the thick trench isolation still traps enough positive charge to invert the p-type surface beside it, which raises the off-state and supply current (M08 §§5, 8). Digital gates switch between rails, so a few tens of millivolts of threshold shift does not change a logic level. In the analog front end the threshold shifts themselves matter, because analog circuits rest on precise bias points and on matching between transistor pairs. A shift of a few millivolts that differs between the two transistors of a differential pair is an input offset, and the gain that follows multiplies it (§3). Bipolar gain loss would enter as well if the process offered bipolar transistors, which a bulk CMOS process does not. The difference arises because digital circuits tolerate parameter changes up to a noise margin, while analog circuits convert any parameter change, or any difference between matched devices, directly into an error.

2 · Feedback and a falling open-loop gain

An operational amplifier, or op-amp, is a differential amplifier with a very large voltage gain, the open-loop gain AOL, typically 10⁵ or more before irradiation. Total dose lowers it: bipolar input and gain stages lose current gain, and in CMOS the bias currents and the transconductances move as the thresholds and the mobility change. Deck 09 p. 12 states the first design response: negative feedback, which makes the closed-loop gain depend on resistors rather than on the amplifier, so that the open-loop gain can fall a long way before the circuit notices.

Why feedback conceals gain loss

In the non-inverting amplifier of Deck 09 p. 12, a divider of two resistors returns a fraction βf = R1/(R1 + R2) of the output to the inverting input; with R2 = 9 kΩ and R1 = 1 kΩ, βf = 1/10. The amplifier multiplies the difference between its inputs by AOL: Vout = AOL(Vin − βfVout). Solving for the ratio of output to input gives the closed-loop gain:

ACL = AOL1 + AOLβf

When the loop gain is large, the 1 in the denominator is negligible and ACL → 1/βf, which depends only on the resistor ratio. Deck 09 gives the reason in words: feedback continuously adjusts the difference between the amplifier's inputs to keep the output accurate. If AOL falls, the output initially falls with it, the fed-back fraction falls, the difference between the inputs grows, and the larger difference restores the output; the loop settles where the remaining input difference, Vout/AOL, is just large enough to sustain the output. Deck 09's figures reproduce from (13.2). For the gain-of-10 amplifier, an open-loop gain of 10⁵ leaves the closed-loop gain 0.010 % low, 10⁴ leaves it 0.100 % low, and 10³ leaves it 0.990 % low. The slide's shaded band, closed-loop gain within 1 % of ideal, therefore begins at AOL ≈ 10³, and the slide's statement follows: if the feedback resistors are matched, the open-loop gain can degrade by two orders of magnitude from 10⁵ and the circuit remains functional. Below about 100 the error grows quickly; at AOL = 10 the closed-loop gain is 5, half the ideal.

How far the open-loop gain of a precision stage may fall

Where feedback stops working

Deck 09 p. 12 names two limits. Feedback cannot compensate if radiation also shifts the values of the feedback resistors or opens leakage paths, because the closed-loop gain 1/βf is set by those resistors and a leakage current into the feedback node changes the fraction fed back. This is one reason for the component rules of §1, which exclude pinched and diffused resistors. And feedback loses its corrective power when the open-loop gain falls towards unity, because the loop gain then no longer dominates the 1 in (13.1). A third limit lies outside (13.1) altogether: the open-loop gain is set by the bias currents of the amplifier's internal stages, so the same degradation that lowers AOL can also move an internal bias point out of its operating range, and a stage that leaves its operating range stops amplifying at all. §§4–5 treat the bias circuits.

An inverting amplifier uses R1 = 1 kΩ at the input and R2 = 20 kΩ in feedback, for an ideal gain of −20. Its op-amp's open-loop gain falls from 10⁵ to 2000. Find the feedback factor, the gain before and after, and the error after; then say whether the same op-amp in a non-inverting stage of gain +20 would do better or worse.

The feedback factor of an inverting amplifier is the fraction of the output that reaches the inverting input through the divider, with the signal source set to zero: βf = R1/(R1 + R2) = 1/21 = 0.0476. Its closed-loop gain is −(R2/R1) multiplied by the same factor AOLβf/(1 + AOLβf), so the fractional error is again 1/(1 + AOLβf). Before irradiation the loop gain is 10⁵/21 = 4762 and the gain is −19.996 (0.021 % low). After, the loop gain is 2000/21 = 95.2 and the gain is −20 × 95.2/96.2 = −19.79, 1.04 % low. A non-inverting stage of gain +20 has βf = 1/20, a slightly larger loop gain (100) and an error of 0.99 %, so it does marginally better. The difference is that an inverting stage of gain G has a feedback factor of 1/(G + 1), not 1/G: its noise gain, 1/βf, is one more than its signal gain, and the error depends on the noise gain.

3 · Offset: the error feedback does not remove

The input offset voltage VOS of an op-amp is the differential input voltage that would bring its output to zero; it arises from mismatch between the two halves of the input stage. Total dose creates offset where none was designed, because two transistors that were matched when made degrade by different amounts. This section explains why they degrade differently, how a design prevents it, and why feedback, which concealed a falling gain in §2, passes an offset straight to the output.

Why matched transistors drift apart

The charge a transistor traps depends on the field across its oxide during irradiation, and so on its bias (M09 §3). Holman states the consequence for analog design: two matched transistors degrade at different rates if they are biased at different average voltages, and small differences between threshold shifts become failures once a large voltage gain multiplies them. Two transistors drawn identically therefore stay matched under dose only if they spend the mission at the same average gate–source voltage VGS.

Deck 09 pp. 19–20, after Edwards et al. (1999), shows the case. In the cascode amplifier's schematic, diode-connected M6 in the bias branch, its source at ground, sets the gate voltage of the cascode transistor M2 in the output stack, whose source is the drain of the input transistor M1. M2 and M6 are drawn as a matched pair, but they operate at different VGS, so their thresholds diverge with dose. Because M1's drain sits at M6's gate–source voltage less M2's, the divergence moves M1's operating point; in Holman's account the input transistor M1 is eventually driven out of saturation and the amplifier stops working. (The slide's bullet labels M6 the output stage and M2 the bias stage, the reverse of its own schematic and of Holman's figure; the schematic is followed here.) The hardened version sizes and biases every matched transistor so that its VGS is identical, and Deck 09 p. 20 reports that the bias point then does not shift significantly even after several Mrad. The same reasoning applies to a comparator (Deck 09 p. 21): unless the average value of the signal input equals the reference voltage, the two input transistors accumulate different average VGS and the comparator develops an offset as dose accumulates. §6 removes that offset by swapping the inputs.

How an offset reaches the output

An input offset acts as a small voltage source in series with the non-inverting input. The feedback loop treats it exactly as it treats a signal there: it amplifies it by 1/βf, the noise gain, whatever the signal gain of the circuit. Feedback therefore gives no protection against an offset; the loop holds the inputs equal, and an offset is a defined inequality between them. The output error of a stage at a dose combines the two contributions of this section and §2:

|ΔVout| ≤ ε · Vinβf + |VOS|βf
(13.3)
Written for the non-inverting stage, the unity-gain buffer included. ΔVout output error from its ideal value Vin/βf (V) · ε fractional gain error (13.2) · Vin input signal (V) · VOS input offset at the dose of interest (V) · βf feedback factor, so 1/βf is the noise gain (1 for a unity-gain buffer). For an inverting stage the offset term keeps the noise gain, which is one more than the signal gain, but the first term becomes ε(R2/R1)|Vin|, since the ideal output is −(R2/R1)Vin. The first term always reduces the output magnitude; the second has the sign of the offset, which is not known in advance. Adding magnitudes gives the worst case, in which the two errors add. The second term neglects the factor AOLβf/(1 + AOLβf), as the first-order budget does.

Two consequences follow. In a high-gain precision stage the offset term usually dominates, because the gain error is divided by a large loop gain and the offset is not. And the two terms respond to different remedies: a gain error falls when the open-loop gain is raised or preserved (§4), while an offset falls only when the matching is preserved (this section) or when the offset is averaged away (§6).

Allocating an end-of-life error budget

Given A non-inverting stage of ideal gain 50 amplifies a 40 mV signal. Its total output error at the end of the mission may not exceed 10 mV. Characterization shows the open-loop gain falling to 2.0×10⁴ by then. Find the largest input offset the op-amp may develop.

Plan. Use (13.3) with the limit as the total. Compute the gain-error term first, since its inputs are all given, and assign what is left of the budget to the offset term. Worst case: the two terms add.

Ideal output. Vin/βf = 50 × 40 mV = 2.000 V.

Gain error. Loop gain AOLβf = 2.0×10⁴ × (1/50) = 400; ε = 1/401 = 0.2494 %. Output contribution: 2.000 V × 0.002494 = 4.99 mV.

What is left for the offset. 10 mV − 4.99 mV = 5.01 mV at the output.

Refer it to the input. Divide by the noise gain, 1/βf = 50: VOS ≤ 5.01 mV ÷ 50 = 100 µV. Why the noise gain and not some other factor: the offset sits at the non-inverting input, where the loop amplifies it as it does the signal.

VOS ≤ about 100 µV. Check: 100 µV × 50 = 5.00 mV, and 5.00 + 4.99 = 9.99 mV, inside 10 mV. Keeping the factor 400/401 that (13.3) neglects gives 100.5 µV, a 0.25 % difference. The offset allowance is a tenth of a millivolt, a threshold mismatch that two differently biased input transistors can accumulate well before the open-loop gain has fallen to 2.0×10⁴: in a precision stage the matching, not the gain, usually sets the dose the stage survives.

The two input transistors of a comparator are biased so that one spends most of the mission conducting and the other mostly off. At the end of the mission their threshold shifts are −45 mV and −30 mV. What input offset has the comparator developed, what does a gain stage of 200 after it make of that, and what design change would have prevented it?

The offset is the difference between the two threshold shifts, 45 − 30 = 15 mV: a comparator whose input pair is otherwise ideal switches when the differential input overcomes the difference in thresholds. Multiplied by a gain of 200 it is 3.0 V, enough to saturate the output on one side, so small inputs of the wrong sign are no longer resolved. The shifts differ because the transistor held conducting had a positive gate–source voltage, and so a larger oxide field, for longer, and trapped more charge (M09 §3). The remedy is to make the two transistors spend the mission at the same average VGS: either by design, so that the signal input averages to the reference (Deck 09 p. 21), or by periodically swapping the two inputs, so that each transistor serves half the time on each side and both accumulate the same history (§6).

4 · Bipolar current mirrors and degraded gain

A current mirror copies a reference current into one or more output branches, and it sets the bias of nearly every stage in an analog circuit. In a bipolar mirror the copy is made through the transistors' bases, so the base currents are a built-in error, and total dose enlarges that error as the current gain falls. Deck 09 pp. 13–16 treats the simple mirror, the hardened mirror that supplies its base current from a third transistor, and what that change did for a complete voltage regulator.

Assumes: collector, base and emitter currents of a bipolar transistor (M06 §7)

In forward-active operation the collector current is set by the base–emitter voltage, IC = IS exp(VBE/VT), and the base current is IB = IC/β, where β is the common-emitter current gain. The emitter carries both, IE = IC + IB = (β + 1)IB. Total dose raises the base current at a given VBE by adding recombination at the oxide over the emitter–base junction, while the collector current at that VBE is nearly unchanged, so β falls (M08 §9).

The simple mirror

In the simple mirror of Deck 09 p. 13, transistor Q1 has its collector tied to its base, so it carries whatever collector current the reference current leaves it and develops the matching VBE. Q2 shares that VBE, because the two bases and the two emitters are connected; if the transistors are identical, the same VBE gives the same collector current, and Q2's collector current is the output. The reference current, however, must supply three currents at the node where it enters: Q1's collector current and the base currents of both transistors. Kirchhoff's current law at that node gives Iref = IC + 2IC/β, and so:

Iout = Iref1 + 2/β = Iref · ββ + 2
(13.4)

With a pre-irradiation gain of 150 the shortfall is 1.32 %; as β falls the shortfall rises, which is Deck 09 p. 13's statement that the output current becomes smaller than the reference current to compensate for the larger base currents. The output also inherits any mismatch between the gains of Q1 and Q2; the lecture stressed that the two gains need not degrade together.

The course's first-order form. The course works the mirror with Iout ≈ Iref·β/(β + 1), which charges the reference node with one base current instead of two. Use that form where the course asks for its first-order result. Kirchhoff's current law at the reference node gives (13.4), and the difference matters exactly where radiation puts the circuit: the two forms agree at high gain (0.993 against 0.987 at β = 150) and diverge as gain falls (0.962 against 0.926 at β = 25). The β-helper mirror

Deck 09 p. 14's hardened mirror adds transistor Q3, often called a β-helper. Its base connects to the reference node and its emitter drives the joined bases of Q1 and Q2, with its collector at the positive supply. The base current 2IC/β now flows out of Q3's emitter, so the reference node supplies only Q3's base current, which is that emitter current divided by β + 1. Kirchhoff's law at the reference node becomes Iref = IC + 2IC/(β(β + 1)):

The gain at which a mirror falls 5 % short, and what a helper buys

One bias circuit, a whole system's tolerance

Deck 09 pp. 15–16 carries the idea to a product, after Adell et al. (2004). In the LM124 op-amp, adding three transistors to the bias circuit for β compensation of its current mirrors reduced the circuit's sensitivity to gain loss at little cost in area or power and without redesigning the rest of the amplifier. In a linear voltage regulator built around the LM124, the simulated output held its value with the hardened bias circuit to degraded gains well below those that caused failure in the original, and the slide states that this effectively doubled the total-dose tolerance of the whole regulator. The leverage comes from the bias circuit's position: every stage of the amplifier takes its current from it, so a mirror error there propagates everywhere. The alternative is a better transistor rather than a better circuit: Holman notes that SiGe heterojunction bipolar transistors tolerate total dose far better than conventional silicon transistors, and Deck 09 p. 18 reports excellent total-dose hardness in the vertical SiGe n-p-n transistors of some BiCMOS processes.

A bipolar mirror has one reference transistor and three identical output transistors, each copying the reference. Its transistors' gain falls from 150 to 60. What fraction of the reference does each output carry before and after, and why is the multi-output mirror more exposed to gain loss than a single-output one?

The reference node supplies the reference transistor's collector current and four base currents, one for each transistor, so Iref = IC(1 + 4/β) and each output carries 1/(1 + 4/β) of the reference. At β = 150 that is 0.974 (2.60 % short); at β = 60 it is 0.9375 (6.25 % short). A single-output mirror at β = 60 carries 0.968. Every additional output adds one more base current drawn from the same node, so the error grows with the number of outputs, and it grows as 1/β: the gain loss is multiplied by the fan-out. A β-helper on the shared base line divides all of these base currents by β + 1 at once, which is why the LM124's three added transistors could protect every stage.

5 · CMOS mirrors, matching and voltage headroom

In CMOS the mirror copies current through gate voltage rather than base current, and the gates draw no current, so the error of §4 does not arise. What does arise is a threshold shift. Deck 09 p. 17 makes two points about it: matching makes the mirror's current ratio insensitive to the shift, and the shift still consumes voltage until the circuit runs out of it, at which point failure is abrupt. This section explains both and shows where in a mirror the voltage is lost.

Assumes: the MOS transistor in saturation (M06 §4)

Above threshold, a MOS transistor whose drain–source voltage exceeds its overdrive is in saturation and carries ID = (μCox/2)(W/L)Vov², where the overdrive is Vov = VGS − Vth, μ is the channel mobility, Cox the gate capacitance per unit area and W/L the width-to-length ratio. For a p-channel transistor the same holds with source-referred magnitudes: VSG = |Vth| + Vov, and saturation requires VSD ≥ Vov. At a fixed current the overdrive is fixed by W/L and μ, so a threshold shift moves VGS by the full amount of the shift.

Matching masks the shift

In Deck 09 p. 17's mirror, two p-channel transistors share their source (at VDD) and their gate. M1 is diode-connected, with its gate tied to its drain, and the reference current IREF flows from its drain to ground; M2 carries the output. Because both have the same VSG, both have the same overdrive if their thresholds are equal, and the square law gives the ratio of their currents as the ratio of their geometries: IOUT/IREF = (W2/L2) ÷ (W1/L1). The two transistors also share their gate bias during irradiation, so they trap charge at the same rate and their thresholds shift together. The current ratio is then, to first order, independent of dose, as the slide states, and so is a common mobility loss, which cancels from the ratio as well. The residual error comes from what the two do not share: their drain voltages differ, so their channel-length modulation differs, and so, slightly, does the bias each sees during irradiation.

Where the headroom goes

Matching keeps the ratio; it does not keep the voltages. At a fixed reference current, M1 must develop VSG = |Vth| + Vov, so its gate–drain node sits that far below the supply. As total dose makes the p-channel threshold more negative, |Vth| grows and the node falls with it:

VG = VDD − (|Vth| + Vov) ,  VOUT ≤ VDD − Vov

Raising an n-channel threshold on purpose

For n-channel transistors the shift runs the other way: the threshold falls, headroom grows, and the problem becomes turning the transistor off. Deck 09 p. 18 gives a remedy at the cost of speed and gain: a resistance in series with the source raises the effective n-channel threshold through the body effect. The body effect is the increase of threshold with source-to-body reverse bias, Vth = Vth0 + γ(√(2ΦF + VSB) − √(2ΦF)) in Colinge's form (IEEE NSREC 2001 Short Course §II), where γ is the body-effect coefficient and ΦF the body's Fermi potential. With a resistor RS in the source, any current the transistor carries, leakage included, lifts the source by IDRS. That lift reduces VGS and adds reverse bias VSB, which raises the threshold, and both act against the leakage that caused them. The same resistor degenerates the transistor's transconductance and adds resistance to every switching path, which is the cost the slide names. Holman describes the active version: where wells can be isolated, charge pumps and bulk–source bias circuits can drive each transistor's body to restore its threshold, the bulk counterpart of the back-gate bias of §8.

An n-channel cascode mirror sinks current to ground. Each transistor has Vth = 0.55 V and runs at Vov = 0.15 V. Total dose lowers every threshold by 0.12 V. How does the minimum output voltage change, and what failure should the designer look for instead of lost headroom?

The n-channel cascode is the mirror image of (13.7): its output must stay above Vth + 2Vov for both output transistors to stay saturated. Before irradiation that is 0.55 + 0.30 = 0.85 V; after, 0.43 + 0.30 = 0.73 V. The output range widens by the 0.12 V of the shift, because a falling n-channel threshold lowers every stacked gate–source drop. The failure to look for is the opposite one: transistors that should be off conduct, because the gate voltage that held them off is now closer to, or above, the lowered threshold, and because the trench and field oxides at their edges leak (M08 §5). In a mirror carrying a small current, the off-state and edge leakage of the output transistors adds directly to the mirrored current. Edgeless layout (§7) and a series source resistance (this section) address that failure.

6 · Averaging an offset away: reconfiguration and chopping

§3 prevented radiation-induced offset by keeping matched transistors at the same average bias. When the signal does not allow that, the circuit can make the transistors share their history by exchanging their roles periodically, and it can go further and cancel whatever offset remains by averaging. Deck 09 pp. 21–24 takes this from a comparator to a measured chopper-stabilized op-amp.

Periodic reconfiguration

A comparator whose signal input does not average to its reference develops an offset as dose accumulates, because its two input transistors spend the mission at different average VGS (§3). Deck 09 p. 22's remedy, after Edwards et al. (1999), swaps the input transistors and the input signals periodically, using additional transistors as switches. Each input transistor then serves half the time on each input, the average voltage at both inputs is the same, and the two transistors accumulate the same threshold shift. Reconfiguration removes the cause of the offset: it equalizes the irradiation bias.

Chopper stabilization

Deck 09 p. 23 names the clocked version of the same idea dynamic offset cancellation, or input chopping, widely used against offsets from device mismatch and process variation. A pair of switches at the amplifier's input, driven by a clock of frequency fch, exchanges the two inputs every half-period; a second pair at the output exchanges the outputs in step. The signal passes both exchanges and emerges with its sign unchanged. The offset enters after the first exchange and passes only the second, so it emerges with alternating sign: during one half-period the output is the signal plus the offset, during the next the signal minus the offset, as Figure 8 shows. A related family stores the offset instead of modulating it. In an auto-zero amplifier (Kugelstadt), a nulling amplifier alternately cancels its own offset, with its inputs shorted, and measures the main amplifier's, storing each correction on a capacitor. In current designs the main amplifier carries the signal at full bandwidth; older auto-zero amplifiers had to limit the input bandwidth to less than half the auto-zero clock frequency (Kugelstadt), so their cost was the sampling and the clock rather than a filter. Deck 09 p. 33 lists chopper and auto-zero op-amps among the techniques validated against radiation-induced offset. Hardening one effect can expose a circuit to another.

During a ground test an op-amp is irradiated to 300 krad(SiO₂) at 50 rad(SiO₂)/s, and its static offset grows by about 9 mV over the exposure. Does a chopper running at 20 kHz see this offset as constant, and what kind of error could chopping not remove however fast it runs?

The exposure lasts 300 000 ÷ 50 = 6000 s, so the offset changes at an average 9 mV ÷ 6000 s = 1.5 µV/s. Over one 50 µs chopping period it changes by 0.075 nV, negligible against the offset itself, so each period sees a constant offset and (13.8) cancels it; dose accumulates far too slowly for chopping to fall behind. Chopping cannot remove an error that the switches cannot distinguish from the signal. A gain error is the example: if the open-loop gain falls, both half-periods are scaled by the same wrong factor and their average is wrong by that factor, so a gain error is handled by feedback (§2), not by chopping. The same holds for a drifting feedback resistor or a leakage current into the signal path after the output switches.

7 · Layout: edgeless transistors and guard rings

§§2–6 compensated for parameter shifts that the process allows. Leakage through the isolation oxides is different: it is a current path that should not exist, and the designer can remove it by drawing the transistor so that the path has nowhere to form. M08 §§5–6 explained the physics of the two paths and how layout weakens or removes them; this section takes the designer's side: which closed geometry to draw, what it costs, how it behaves in a circuit, and why it cannot be drawn in a FinFET process.

Two paths, two remedies

Deck 09 p. 25 names the two total-dose leakage problems of a bulk n-channel transistor. The first runs from drain to source along the edges of the transistor, where the polysilicon gate crosses from the thin gate oxide onto the thick field or trench oxide; positive charge trapped there inverts the p-type edge and forms a parasitic channel in parallel with the real one. The second runs between adjacent n-type diffusions, from one transistor to its neighbour or to an n-well, under the isolation oxide that separates them. Deck 09 p. 26 gives one remedy for each. An edgeless n-channel layout removes the drain-to-source edge path: the gate is drawn as a closed ring, so source and drain are separated only by polysilicon over thin gate oxide everywhere (Holman). An individual p-type guard ring around each n-channel transistor removes the path to adjacent n-type diffusions: the charge that inverts the lightly doped substrate beside it cannot invert the heavily doped p⁺ ring (Faccio). That protection has a limit, which M08 §6 quantifies: the ring's margin is fixed by its doping while the trapped charge keeps growing, so at a high enough dose even the p⁺ surface inverts. Holman adds that guard rings also suppress latchup, the second reason hardened bulk libraries use them.

Choosing the closed geometry

Nowlin, Bailey, Turfler and Alexander, in the course textbook, distinguish two closed geometries. In the annular transistor, also called re-entrant or edgeless, the gate forms a ring around one diffusion. The usual choice puts the drain inside, because that minimizes the drain area and with it the drain-to-substrate capacitance. The field at the inner electrode varies roughly as 1/r, however, so an inner drain concentrates the field and can become a reliability problem; inside corners of 90° are therefore usually avoided and the ring is drawn with corners bevelled at 45°. Conduction is radial, which constrains the dimensions: the minimum effective width is set by the contact size and spacing inside the ring, the channel length cannot be chosen independently of the width, and adding contacts to carry more current enlarges the inner diffusion and with it the width. In the ringed-source transistor the gate surrounds the source instead and the channel to the drain runs straight across, as in a standard transistor. The width can be made smaller than an annular device allows and the length is unconstrained, but the excess polysilicon around the source adds a large parasitic gate-to-source capacitance. Nowlin et al. draw the ringed source to eliminate the source-to-drain edge path: an edge channel from the drain must still cross thin-oxide gate to reach the enclosed source. M08 §6 gives the same account, beside Deck 08's preference for the fully enclosed transistor. Enclosing the drain instead would place that capacitance between gate and drain, where the transistor's gain multiplies it as a Miller capacitance, so the usual choice is to enclose the source.

Faccio, in the same textbook, reports what an annular library cost at CERN in a quarter-micron process. The transistors are not source–drain symmetric: their output conductance is higher when the central electrode is the drain, which standard circuit models did not represent. The gate must surround a minimum-size drain, which set a lower limit on gate width of about 3.3 µm in that process. And the enclosed transistors and guard rings together cost a density penalty of 1.5 to 3.5 for the digital cells of the library. Deck 09 p. 27, from Atkinson's dissertation, shows the choice a designer makes in 180 nm. Symmetrical edgeless layouts provide matching and closely resemble standard layouts, while asymmetrical layouts, such as the source-enclosed shape, suit transistors whose voltages are DC or mostly static: a transistor whose source and drain never exchange roles has one fixed asymmetry, which can be characterized, whereas a switch conducting both ways would behave differently in each direction. The fully enclosed shape conducts in four directions, and its corner transistors increase the effective W/L.

Deck 09 pp. 29–30, from the same dissertation, shows the result. Standard n-channel layouts developed large current increases at low gate voltage by 1 Mrad, while the slide states that the edgeless layouts eliminate the leakage path; its plot shows one excursion, at 100 krad and low gate voltage, about a decade above the pre-irradiation curve, which the 300 krad and 1 Mrad curves do not repeat. Measured as the change in gate voltage needed to hold a bias current, the shift of the edgeless layouts was an order of magnitude smaller than that of the standard ones, about −5 mV against about −60 mV at the highest dose plotted. The slide marks the edgeless data as varying strongly with the test setup and does not say what the residual shift is.

The aspect ratio an annular transistor can provide

Given A square annular n-channel transistor whose inner drain is 1.0 µm on a side, with a gate ring of drawn length L = 0.5 µm. The course's rule takes the effective width as the perimeter of the line running through the middle of the gate ring (M08 §6). Find the width and W/L; then the aspect ratio of a minimum device with an assumed 0.6 µm drain and L = 0.25 µm, and how low W/L can go as the ring is lengthened.

Plan. The midline of a square ring of inner side a and width L is a square of side a + L, so W = 4(a + L) and W/L = 4a/L + 4. Evaluate, then let L grow and test the rule against radial conduction, which it approximates.

Width. W = 4 × (1.0 + 0.5) µm = 6.0 µm.

Aspect ratio. W/L = 6.0/0.5 = 12.0.

Minimum device. W = 4 × (0.6 + 0.25) µm = 3.4 µm, so W/L = 13.6. The 0.6 µm drain is an assumed minimum that can hold a contact, not a value from the sources; Faccio reports the resulting width limit, about 3.3 µm, for a quarter-micron process.

Long rings by the rule. W/L = 4a/L + 4 falls towards 4 as L grows: 6.4 at L = 1 µm, 4.6 at 4 µm, 4.12 at 20 µm for the 0.6 µm drain, because lengthening the ring also lengthens its midline.

Long rings by radial conduction. The rule treats the ring as a straight channel as wide as its midline, which holds only while L is small compared with a. In a ring the current flows radially. For a circular ring of inner radius Rin and outer radius Rout, a thin annulus of radius r and width dr has resistance Rsdr/(2πr) for a channel sheet resistance Rs; summing from Rin to Rout gives Rsln(Rout/Rin)/2π, so W/L = 2π/ln(Rout/Rin). It has no floor. With the circle inscribed in the 0.6 µm drain (Rin = 0.3 µm, Rout = 0.3 µm + L) it gives 4.3 at L = 1 µm, 2.4 at 4 µm and 1.5 at 20 µm. A square ring conducts somewhat more through its corners but follows the same trend.

W = 6.0 µm and W/L = 12 for the first device. By the course's rule W/L cannot fall below 4, but that floor belongs to the approximation, not to the geometry: radial conduction lets the aspect ratio keep falling, only logarithmically, as the ring lengthens. Check: for L ≪ Rin, ln(Rout/Rin) ≈ L/Rin, and the radial result becomes 2πRin/L, the circumference over the length, which is the rule's idea applied to a circle. A low aspect ratio is therefore possible but expensive in area: W/L ≈ 1.5 takes a ring about 40 µm across around a 0.6 µm drain. A weak transistor, such as a long-channel load or a low-current bias device, is impractical to draw annular; it uses the ringed-source shape or a series stack.

Where edgeless layout ends

Deck 09 p. 31, after Chatterjee et al. (2014), records two facts about advanced technologies. Many modern FinFET structures have shown total-dose leakage currents comparable to those of older bulk CMOS, and geometric constraints make edgeless layouts impossible below 65 nm, which limits their use in modern hardened designs. In a FinFET each fin is a thin vertical slab of silicon rising through shallow trench isolation (STI), and the gate wraps over it; a ring-shaped gate around a contacted drain cannot be formed from such fins. Deck 09 p. 34 states the consequence, that STI sidewalls dominate the leakage, and summarizes the measurements of Bonaldo et al. (2024) on 16 nm silicon FinFETs. Positive charge trapped deep in the STI sidewalls and negative charge in the upper STI corners make the drain-to-source leakage of n-channel FinFETs rise steadily and then plateau at very large doses, and make the transconductance increase up to 3–10 Mrad and then decrease. Only the leakage depends on the number of fins: at 500 Mrad it rose about tenfold in two-fin transistors against about 2.5-fold in twenty-fin ones, while the transconductance change was practically the same for all. The authors attribute this to the lateral STI beside the outermost fins, which is larger than the STI between fins, so a device with few fins has a larger share of exposed edge fins (the paper's abstract says inter-fin STI; its body and conclusion say lateral). Deck 09 p. 34 adds fin spacing as a factor. The designer's lever in a FinFET process is therefore the choice of fin count, and on the slide's account fin spacing, among the devices the process offers; the leakage itself is set by the quality of the isolation oxide, which the process controls (M08 §8). Deck 09 p. 36 draws the conclusion that design and process must now work together.

A designer needs a 1:2 current mirror built from annular n-channel transistors with the same gate length. Using the course's width rule, what inner drain size gives one transistor twice the aspect ratio of a reference device with a 1.0 µm drain and a 0.5 µm ring, and why is that not the better design?

At equal length, twice the aspect ratio needs twice the width: 4(a₂ + 0.5) = 2 × 4(1.0 + 0.5) = 12.0 µm, so a₂ = 2.5 µm, a drain more than six times the area of the reference's. That device matches the reference poorly. The course's rule is an approximation whose error lies in the corners: both devices have four corners, but the corners are a larger fraction of the small device's width, so the real current ratio is not exactly 2. The drain-inside asymmetry and the drain capacitance also differ between the two. The better design draws the output as two copies of the reference transistor connected in parallel: every unit then has the same geometry, the same corners and the same irradiation bias, so the ratio is 2 to the extent the units match, which is the same principle as the matched gate–source voltages of §3.

8 · Silicon on insulator: back-gate bias

In silicon on insulator, each transistor sits in a thin film of silicon on a buried oxide (BOX), with the silicon substrate beneath. The buried oxide isolates every transistor dielectrically, so the leakage between adjacent n-type diffusions of §7 cannot form, and Holman notes that SOI processes need no guard rings. The buried oxide is, however, a thick oxide under every channel, and the charge it traps opens a back channel along the bottom of the film (M08 §7). The lecture on Deck 06 raised a remedy that leaves the oxide alone: change the potential behind it, using the substrate as a second gate, and monitor and adjust that potential as the charge builds up. This section quantifies the remedy for a fully depleted device.

Given A fully depleted SOI n-channel transistor with a 5 nm front gate oxide, a 20 nm silicon film and a 145 nm buried oxide, operated with the back surface depleted at VB = 0. Irradiation traps a net 5×10¹¹ cm⁻² of positive charge at the buried-oxide interface. Find the coupling coefficient, the shift of the front threshold, and the back-gate bias that restores it.

Plan. Compute the three capacitances per unit area, combine them in (13.11), then apply (13.12). Keep every capacitance in F/cm², so thicknesses go in cm.

Capacitances. Cox = 3.45×10⁻¹³/5×10⁻⁷ = 6.90×10⁻⁷ F/cm²; CSi = 1.04×10⁻¹²/2.0×10⁻⁶ = 5.20×10⁻⁷ F/cm²; CBOX = 3.45×10⁻¹³/1.45×10⁻⁵ = 2.38×10⁻⁸ F/cm².

Coupling. k = (5.20×10⁻⁷ × 2.38×10⁻⁸) ÷ (6.90×10⁻⁷ × (5.20×10⁻⁷ + 2.38×10⁻⁸)) = 1.237×10⁻¹⁴ ÷ 3.752×10⁻¹³ = 0.0330. Why it is small: the thick buried oxide is the smallest capacitance in the series, and it dominates.

Back-threshold shift. qΔNBOX = 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ × 5×10¹¹ = 8.01×10⁻⁸ C/cm²; ΔVtb = −8.01×10⁻⁸ ÷ 2.38×10⁻⁸ = −3.37 V.

Front-threshold shift. ΔVtf = 0.0330 × (−3.37 V) = −0.111 V.

Compensation. ΔVB,comp = −3.37 V: moving the back-gate bias from 0 to −3.37 V raises the front threshold by k × 3.37 V = 0.111 V, cancelling the shift.

k = 0.033; the front threshold falls 111 mV, and a back-gate bias of about −3.4 V restores it. Check: the sign is right, since trapped holes lower an n-channel threshold and a more negative back gate raises it, and the compensating bias equals the back-channel shift, independent of k. Because the charge keeps growing with dose, the compensation must grow with it, which is why the lecture paired the bias with monitoring.

Biasing the back surface into accumulation is the limiting case. Musseau and Ferlet-Cavrois note that with the back surface accumulated the fully depleted transistor works like a partially depleted one and no longer depends on the buried-oxide charge; the bias needed to hold accumulation grows with the trapped charge, however, and the device gives up the coupling that defines fully depleted operation. Deck 09 p. 35 brings the question to current technology. In 22 nm fully depleted SOI (22FDX) the slide reports a stable threshold and a reproducible total-dose response across arrays, with biasing strategies still critical (NSREC 2025), and p. 33 lists a 22 nm FDSOI RISC-V microcontroller demonstrated as radiation-tolerant. In three-dimensional sequential integration, where transistor layers are stacked with an oxide between them, trapped charge in the middle oxide couples one layer to the next, and the layer-to-layer shift depends on bias (Toguchi et al., 2022). The slide's design implications are to isolate the supply rails, decouple the transistor bodies and budget for the vertical coupling, the coupling that (13.11) describes with a lower transistor layer acting as the back gate.

An ultrathin-BOX fully depleted device has a front oxide with an equivalent thickness of 1.2 nm, a 7 nm film and a 25 nm buried oxide. For the same 5×10¹¹ cm⁻² of interface charge as the example, find k, the front-threshold shift and the compensating back-gate bias. Which of the three thinner layers is responsible for the smaller front shift, and what does the thin buried oxide itself buy?

Cox = 3.45×10⁻¹³/1.2×10⁻⁷ = 2.88×10⁻⁶ F/cm², CSi = 1.04×10⁻¹²/7×10⁻⁷ = 1.49×10⁻⁶ F/cm², CBOX = 3.45×10⁻¹³/2.5×10⁻⁶ = 1.38×10⁻⁷ F/cm². Then k = (1.49×10⁻⁶ × 1.38×10⁻⁷) ÷ (2.88×10⁻⁶ × 1.62×10⁻⁶) = 0.044. The back-threshold shift is −8.01×10⁻⁸ ÷ 1.38×10⁻⁷ = −0.58 V, so the front threshold moves by 0.044 × (−0.58) = −25.5 mV, and −0.58 V of back-gate bias restores it. The front shift is 4.4 times smaller than the example's, but the buried oxide is not the reason. Combining (13.11) and (13.12), ΔVtf = −(qΔN/Cox) × CSi/(CSi + CBOX): CBOX cancels to first order, because a thinner buried oxide shrinks the back-channel shift and raises k in the same proportion. Thinning only the buried oxide of the example to 25 nm gives k = 0.158 and −91.7 mV, barely less than −111 mV; thinning only the front oxide and film gives −27.4 mV. The thinner front oxide does the work. Musseau and Ferlet-Cavrois found the same in measurement: a SIMOX buried oxide five times thinner shifted five times less, its k rose by the same ratio, and the thin-BOX transistor was about as sensitive as the standard one. What the thin buried oxide buys is the compensating bias, which is set by CBOX alone: −0.58 V instead of −3.37 V, small enough to generate on chip.

9 · Designing to the end of the mission

The circuit techniques of §§2–6 hide degradation rather than prevent it, and Holman's warning from §1 applies to them: the circuit may work normally until a critical dose and then fail abruptly. Layout (§7) removes a leakage path and back-gate bias (§8) cancels a charge, but the gate-oxide shifts and the growing charge they leave behind still accumulate. A measurement at one dose therefore says little about the margin left, and a design is judged by checking each of its conditions with the parameter values expected at the end of the mission. This section states that procedure and sets the techniques side by side.

End-of-life parameters and margin

The procedure has four steps, each drawing on an earlier module:

  1. Characterize the transistors of the process under total dose at worst-case bias (M15 §6): threshold shifts of both polarities, current gain, leakage and mobility against dose, at the dose rate the mission will see, since the two trapped-charge components respond differently to it (M15 §§3–4).
  2. Choose the design dose: the mission dose at the part's location multiplied by the radiation design margin, the ratio of failure level to specification (M15, (15.1)).
  3. Evaluate every circuit condition with the parameters at the design dose: the gain error (13.2) and the error budget (13.3), the mirror ratios (13.4) and (13.5), the headroom conditions (13.6) and (13.7), and the leakage the layout leaves.
  4. Simulate the circuit with transistor models extracted from irradiated devices, which M08 §10 described, because devices in a circuit interact and each degrades according to its own bias.

The space-systems guest lecture (Airbus, 9 Nov) described derating for wear-out in highly scaled parts: keeping each part inside a safe operating area, with bias, temperature, performance and duty cycle derated to meet the lifetime objective. The end-of-life check is the total-dose counterpart, in this page's comparison rather than the lecture's: the circuit is designed to work with the gain, threshold and leakage it will have at the design dose, not with their data-sheet values, which M15 §8 treats as parameter-delta design margins.

The table separates two kinds of technique. Feedback, matching and chopping compensate for changes in the transistors that the circuit can absorb, and each fails at a threshold that the end-of-life check must locate. Layout and back-gate bias remove or cancel a physical path for leakage or charge, and each depends on a geometry or a bias that advanced processes may not permit. A hardened design usually needs both kinds, chosen against the effect that dominates each block: leakage in digital logic, offsets and headroom in analog circuits, gain in bipolar bias networks.

The bias network of the example in §4 flies on a mission whose specified dose at the part is 40 krad(Si). The project requires a radiation design margin of 2, and characterization at worst-case bias gives a transistor gain of 45 at 80 krad(Si). Does the simple-mirror network pass its 5 % limit, and what would you check next?

The design dose is the specification times the margin, 40 × 2 = 80 krad(Si), so the circuit is checked with β = 45. From (13.4) each simple mirror carries 1/(1 + 2/45) = 0.957 of its reference, 4.26 % short, inside the 5 % limit: the network passes, with the gain still above the failure value of 38 found in §4. The margin is thin, though, so the next checks are the ones that the single number hides: whether the gain was measured at the dose rate the mission will see, since a bipolar process may show enhanced low-dose-rate sensitivity (M15 §7); whether the transistors of the network are biased alike, since unequal bias makes their gains degrade differently and adds mismatch to the base-current error; and whether any mirror has several outputs, since each output adds a base current (§4). Replacing the simple mirrors by β-helper mirrors would remove the question.

Summary

  • RHBD: hardening by layout, topology and component choice in a commercial process (Deck 09 p. 9); hardened processes are rare (0.05 % of ICs in 2000). Design as if process control were very poor (p. 11). Four effects: p-channel |Vth| up, n-channel |Vth| down, β down, n⁺-to-n⁺ leakage up. Avoid diffused and pinched resistors, MOS capacitors, surface Zeners, lateral p-n-p. Circuit-level compensation masks degradation until a possibly abrupt failure; layout removes paths.
  • Feedback: ACL = AOL/(1 + AOLβf) (13.1); error 1/(1 + AOLβf) (13.2), always low. Gain-of-10 stage: within 1 % for AOL ≥ 990, so two decades of loss from 10⁵ are tolerated. Fails with resistor drift, leakage, or loop gain near 1.
  • Offset: matched transistors at different average VGS shift differently; design for identical VGS. Offset reaches the output times the noise gain 1/βf; worst case |ΔVout| ≤ εVin/βf + |VOS|/βf (13.3).
  • Bipolar mirror: Iout = Iref/(1 + 2/β) (13.4), 5 % short at β = 38; course's first-order form β/(β + 1). β-helper: 1/(1 + 2/(β(β + 1))) (13.5), effective gain ≈ β², one extra VBE. LM124 bias fix: three transistors, regulator tolerance doubled. N outputs: 2 → N + 1.
  • CMOS mirror: ratio (W₂/L₂)/(W₁/L₁) independent of dose to first order; gate node VDD − (|Vth| + Vov) falls with dose, M2 needs only VOUT ≤ VDD − Vov (13.6); cascode VOUT ≤ VDD − (|Vth| + 2Vov) (13.7). The course writes VOUT,min = VDD − (|Vth| + Vov). Source resistor raises n-channel Vth through the body effect.
  • Chopping: swap inputs (reconfiguration) or modulate–amplify–demodulate–filter; [(VIN + VOS) + (VIN − VOS)]/2 = VIN (13.8). Measured 9.82 mV static against 50 µV chopped; residual (1 − r)VOS, factor 1/(1 − r) (13.9). Ripple fundamental (4/π)VOS/√(1 + (fch/fc)²), but the peak is π²/8 larger, ≈ (π/2)VOSfc/fch (13.10). Costs bandwidth; cannot fix gain error.
  • Layout: edgeless gate ring removes the edge path; p⁺ guard ring removes the path to neighbours up to a dose and helps against latchup. Annular: drain inside, 45° corners; midline rule W = 4(a + L) for short rings, radial 2π/ln(Rout/Rin) for long ones, so low W/L costs area; ringed-source: enclose the source. ELTs asymmetric; ~3.3 µm minimum W at 0.25 µm; density ×1.5–3.5. Edgeless gate shift ~10× smaller. Impossible below 65 nm; FinFET STI sidewalls dominate.
  • FD-SOI: k = CSiCBOX/(Cox(CSi + CBOX)) (13.11); BOX charge ΔVtb = −qΔN/CBOX, ΔVtf = kΔVtb, compensate with ΔVB = ΔVtb (13.12). Front shift ≈ −qΔN/Cox: a thin BOX shrinks the compensating bias, not the front shift. Accumulated back surface decouples.
  • End of life: design dose = mission dose × RDM; check every condition with parameters at that dose, not data-sheet values.
