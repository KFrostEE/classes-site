Module 05

The Three Effects: A Taxonomy

Two questions sort every radiation effect in microelectronics: what the deposited energy did, and how many particles it took. The answers generate total ionizing dose, displacement damage and single-event effects, and decide which test source, which unit and which countermeasure apply.

Prerequisites M04 Read time ~30 min Sources Deck 03 pp. 9–10, 16–19, 23–27, 30–34, 36–38, 45; Lectures 4–5

M04 ended with two quantities that measure the same event in different currencies. Absorbed dose measures the energy a particle leaves behind that goes into liberating charge; non-ionizing energy loss measures the much smaller share that goes into moving atoms off their lattice sites. Both are per unit mass, both are computed from the same stopping power, and both describe one particle crossing one piece of silicon. What neither says is what the device does about it.

That question has a second variable, and it is the one this module adds. Energy deposited by a particle is either spread thinly over the whole part by a great many particles, in which case the device responds to the running total and drifts slowly, or it is concentrated by one particle into one micrometre-scale volume, in which case the device responds to that single event and nothing about the running total matters. The first is a cumulative effect and is measured in dose; the second is a single-event effect and is measured as a probability per unit fluence. A part can be perfectly tolerant of one and destroyed by the other.

Crossing those two questions produces the classification the rest of the course follows. This module states it, places each of the three named effects inside it, describes what each does to a device in enough detail to tell them apart, and then shows where the divisions blur — because a single proton routinely produces all three at once. Parts III, IV and V then take one cell each: total ionizing dose in M06 through M10, single-event effects in M11, displacement damage in M12.

1 · The two questions

A particle that enters a device deposits energy through the mechanisms of M01: it ionizes, it excites, and in a small minority of encounters it strikes a nucleus hard enough to move it. The device response is determined by two properties of that deposition, and only two.

The first is where the energy went. Energy transferred to atomic electrons breaks bonds and creates free electron–hole pairs; this is the ionizing channel, it carries almost all of the energy, and its measure is absorbed dose. Energy transferred to whole nuclei displaces atoms from lattice sites and leaves vacancies and interstitials behind; this is the non-ionizing channel, it carries a small fraction of one per cent of the energy for most particles, and its measure is non-ionizing energy loss. The two produce entirely different damage. Liberated charge either recombines, is swept away, or is trapped in an insulator; displaced atoms form defect states inside the semiconductor bandgap that persist regardless of what the charge does.

The second is how many particles were needed. A cumulative effect requires the deposition of many particles to accumulate before any device parameter moves measurably, so the device degrades gradually and monotonically with exposure, and the controlling variable is the integrated quantity — dose, or displacement damage dose. A single-event effect requires exactly one particle, because that particle deposits enough energy in a small enough volume to exceed a circuit threshold by itself. Its controlling variable is not an integral but the energy density along one track, which is the linear energy transfer of M04 §2, and the observable is a rate: how often such a particle arrives and lands somewhere that matters.

The two axes are independent, and that independence is the practical content of the classification. Ionizing against non-ionizing is a statement about the particle and the target — fixed once the species, its energy and the material are fixed. Cumulative against single-particle is a statement about the circuit — the same track in the same silicon is a negligible dose contribution and a catastrophic single event, depending only on whether a node nearby happens to hold state.

2 · Total ionizing dose

Total ionizing dose is the accumulated ionizing energy deposited per unit mass, and the effect named after it is the drift in device behaviour that accumulates alongside it. M03 §1 defined the quantity: one rad is 100 erg per gram, one gray is one joule per kilogram, and one gray is 100 rad. The material must always be named, because the energy absorbed per unit mass differs between silicon and its oxide, so a dose is quoted as rad(Si) or rad(SiO₂).

The mechanism is charge that does not leave. Ionizing radiation liberates electron–hole pairs everywhere in the device, at the cost of 3.6 eV per pair in silicon and 17 eV per pair in silicon dioxide. In the silicon, those carriers are mobile, the material is conductive, and they recombine or are swept out within nanoseconds; nothing persists. In the oxide, electrons remain mobile but holes are slower by six to eight orders of magnitude — they move only by hopping between localized states — and the material offers no path to ground. A fraction of the holes therefore survives, transports slowly toward the silicon interface under whatever field the oxide carries, and is captured at defect sites near that interface. The charge stays there, and a device sees it as a permanent change in the potential its gate applies.

Two device consequences follow, and they are separable because they have opposite signs. Trapped charge in the oxide is net positive, so it shifts a transistor's threshold voltage negative whether the transistor is n-channel or p-channel. Defect states created at the silicon–oxide interface exchange charge with the silicon and are negatively charged at threshold in an n-channel device, shifting its threshold positive. The two build up on different timescales and anneal differently, so the net shift of a real device is a sum of two competing terms. M07 develops both mechanisms and the measurement that separates them.

Any ionizing radiation produces total ionizing dose, which makes it the effect with the broadest set of causes: photons, electrons, protons, neutrons through their charged recoils, and heavy ions all contribute. Laboratory testing exploits that breadth by choosing a source that produces dose and nothing else — a cobalt-60 gamma source or a 10 keV X-ray tube deposits dose without displacing a significant number of atoms and without any single photon depositing enough charge to upset a node, so a total-dose test isolates one variable.

3 · Displacement damage

Displacement damage is the accumulation of stable defects in the semiconductor lattice, produced by the non-ionizing share of the deposited energy. Deck 03 states the scale plainly: only a very small fraction of the deposited energy goes into displacement, and the applications affected are solar cells, charge-coupled devices and bipolar devices.

The mechanism begins with a single elastic or inelastic nuclear collision. An incident particle strikes a lattice atom and transfers enough energy to break it free of its site. That atom is the primary knock-on atom, and the vacancy it leaves together with the interstitial it becomes is a Frenkel pair. The threshold for the process in silicon is 21 eV; below it the atom returns to its site. A primary knock-on atom of 50 keV, which Deck 03 gives as typical of a 1 MeV neutron or an energetic proton, carries far more than the threshold, so it displaces further atoms along its own path and produces a cascade of secondary displacements clustered in a region tens of nanometres across. About 90% of the resulting Frenkel pairs recombine within the cascade; the surviving 10% migrate and settle into stable configurations — vacancy–impurity complexes, divacancies — that sit as energy levels inside the bandgap.

Defect levels inside the bandgap act as generation and recombination centres, and their device consequences follow from that one property. They shorten minority-carrier lifetime, which reduces bipolar current gain and reduces the collection efficiency of a solar cell. They generate carriers thermally in a depleted region, which raises dark current in any imaging device and produces hot pixels. They trap and re-emit signal charge on its way through a charge-coupled device, which degrades charge transfer efficiency and smears an image along the transfer direction. None of these mechanisms involves an insulator, and none is affected by anything done to the gate oxide.

The measure is non-ionizing energy loss, in MeV·cm²/g, and the accumulated quantity is the displacement damage dose: the product of particle fluence and NIEL, which is the non-ionizing counterpart of absorbed dose. Its usefulness is that damage scales with that product, so a measurement made with one particle at one energy transfers to another — within the limits M04 §6 records for gallium arsenide above roughly 40 MeV protons. Protons and neutrons dominate the displacement budget in practice because they have both large nuclear cross-sections and high fluences in the environments of M02.

4 · Single-event effects

A single-event effect is a disruption caused by one particle. Deck 03 states the chain in one line: a single energetic particle deposits charge, and that charge upsets the circuit. Nothing accumulates, the device was not degraded beforehand and is not degraded afterwards in the non-destructive case, and the probability that it happens again in the next second is exactly what it was in the last one.

The particle loses energy by ionization along its path and leaves a dense column of electron–hole pairs behind it — Deck 03 calls it a plasma track — with the linear energy transfer of M04 §2 setting how dense. Where that track crosses a reverse-biased junction, the junction field sweeps the carriers out as a current pulse. The circuit node connected to that junction receives a quantity of charge it did not expect, and its voltage moves.

Whether anything follows is decided by a comparison between two charges. The collected charge is what the track delivers to the node, and it is set by the particle: its LET, its angle, and how much of its track lies inside the volume from which the junction can collect. The critical charge is the smallest collected charge that flips the node, and it is set by the circuit: the node capacitance, the supply voltage and the drive strength of the transistors holding the state. Deck 03 gives it as below 50 fC — equivalent to 3×10⁵ electrons — in modern circuits that have not been hardened. An upset occurs when the collected charge exceeds the critical charge.

Critical charge is a property of the technology and the circuit, not of the ion. The same node has the same critical charge under every beam; changing the ion changes the collected charge on the other side of the comparison.

The observable is not a threshold voltage but a rate, and the quantity that carries it is the cross-section: the number of events divided by the particle fluence that produced them, in cm² per device. It has the dimensions of an area because it is one — the effective target area a particle must hit to cause the event. A cross-section measured as a function of LET is the standard characterisation of a part's single-event response. M11 §6 develops it, and §8 the conversion from cross-section and environment to an in-orbit error rate.

Heavy ions cause single-event effects by direct ionization, because their LET is large enough to exceed a critical charge on its own. Protons usually cannot: Deck 03 states that direct proton ionization is rarely sufficient. Protons matter instead through nuclear interaction — a proton that strikes a silicon nucleus produces recoils and alpha particles whose LET is far higher than its own, and those secondaries cause the event. This is why proton fluence predicts upset rates in the proton-dominated environments of the inner belt and a solar event, where heavy ions are far too rare to account for the observed rate, and it is treated in M01 §7.

5 · Soft errors and hard errors

Single-event effects divide again, on whether the device survives. Deck 03 draws the line by whether the error can be removed by rewriting the circuit's state.

A soft error is a corruption of stored or propagating information with no damage to the device. It arises when a particle deposits enough energy to change the logic state of a circuit node — a bit flip in an SRAM cell or a register, or a transient pulse in combinational logic that a latch captures. The silicon is unharmed, and reprogramming the circuit into its correct state repairs it completely. What makes soft errors serious is rate rather than severity: if the rate is high enough, the system degrades and the mission can fail even though no part is damaged.

A hard error is an event that rewriting the circuit's state does not recover. Deck 03 lists the types: latchup, snapback, single-event burnout and single-event gate rupture. Each is a mechanism by which the deposited charge triggers a self-sustaining or destructive current path rather than merely displacing a stored voltage — a parasitic four-layer structure in the CMOS well that latches on and draws supply current until power is removed, a current density in a power device high enough to destroy it thermally, or a conductive path across a thin gate oxide that lets the applied field break it down. Deck 03 places hard errors predominantly in high-power devices, where the stored energy available to feed the event is large; its burnout example is a power MOSFET, and its gate-rupture examples are a power MOSFET and thin-oxide capacitors. In each, the controlling variables are the LET of the ion and the voltage applied across the struck structure — gate voltage for rupture, joined by the drain voltage in a power MOSFET, and drain voltage for burnout — and the threat rises with both, the dependence M11 §10 develops.

The two ends of that list differ in how final they are, and the difference matters for system design. Gate rupture and burnout destroy the device outright. Latchup and snapback are self-sustaining current states rather than damage: removing power collapses the parasitic conduction and the part returns to service, which is why spacecraft carry latchup-detection circuits that cycle power on an over-current. They become permanent only when the sustained current causes thermal damage first. Deck 03 groups all four as hard errors on the criterion that matters for the classification — none is corrected by reprogramming — and that is the criterion used here.

The distinction governs which countermeasures are available. A soft error can be tolerated by design: redundancy, error-correcting codes and voting schemes detect and repair the corrupted state, and the fault never reaches the system. A hard error must be prevented, because once it has occurred there is nothing left to correct — which moves the mitigation from the architecture into the process and the layout. Error-correcting codes address single-event upsets and have no effect on parameter drift from total ionizing dose, which changes no stored value and corrupts no codeword.

6 · Where the divisions blur

The classification sorts effects cleanly. It does not sort particles, and a single particle commonly produces more than one of the three.

A proton crossing a device deposits ionizing energy continuously along its path, so it contributes to total ionizing dose. It transfers a small non-ionizing share to nuclei it passes, so it contributes to displacement damage. And it may undergo a nuclear interaction that produces a short, heavily ionizing recoil, so it may cause a single-event effect. All three at once, from one particle, and the reason the three are nonetheless treated separately is that each has a different controlling variable, a different accumulation law and a different countermeasure.

The bottom-right cell of Figure 1 is the classification's genuine edge case. A single nuclear interaction produces one displacement cascade, and in a transistor of conventional size that is far too small to move any terminal quantity; in a nanoscale transistor one cascade can be enough (M12 §7). In an imaging array it is not, because each pixel is an independently readable, very low-current device. Deck 03 shows the signature and its fluence dependence together. A pixel array's dark-current distribution carries a high-energy tail produced by very infrequent but large nuclear reaction events — one pixel at a time, which is single-particle displacement damage — alongside a bulk that represents damage accumulated from many small interactions. As the number of primary proton–silicon interactions per pixel rises, the distribution approaches a Gaussian: the tail is most prominent at low fluence and the central limit theorem absorbs it as exposure continues. The single-particle and cumulative contributions are therefore separable in one histogram, and they separate best early.

Two further qualifications matter later. Total ionizing dose depends not only on how much dose was delivered but on how fast, because the trapped charge responsible anneals on its own timescale and the interface states build on another; a bipolar part can be degraded more by a small dose delivered slowly than by a large dose delivered quickly. And a hard error can require both classes at once — a gate oxide weakened by accumulated dose ruptures under a strike that an undamaged oxide would have survived. In both cases the classification still holds; what fails is the assumption that the cumulative and single-event budgets can be evaluated independently.

7 · What the classification decides

The taxonomy earns its place because each cell implies a different engineering programme. Choosing the wrong cell for a failure produces a countermeasure that cannot work.

The consequence worth carrying forward is that hardness is never a single number. A part qualified to a total-dose level has been shown to survive accumulated charge trapping and has been shown nothing about its response to one heavy ion. A part with a measured heavy-ion cross-section may still drift out of specification after a year in orbit. Displacement damage will be invisible in both tests if the part contains no minority-carrier device. Each cell of Figure 1 requires its own evidence, and hardness assurance is the discipline of assembling all of it; M15 develops that discipline for total ionizing dose.

A part is qualified to 100 krad(Si) and flown in an orbit where it accumulates 5 krad(Si) per year. Two years in, its imaging sensor shows rising dark current and its data bus shows occasional corrupted words. Which effects are these, and does the total-dose margin bear on either?

Rising dark current in an imager is displacement damage: nuclear collisions from trapped and solar protons have left stable defect levels in the bandgap of the silicon, and those levels generate carriers thermally in the depleted pixel. It is cumulative and non-ionizing. Total ionizing dose also raises dark current in a charge-coupled device, through interface traps rather than lattice defects, so the two contributions must be separated by which dominates for that sensor and orbit — but the total-dose margin bounds neither, because 100 krad(Si) is a statement about trapped charge in insulators and says nothing about lattice damage.

Corrupted words on a bus are single-event effects: individual particles depositing more than the critical charge on individual nodes. They are not cumulative, so they would have appeared at the same rate in the first week as in the second year, and the 10× total-dose margin is irrelevant to them entirely. The rate depends on the LET spectrum at that orbit and the part's cross-section, neither of which a total-dose qualification measures.

Where this leaves us

Every radiation effect in this course is located by two answers. Ionizing energy that accumulates as trapped charge in an insulator is total ionizing dose; non-ionizing energy that accumulates as lattice defects is displacement damage; ionizing energy concentrated by one particle into one sensitive volume is a single-event effect, soft if the state can be rewritten and hard if the device is damaged. The three are produced by overlapping sets of particles and often by the same particle, but they have different controlling variables, different accumulation laws, different test sources and different countermeasures, and no measurement of one bounds another.

Part III now takes the first cell. The chain sketched in §2 — pairs generated in the oxide, holes surviving and transporting, charge trapped near the interface, a parameter drifting — is four distinct pieces of physics, each with its own dependence on field, thickness, temperature and time. M06 supplies the device electrostatics that make the last step quantitative: what a charge in an oxide does to a transistor, and where it has to sit to do the most.

Quick reference

  • Two axes: ionizing against non-ionizing energy (a property of the particle and target), crossed with cumulative against single-particle response (a property of the circuit).
  • TID — ionizing, cumulative. Charge trapped in insulators; threshold shifts, leakage, gain loss. Measured in rad(Si)/rad(SiO₂). Any ionizing species contributes.
  • DD — non-ionizing, cumulative. Vacancies and interstitials in the lattice; lifetime, dark current, charge transfer efficiency. Measured as DDD = Φ · NIEL. Protons and neutrons dominate.
  • SEE — ionizing, single particle. Upset when Qcoll > Qcrit. Governed by LET; measured as a cross-section in cm² per device. Qcrit is set by the technology and circuit; Qcoll is set by the ion and by the volume the struck junction can collect from — below 50 fC (3×10⁵ electrons) in unhardened modern circuits.
  • Soft error — state corrupted, device undamaged, corrected by rewriting: SEU in SRAM or logic. Hard error — permanent damage, not correctable: latchup, snapback, single-event burnout, single-event gate rupture. Predominantly a high-power-device concern. Controlled by ion LET and applied voltage (gate voltage for rupture, drain voltage for burnout); the threat rises with both.
  • Displacement numbers: threshold 21 eV in Si; 50 keV primary knock-on atom typical of a 1 MeV neutron or energetic proton; about 90% of Frenkel pairs recombine, about 10% remain.
  • Pair creation: 3.6 eV in Si, 17 eV in SiO₂. Silicon does not retain charge because it is conductive and the carriers recombine or are swept out; an oxide does because holes are immobile and have no path to ground.
  • One particle, three effects. A proton ionizes (TID), recoils nuclei (DD), and may produce a heavily ionizing nuclear secondary (SEE). The effects are separated by their controlling variable, not by the particle.
  • No test bounds another. A total-dose qualification says nothing about single-event response or lattice damage, and a heavy-ion cross-section says nothing about parameter drift.
  • Error-correcting codes address single-event upsets. They have no effect on total-dose parameter drift, which corrupts no stored value.
