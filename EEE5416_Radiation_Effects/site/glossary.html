Particles and photons

TermWhat it isKey factsWhy it matters
Photon
X-ray, gamma ray		 A quantum of electromagnetic radiation. No charge, no rest mass. X-ray and gamma differ by origin, not wavelength: gamma from nuclear transitions, X-ray from electron transitions or machine bremsstrahlung. Test sources: Co-60 at 1.17 and 1.33 MeV, Cs-137 at 662 keV. The standard total-dose test species. Ionizes indirectly, via the electron it liberates. M01 §§2, 5
Electron
beta particle, β		 Elementary particle, charge −1e. Called a beta particle when emitted from a nucleus. Rest mass 0.511 MeV/c². Outer Van Allen belt: 0.1–10 MeV, max ~7 MeV. Dominant trapped species at GEO. Stopped readily by aluminium, but generates bremsstrahlung doing so. M01 §2, M02 §7
Proton Hydrogen nucleus, charge +1e. Rest mass 938 MeV/c². Inner belt >100 MeV, some >400 MeV. ~89% of GCR, 90–95% of solar particles. The most abundant energetic species in space. Causes upsets indirectly via nuclear reactions, and directly below ~65 nm. M01 §§2, 7, M02 §7
Neutron Uncharged nucleon. Rest mass 940 MeV/c². A free neutron decays to proton + electron + antineutrino, mean life ≈ 880 s (about 15 min). Dominates the atmospheric environment. No Coulomb interaction, so it cannot be stopped — only attenuated. M01 §6, M02 §7, M03 §8
Alpha particle
α		 Helium-4 nucleus: two protons, two neutrons, charge +2e. Rest mass 3727 MeV/c². Range: a few cm of air, or a sheet of paper. ~9% of GCR. In microelectronics the source is usually the package itself — trace uranium and thorium in mould compound and solder. M01 §2, M11 §4
Heavy ion Any nucleus heavier than helium, stripped of electrons. Charge +Ze. 1–2% of GCR. Defines the worst-case LET a part must survive. Ionizes densely enough to upset or destroy a node with no intermediary. The species accelerator testing is spent on. M01 §2, M11 §1
Delta ray A secondary electron liberated with enough kinetic energy to ionize further atoms itself. Spreads ionization laterally away from the primary track. Widens the volume from which a circuit node can collect charge. M01 §3

Interaction mechanisms

TermWhat it isKey factsWhy it matters
Ionization Transfer of enough energy to free a bound electron, leaving an ion pair. Threshold ~10 eV. Pair creation costs 3.6 eV in Si, 17 eV in SiO₂. The only process that produces mobile charge. Everything else matters through how much ionization it causes. M01 §§1, 3, M04 §4
Excitation Promotion of a bound electron to a higher state without freeing it. Occurs at larger distances from the track than ionization. Atom de-excites, emitting a low-energy UV photon. Removes energy from the incident particle without producing free charge. M01 §3
Bremsstrahlung Radiation emitted when a charged particle is deflected by a nuclear Coulomb field. Scales as Z² of the absorber and E/m² of the particle. Radiative fraction ≈ ZE/800, E in MeV. Sets the floor in a dose-depth curve. A high-Z shield converts stopped electrons into penetrating X-rays. M01 §4, M03 §§5–6
Cherenkov radiation Light emitted when a charged particle exceeds the phase velocity of light in a dielectric. Minor energy-loss channel; source of the blue glow around a reactor core. Background in radiation detectors. M01 §4
Photoelectric absorption A photon is absorbed entirely by a bound electron, which is ejected. Cross-section rises steeply as photon energy falls and atomic number rises. Dominant in Si at 10 keV. Its strong Z dependence is what produces dose enhancement. M01 §5
Compton scattering A photon transfers part of its energy to an electron and continues, degraded. Dominant at intermediate energy. Co-60 gammas interact with Si this way. The mechanism behind standard Co-60 total-dose testing. M01 §5
Pair production A photon converts into an electron–positron pair in a nuclear field. Threshold 1.022 MeV — twice the electron rest energy. Third photon channel; only relevant above ~1 MeV. M01 §5
Dose enhancement Local dose increase near a high-Z / low-Z interface, from the imbalance of secondary electrons crossing it. Largest for low-energy photons and high atomic number, and a significant issue for 10 keV X-ray testing. The largest measured factors, 8–10× in thin oxides, are beside very high-Z layers such as gold near 100 keV; no more than 2–3× in typical well photocurrent. A part can absorb far more dose than the incident fluence suggests, purely from its packaging and metallisation. Distinct from ELDRS. M04 §5
Neutron capture A nucleus absorbs a neutron and emits a charged product — (n,α), (n,p). 10B(n,α)7Li releases ~2.8 MeV. Strongly favoured at thermal energies. Boron in dielectrics made this a leading terrestrial soft-error mechanism. Why thermal-neutron testing is a separate step. M01 §6
Charged-particle equilibrium The condition in which as many secondary electrons enter a small volume as leave it. Holds inside a uniform material under photon irradiation; fails within about one secondary-electron range of an interface between materials of different atomic number. Dose from a photon field is computable from tabulated coefficients only where it holds. Where it fails, the correction is the dose enhancement factor. M04 §5
Displacement
non-ionizing energy loss		 Energy transferred to a nucleus, knocking the atom off its lattice site. Produces a vacancy and an interstitial. About 90% of the pairs recombine at once; the stable defects that remain only partly anneal at room temperature. Introduces bandgap states; degrades minority-carrier lifetime. The origin of displacement damage. M01 §8, M04 §6, M12 §§2, 5
Primary knock-on atom
PKA		 The first atom displaced by an incident particle, which then displaces others as it travels. A 50 keV PKA is typical of a 1 MeV neutron or an energetic proton — enough to produce a cascade rather than one isolated defect. The recoil spectrum, not the incident particle alone, determines the defect structure left behind. M04 §6, M12 §§1–2
Frenkel pair A vacancy and the interstitial atom that left it. Roughly 90% recombine promptly, in under a millisecond; about 10% survive as stable defects. Only the surviving fraction degrades the device, which is why displacement damage is far smaller than the raw count of displacements suggests. M04 §6, M12 §§2, 5

Quantities and units

TermWhat it isUnitsWatch out for
Stopping power
S = −dE/dx		Energy a charged particle loses per unit path length. Splits into electronic stopping, which ionizes, and nuclear stopping, which displaces atoms.MeV/cmScales as z²/β² by the Bethe expression, so it rises as the particle slows. Electronic stopping dominates for every particle in this course. M04 §1
LET
linear energy transfer		Energy deposited per unit path length, divided by density. Used interchangeably with stopping power in this course.MeV·cm²/mg here; MeV·cm²/g in stopping-power tables; keV/µm in the radiological literature (1 MeV·cm²/mg = 233 keV/µm in Si)Normalised by density, hence the mass in the denominator. Formally the local deposition, so it differs from stopping power by the energy delta rays carry away. Anchors in Si: minimum ionizing 1.66 MeV·cm²/g, 100 MeV proton 5.85 MeV·cm²/g, accelerator ions 6–35 MeV·cm²/mg. M04 §2
Bragg peakThe maximum of energy deposition per unit length, close to the end of a charged particle’s track.—Charge collection is greatest when the peak coincides with the sensitive volume, which is what a heavy-ion test must arrange. Past the peak the ion captures electrons and deposition falls to zero. M04 §3
Range
R = ∫dE/S		Total path a charged particle travels before stopping.g/cm², or a length after dividing by density. 5.5 MeV alpha in Si: CSDA 6.49 mg/cm² = 27.9 µmFrom NIST PSTAR/ASTAR or SRIM, never by hand. Straggling spreads the endpoint by of order 1%. The straight-path assumption fails for electrons, which need an extrapolated range instead. M04 §3
Pair creation energy
Ep		Mean energy deposited per electron–hole pair produced.3.6 eV in Si · 17 eV in SiO₂Exceeds the bandgap because energy also goes to phonons and to carrier kinetic energy. Near Ep ≈ 2.8Eg + (0–1 eV) across semiconductors. A MOS device contains both materials and the constants are not interchangeable. M04 §4
Pair generation constant
kg, also written g0		Electron–hole pairs created per unit volume per unit dose.pairs/(cm³·rad): 8.1×10¹² in SiO₂, 4×10¹³ in Si, ~7×10¹³ in GaAsMaterial-specific, and the dose must be in the same material. Reproducible from 6.24×10¹³ eV/g per rad × 2.2 g/cm³ ÷ 17 eV for SiO₂. Deck 05 writes kg, Deck 03 writes g0 — same quantity. M04 §4, M07 §1
NIEL
non-ionizing energy loss		The part of the stopping power that goes into displacing atoms rather than ionizing. The exact counterpart of LET for the other channel.MeV·cm²/g; also MeV·mb (100 MeV·mb = 2.144 keV·cm²/g in Si)Counts only recoils above the displacement threshold, weighted by the damage energy each keeps. Tabulated per particle, energy and target; used from tables. Deck 03 p. 40's 1/ρ prefactor needs a macroscopic cross-section; with a per-atom one it is NA/A. M04 §6, M12 §§1, 4
DDD
displacement damage dose, Φ·NIEL		Non-ionizing energy deposited per unit mass — the non-ionizing counterpart of total dose.MeV/gDegradation scales with the product, which is what transfers a damage factor between particles. Checked per material and particle: GaAs departs from NIEL scaling above roughly 40 MeV protons; electrons in p-type Si go as NIEL² and sit ~10× below n-type at 1 MeV; GaAs solar-cell electron damage follows NIEL1.7 (Walters 1999). M04 §6, M12 §4
Displacement threshold
Td, Eth		Minimum energy a recoiling atom needs to leave its lattice site permanently.≈21 eV in SiBelow it the energy becomes lattice vibration and no defect remains. Roughly six times the 3.6 eV that liberates a carrier. M04 §6, M12 §2
DEF
dose enhancement factor, fDE		Dose actually deposited in the sensitive region divided by the dose under charged-particle equilibrium.dimensionless≈1 for Co-60. Up to 8–10 in thin oxides beside gold near 100 keV; no more than 2–3 in typical well photocurrent — the difference is layer thickness against the secondary-electron range. HfO₂ gate stacks: ~2–4 calculated for TiN-gated 1–8 nm stacks (Deck 03 p. 22), 2–8 quoted for HfO₂/TiN capacitors (Deck 07 p. 41); stack- and spectrum-specific. The professor's worked 10 keV X-ray value is 1.55. M04 §5, M07 §3, M10 §10
Mass energy-absorption coefficient
µen/ρ		Converts a photon energy fluence into an absorbed dose in a named material.cm²/g, tabulated by NISTThe route to dose for photons, which have no LET. Strongly material- and energy-dependent, which is why dose enhancement exists. SiO₂ is not in the NIST compound tables; it is mass-weighted from Si and O. M04 §§5, 8
Absorbed dose
D		Mean energy imparted per unit mass at a point: energy entering the volume minus energy leaving, divided by the mass.rad, or gray. 1 rad = 100 erg/g = 0.01 J/kg = 6.24×10¹³ eV/g; 1 Gy = 1 J/kg = 100 rad = 6.24×10¹⁵ eV/gAlways name the material — rad(Si) and rad(SiO₂) are not interchangeable, and a dose with no stated shield depth cannot be compared with another. M03 §1, M04 §8
Dose rateDose per unit time.rad(Si)/s. Space: 10⁻⁶ to mid-10⁻³. 1 rad/s = 3.156×10⁷ rad/yrA separate requirement from total dose, not a restatement of it: oxides anneal during a slow irradiation, and some bipolar technologies degrade more at low rate. M03 §2, M15 §§4, 7
FluenceParticles per unit area, integrated over time.cm⁻²Distinct from flux, which is per unit time. M03 §2
FluxParticles per unit area per unit time.cm⁻²s⁻¹An orbital rate uses the flux spectrum, a test the fluence. M03 §2, M11 §8
Cross-section
σ, single-event		Errors observed per unit fluence.cm²/device; per bit, cm²/bitThree quantities here carry cm²: cross-section (cm²/device), LET (MeV·cm²/mg) and dose. Do not blur them. Per-bit values divide by the bit count, and 1 Mbit = 1,048,576 bits. M05 §4, M11 §6
FITFailures in time.1 per 10⁹ device-hoursAn error rate per day becomes FIT by ÷ 24 × 10⁹. M03 §8, M11 §8
Equivalent dose
H = wRD		Absorbed dose in tissue, weighted by particle type for biological effect.rem, or sievert. 1 Sv = 100 rem, so 10 mSv = 1 remA tissue quantity — never appears in a device specification. Rem and rad coincide only where wR = 1. The course's "1 rem ≈ 1 rad to within 15–20%" describes the mixed atmospheric aircrew field specifically, not particles in general. M03 §3
Radiation weighting factor
wR		Multiplier converting absorbed dose to equivalent dose, reflecting how densely a species deposits energy along its track.dimensionlessICRP 103: 1 photons, electrons, muons; 2 protons and charged pions; 20 alphas, heavy ions, fission fragments; for neutrons a continuous function of energy from 2.5 to 20.7 at 1 MeV. ICRP 60 gave 5 for protons and a 5–20 step for neutrons, so older references differ. M03 §3
Areal density
mass thickness, xρ = ρt		Mass of material per unit area along the beam path.g/cm². Aluminium: 1 mm = 0.270 g/cm², 200 mils = 1.37 g/cm²The correct variable for shield depth and for every range table, because energy loss follows the number of electrons encountered rather than the length. Equates materials only to first order. M03 §5

The space and terrestrial environment

TermWhat it isKey factsWhy it matters
Galactic cosmic rays
GCR		Nearly isotropic flux of energetic nuclei from outside the solar system.~89% protons, ~9% helium, 1–2% heavier ions and electrons. Flux peaks 100–1000 MeV/nucleon; max ~10²⁰ eV.Essentially unshieldable. Sets the heavy-ion single-event requirement; contributes little else in Earth orbit. M02 §2
Solar particle event
SPE, flare, CME		Episodic burst of energetic particles from the Sun.90–95% protons. Flux can rise ~10⁴× in a large flare. CMEs can exceed 5×10⁵ protons/cm²·s.Episodic, tied to the ~11-year solar intensity peak. Drives worst-case mission planning. M02 §3
Van Allen belts
trapped radiation		Particles confined by the geomagnetic field, gyrating, bouncing and drifting.Protons: one continuous belt from L = 1.14 outward, no slot; several hundred MeV, peak >10 MeV flux ~10⁵ cm⁻²s⁻¹ at L ≈ 1.7 (course extent ~1,000–12,000 km), stable over years. Electrons: inner zone L = 1–2, slot L = 2–3, outer zone L = 3–10 to ~7–10 MeV, ~3×10⁶ cm⁻²s⁻¹ above 1 MeV at L = 4–4.5, variable by orders of magnitude. 1 RE = 6,380 km.The dominant total-dose source for most Earth orbits. Fixed geography, so it can be designed around. M02 §7
Solar windSteady outflow of plasma from the Sun, carrying an embedded magnetic field.Protons, alphas and electrons at eV to keV energies — too low to penetrate a spacecraft wall.Not a bulk damage source. It modulates GCR, shapes the magnetosphere, and charges exposed surfaces. M02 §1
Solar flareSudden localized brightening of the Sun: a burst of electromagnetic radiation released by coronal magnetic reconnection.Electron-rich, lasts hours, high ³He/⁴He. Often but not always accompanied by energetic particles.Distinct from a CME, which is what produces the large proton fluences. M02 §3
Coronal mass ejection
CME		Eruption of magnetized plasma that drives a shock and accelerates particles.Proton-rich, lasts days, low ³He. 10¹⁵–10¹⁷ g at 50–2,500 km/s, average ~450 km/s; hours to days to reach Earth.The source of the large solar particle events and of the geomagnetic storms that reshape the belts. M02 §3
Solar activity cyclePeriodic variation in solar output.~11 years in intensity, ~22 years in magnetic polarity. Tracked by sunspot number and the 10.7 cm radio flux F10.7.GCR peak at solar minimum and solar events at solar maximum, so no single phase is worst for everything. M02 §4
HeliosphereMagnetised cavity blown in the interstellar medium by the solar wind, bounded by the heliopause.Scatters incoming GCR; the lower the energy, the less likely a particle is to reach 1 AU. Modulation is negligible above ~20 GeV/nucleon.The reason GCR flux is anti-correlated with solar activity. M02 §2
CRAND
cosmic ray albedo neutron decay		The source process for the inner proton belt.GCR make atmospheric neutrons; some escape upward and decay (mean life ≈ 880 s) into protons that the field traps.Ties the inner belt to the GCR flux, so it too is stronger at solar minimum. M02 §7
Slot regionLocal minimum in electron flux between the inner and outer electron zones. An electron feature only — the proton belt runs straight through these shells.L ≈ 2–3. Storms inject electrons that then decay with a ~10 day time constant.Attractive for some missions, but highly variable. M02 §7
South Atlantic Anomaly
SAA		Region where the offset geomagnetic field lets the inner proton belt reach unusually low altitude.A LEO spacecraft takes most of its trapped-proton dose and upsets here.Makes LEO exposure episodic and predictable — schedulable. M02 §8
LEO / MEO / GEOOrbit bands.LEO ≤ 2,000 km. MEO includes GPS at 20,200 km. GEO at 35,800 km.Orbit choice changes annual dose by ~1000×. M02 §9
Atmospheric cascadeShower of secondaries produced when a cosmic ray strikes the upper atmosphere. A primary interacts with N or O, producing pions, neutrons, protons and fragments; π± decay to muons, π⁰ to photon pairs that start electromagnetic showers.Sea level presents 1,033 g/cm² of atmosphere. Neutrons dominate at aircraft altitude, where the upset rate is ~300× the sea-level value.TID is not a concern in avionics; single-event effects are, because a neutron needs no accumulated dose to flip a bit. M03 §§7–8
Pfotzer–Regener maximumAltitude at which the atmospheric cascade peaks — the balance point between build-up of secondaries and their absorption.15–20 km, an atmospheric depth of 50–100 g/cm². Aircraft cruise below it, at 10–13 km.Neutron flux is not monotonic in altitude. Above the maximum the neutron flux falls while primary ion flux keeps rising, so the upset mechanism shifts from indirect to direct ionization. M03 §7
Terrestrial neutron environmentThe ground-level remnant of the cascade.JEDEC reference: New York City, sea level, 3.6×10⁻³ neutrons cm⁻²s⁻¹ above 10 MeV ≈ 13 cm⁻²h⁻¹. A 90 nm SRAM runs 777 FIT/Mbit.A small flux acting on a very large number of nodes: one error per Tbit per 1.3 hours. The reason ECC is standard in server, automotive and avionics memory. M03 §8
Man-made environmentsRadiation fields produced deliberately, as opposed to encountered.Accelerators (LHC detectors, projected into the Grad range), reactors and their accident conditions, and nuclear weapon environments. The Van Allen belts are not among them.Some systems must survive them; all of them double as the ground test facilities used to qualify parts. M03 §9
Weapon environmentThe field from a nuclear detonation.Prompt gamma rays, X-rays hot and cold, neutrons, and the secondary charged particles those produce. Delivered as a pulse.Dose-rate effects and prompt photocurrents dominate rather than slow trapping, and the X-ray component drives dose enhancement at high-Z interfaces. M03 §9

Shielding

TermWhat it isKey factsWhy it matters
Dose-depth curveDose behind a given depth of shielding, plotted against that depth.Decomposes into trapped electrons, which fall steeply and stop; bremsstrahlung, made inside the shield, which attenuates slowly; and trapped protons, which fall slowly because the spectrum reaches hundreds of MeV.The total flattens once the electrons are gone. That flattening is why shielding saturates. M03 §5
Extrapolated range
Katz–Penfold		Depth of material beyond which essentially no electrons of a given energy emerge.In aluminium, from a Katz–Penfold fit to aluminium absorbers: 1 MeV → 0.41 g/cm² (1.5 mm); 3 MeV → 1.48 g/cm² (5.5 mm); 7 MeV → 3.60 g/cm² (13.4 mm); 10 MeV → 5.19 g/cm² (19.2 mm). A 100 MeV proton needs 9.99 g/cm², or 37 mm.Sets the depth at which the electron contribution disappears. Not the same as the CSDA range, which is longer because it follows the wandering path. M03 §5
Shielding saturationThe point beyond which additional shield mass removes very little further dose.Reached once the shield exceeds the electron range. What remains is bremsstrahlung generated in the shield and the high-energy end of the proton spectrum.Makes shielding a bounded solution. Beyond it the remaining options are part selection, spot shielding and orbit choice. M03 §5
Why aluminiumThe standard spacecraft shield and structure material.Mass is the binding constraint; the radiative-to-collisional stopping ratio ≈ ZE/700 is 1.9% in Al and 11.7% in Pb at 1 MeV, higher in proportion to atomic number, so lead diverts far more energy into penetrating photons at equal areal density; and a conductive bonded skin prevents differential charging.High Z is right only for spot shielding one small part, or as the front layer of a graded stack backed by a low-Z absorber. M03 §6
Spacecraft chargingAccumulation of differing electrostatic potentials on a vehicle with no ground.Sunlit surfaces photoemit while shadowed surfaces collect plasma electrons; differential potentials can reach kilovolts and discharge through the structure.An established cause of on-orbit anomalies, and the reason the shield must also be conductive and bonded. M03 §6, M04 §7

Magnetospheric geometry and trapping

TermWhat it isKey factsWhy it matters
MagnetosphereThe region in which the Earth's magnetic field controls charged-particle motion.Internal field approximately dipolar to L = 4–5; axis tilted ~11.5°, centre offset >500 km. Compressed to ~10 RE sunward (~6 in a storm), tail to ~1,000 RE.It creates the belts and filters everything else. M02 §5
L-shell
(B, L) coordinates		Label for a drift shell: the geocentric distance, in Earth radii, at which its field line crosses the magnetic equator.L = 1 + heq/RE at the equator, with RE = 6,380 km. B gives position along the line.Trapped flux is a function of (B, L) alone, not of geography. Measured from the Earth's centre, not its surface. M02 §§5, 7
Gyroradius
rg		Radius of the circular component of a charged particle's helical path.rg = p⊥/(|q|B); 235 km for a 100 MeV proton at L = 1.7.Small against the shell radius, which is why the guiding-centre picture works. M02 §5
Pitch angle
α		Angle between a particle's velocity and the local field.sin²α/B is constant, so α grows as the field converges.Decides where a particle mirrors, and so whether it stays trapped. M02 §6
Mirror pointWhere the velocity along the field reaches zero and the particle reverses.Bm = Beq/sin²αeq. Bounce period a few tenths of a second.The confinement mechanism along a field line. M02 §6
Loss coneThe small equatorial pitch angles whose mirror point would lie inside the atmosphere.Sets the belts' lower edge near 200 km altitude.Any process that scatters pitch angle toward the field direction empties the belt. M02 §6
Drift shellToroidal surface swept out by a trapped particle's azimuthal drift.Protons drift west and electrons east, with a period of tens of seconds to tens of minutes that falls with energy. The ring current is this same motion in the 10–200 keV ions near L = 3–8.The surface labelled by L, around which the environment is averaged. M02 §6
Magnetic rigidity
R		Momentum per unit charge — a particle's resistance to magnetic deflection.R = pc/Ze, in volts and usually GV; 0.445 GV for a 100 MeV proton.Equal rigidity means an equal trajectory, whatever the species. M02 §9
Geomagnetic cutoff
Störmer cutoff		Minimum rigidity needed to reach a point in the magnetosphere.Rc ≈ 14.9 cos⁴λ/(r/RE)² GV. Geomagnetic storms lower it.Why polar orbits admit solar protons and equatorial ones do not — inclination matters as much as altitude. M02 §9

Effects

Key facts open with whether the effect accumulates over many particles or comes from one.

TermWhat it isKey factsRecoverable?
Total ionizing dose
TID		Charge liberated by ionization becomes trapped in insulating layers and at their interfaces, shifting device parameters.Cumulative. Two charges with opposite time behaviour: oxide-trapped charge, largest right after exposure, and interface traps, smallest then and still building. M05 §2, M07, M08Partially, by annealing: trapped holes anneal at room temperature; interface traps only above ~125 °C
Displacement damage
DD		Atoms knocked off lattice sites, creating defect states in the bandgap.Cumulative. Scales with DDD. Lifetime degrades before carrier concentration; the results are gain loss, dark current, CTI and solar-cell output loss. M05 §3, M12 §3Partly: short-term annealing over minutes to ~1 h, slow long-term annealing to ~1 yr; the residue is treated as permanent. M12 §5
Single-event upset
SEU; SBU		Collected charge from one particle flips a stored bit in a memory cell, latch or register. A single-bit upset (SBU) when one cell flips.Single particle. Occurs when the collected charge exceeds Qcrit. Counted as a cross-section. M05 §4, M11 §§1, 4Yes, on rewrite; corrected by single-error-correcting codes
Multiple-cell upset
MCU; MBU		One particle upsets several cells. Dodd and Massengill call this a multiple-bit upset; JEDEC reserves MBU for the subset whose flipped bits lie in one logical word.Single particle. Grows with scaling as cell pitch approaches the track and diffusion size. M11 §§1, 9Yes, but an MBU defeats one-bit-per-word correction unless physically adjacent bits are interleaved into different words
Single-event transient
SET		A voltage or current pulse on a circuit node from one strike.Single particle. An error only if it has an active path, survives the gates and arrives in a latch's setup-and-hold window, so combinational error rate rises with clock frequency. Simulated bulk widths ~700–950 ps at LET 70; SOI < 250 ps. Predicted to become a primary failure mode below 0.35 µm. M11 §5Yes — transient; harmless unless a storage element captures it
Single-event functional interrupt
SEFI		A strike that throws a complex part into a test, reset or idle mode.Single particle. No stored data bit need have changed. Deck 03 p. 25 counts it among the soft errors. M11 §1Yes, by reset or power cycle
Single-event latchup
SEL		A strike switches on the parasitic four-layer thyristor of bulk CMOS (vertical pnp + lateral npn, loop gain > 1); supply current rises to compliance.Single particle. Needs a supply above the thyristor's holding voltage; sensitivity rises with supply voltage and temperature (A1 §4). Guide, not a requirement: immune if none after 10⁷ ions/cm². SOI eliminates it. M11 §10Usually, by power cycling — permanent if current persists
SnapbackSustained high-current state of one parasitic transistor — the source–body–drain npn of an n-channel device — held on by impact ionization at a high drain voltage.Single particle. Needs no four-layer structure. The concern in SOI, where the floating body makes the bipolar action easy and latchup is absent. M05 §5, M11 §10Yes, removing power clears it
Single-event burnout
SEB		A regenerative current in a power device, held off at high drain voltage, destroys it thermally (parasitic npn plus avalanche, second breakdown).Single particle. Set by ion LET and drain voltage; the failure voltage falls as LET rises (V = 399.13·LET−0.386, Liu 2011). SiC: threshold saturates above LET ~10, near 800 V for 3300 V parts (~20% of avalanche breakdown) and 500 V for 1200 V parts (~30%). p-channel SEB not reported (2004 textbook). M05 §5, M11 §10No
Single-event gate rupture
SEGR		Destructive breakdown of a gate dielectric along an ion track.Single particle. Threat rises with LET and gate voltage, and in a power MOSFET, held off with the ion through the neck, with drain voltage too; data are VGS–VDS boundaries per LET. Critical voltage falls with LET and thinner oxide (18 nm ~15 → 9 V, 7 nm ~6 → 4 V); thin oxides show precursor damage. M05 §5, M11 §10No
Single-event leakage current
SELC		Permanent increase in leakage in a power device from strikes that do not burn it out.Single particle; the damage is permanent. In SiC its threshold voltage falls steeply up to LET ~10 and then keeps falling more slowly (about 620 → 400 V between LET 10 and 65 for 3300 V parts), unlike the saturating SEB threshold. M11 §10No
ELDRS
enhanced low dose rate sensitivity		Some bipolar technologies — linear circuits with lateral or substrate pnp inputs — degrade more from a given total dose delivered slowly.Cumulative, a true dose-rate effect. LM324 ×5.8 and LM111 ×4.7 against 50 rad(Si)/s; npn LM108 and discrete transistors show none. Mechanism as the course gives it: space charge at high rate, plus the key to ELDRS (see below); other contributions remain in the literature, and package hydrogen alone moves the result by up to 100×. Defects, traps and charge in oxides

TermWhat it isKey factsWhy it matters
Oxide-trapped charge
ΔNot, ΔVot		 Holes captured at deep traps in an insulator after ionizing radiation, mostly within a few nm of the Si/SiO₂ interface. Net positive. Maximum immediately after irradiation, then anneals. Shift scales as tox2 to first order; tunnelling pulls it below that line under about 20 nm, and below ~5 nm almost no net hole trapping remains (H⁺ can still leave positive charge). Shifts VT negative in n- and p-channel devices alike. M07 §3, M09 §7
Interface traps
Dit (cm⁻²eV⁻¹), Nit (cm⁻²)		 Electrically active states at the Si/SiO₂ boundary whose charge depends on the surface potential. Donor-like below midgap, acceptor-like above, so net charge is zero at midgap. Minimum immediately after irradiation, then builds: begun by ~1 s, half formed after tens of seconds, ~80% by 10³ s, saturated near 10⁵ s. The course's 10³–10⁶ s is the time to completion, latent rise included. Anneal significantly only above ~125 °C. Shifts n-channel VT positive and p-channel negative; stretches the subthreshold curve; lowers mobility; raises bipolar base current. M06 §5, M07 §8, M08 §4, M09 §8
Charge yield
fy(Eox)		 Fraction of radiation-generated holes that escapes prompt recombination. Rises with oxide field, falls with the ionization density of the particle. At 1 MV/cm: ≈0.9 Co-60 and 12 MeV electrons, ≈0.55 5 keV electrons, ≈0.15 700 keV protons, ≈0.07 2 MeV alphas. Never zero: ≈0.3 for Co-60 at zero field on Deck 05 p. 32, and a device at zero bias still has a built-in oxide field. A dose without its source does not predict a threshold shift. M07 §2
Hole trapping efficiency
fot		 Fraction of the pairs generated that ends as trapped charge. Defined by fot = ΔNot/(toxkgfyfDED). Process-dependent. Capture falls with field as about Eox−1/2 while the yield rises, so the net trapped charge peaks near 1–2 MV/cm. The quantity a total-dose measurement returns, and the last term in the course's master equation. M07 §§3–4
E′ centre An unpaired electron highly localized on a silicon atom bonded to three oxygens. Formed when a hole breaks the weak Si–Si bond of an oxygen vacancy. At least nine variants. Paramagnetic, so visible to EPR. Carries the oxide-trap charge and anneals at room temperature by tunnelling and thermal emission — a separate defect from Pb. The defect that holds oxide-trapped charge. Correlation with ΔNot is strong in some oxides, weaker in others including SOI buried oxides. M07 §§6, 8
Pb centre
•Si≡Si₃		 A silicon dangling bond at the Si/SiO₂ interface, back-bonded to three silicons (Deck 05 p. 60): the interface trap itself, an interface defect distinct from the E′ oxide trap. Created when a proton, released near an interface by transporting holes, depassivates an Si–H bond: H⁺ + H–Si≡Si₃ → H₂ + (+)Si≡Si₃. Also identified by EPR. Anneals only above ~125 °C. The defect that is an interface trap. M07 §8, M09 §8
Border traps Near-interface oxide traps close enough to the silicon to exchange charge with it on the timescale of a measurement. Electrostatically oxide traps, kinetically interface traps: switching band ~0.01 Hz–10 kHz (oxide traps slower, interface traps faster). In SiO₂, 2.5 nm ↔ ~1 s, ±0.25 nm per decade; below ~4 nm every oxide trap may be one. Separated from interface traps by charge pumping; measured by room-temperature C–V hysteresis after irradiation or high-field stress (Deck 07 pp. 39–40). The same defects as switching oxide traps. Carry 1/f noise with oxide traps, hysteresis, switched-bias reversibility and compensation. M06 §3, M07 §9, M10 §§6, 8
Electron traps Sites in thermal oxide that capture electrons rather than holes. In some gate oxides their number exceeds half that of hole traps. They compensate trapped holes without removing them. Compensated fraction fe/fh = Q(−)/Q(+): wet ≈0.1–0.25, dry ≈0.3–0.65; net shift = Nh(1 − fe/fh). Measured ΔVot is a net quantity and understates the holes actually trapped. M07 §7, M09 §6
Switching oxide trap An E′ centre compensated as a dipole: an electron sits on the neutral silicon beside the positive one, cancelling the charge without re-forming the bond. Reversed bias returns the electron to the silicon: after a week's +10 V anneal at 100 °C, −10 V restored nearly half the "annealed" charge within a day; ±1 MV/cm cycling moves ~0.5 V reversibly. Common in hardened oxides, almost absent in soft commercial ones. Makes "annealed" charge reversible, so a part's threshold depends on its bias history. M09 §6, M10 §9
Polaron hopping
dispersive transport, CTRW		 A hole in amorphous SiO₂ localizes on an oxygen site and moves only by thermally assisted hops to a neighbour. Hole mobility 10⁻⁵ to 10⁻⁷ cm²/V·s against ~20 cm²/V·s for electrons. Recovery spans many decades in time and scales as tox⁴; faster at higher field. Why oxides charge positive, and why an irradiated device keeps changing after the beam stops. M07 §5, M09 §2
Fixed oxide charge
QF		 Positive charge within a few nm of the interface, created during oxidation. Bias-independent and immobile. Distinct from radiation-induced trapped charge. Translates the C–V curve. M06 §3
Mobile ionic charge
QM		 Alkali contamination, principally sodium and potassium, that drifts under bias at elevated temperature. Signature is a flat-band shift after bias–temperature stress at elevated temperature, which persists after the device cools. Room-temperature C–V hysteresis is the border-trap signature instead. A process-control problem that mimics radiation damage. M06 §3, M10 §8

Oxide charge in time, temperature, dose rate and bias

TermWhat it isKey factsWhy it matters
Tunnelling front
xm(t) = (1/2β)ln(αt)		 The depth from an electrode inside which trapped holes have been neutralized by electrons tunnelling from that electrode. ~0.2 nm wide and advancing ~0.2 nm per decade of time (Oldham), or by the course's rule 2.5 nm at ~1 s and 0.25 nm per decade (Deck 07 p. 19). Runs from both the silicon and the gate. Temperature-independent, so it continues at 80 K. The analytical basis of ln t annealing; a 5 nm oxide is within reach throughout by ~1 s. M09 §§5, 7, M10 §6
Thermal-emission front
φm(t) = (kT/q)ln(AT²t)		 The energy depth above the SiO₂ valence-band edge shallower than which trapped holes have been thermally emptied. Advances (kT/q)ln10 per decade: 59.5 mV at 300 K, 74 mV at 100 °C, 15 mV at 77 K. Why heating accelerates oxide-trap annealing and cooling nearly stops it. M09 §5
Isochronal anneal Equal-time anneals at a rising series of temperatures, measuring after each step. Deck 06 p. 42 (30 min per step, 2.5 MV/cm): ΔVot unchanged at 50 °C, then −1.5 → −0.35 V by 300 °C (~10% gone by 100 °C); ΔVit grows 1.3 → 2.2 V to a peak at 125 °C, then falls. Shows which charge changes at which temperature: interface-trap annealing needs >125 °C, the physical basis of the 100 °C bake. M07 §8, M09 §9
Latent interface-trap buildup A second rise in Dit long after normal buildup has levelled off. Flat across the interface-trap window, ~10³–10⁶ s, then rises after ~2×10⁶ s to a maximum by ~2×10⁷ s: up to fourfold, arriving near 10⁶ s. Seen in oxides with high vacancy densities, initiated by H₂ transport, EA ≈ 0.41 eV (~25× faster at 100 °C than at 25 °C). A part can degrade further about eleven days after a test ends. A possible concern for space systems. M07 §8, M09 §8
Rebound
superrecovery		 An n-channel threshold that moved negative during irradiation turning around, crossing its pre-irradiation value, and continuing positive. ΔVot is maximum at end of irradiation and anneals; ΔVit is minimum then and grows. Opposite signs, opposite time behaviour. Worst case can arrive long after the exposure. MIL-STD-883 TM 1019's accelerated-anneal test bounds it: the flowchart decides whether it and a +0.5× dose overtest are required; the routine anneal is 168 h biased at 100 °C, which anneals trapped holes and drives interface traps to their plateau (they anneal only above ~125 °C). M08 §3, M15 §5
Time-dependent effect
against a true dose-rate effect		 A response that evolves on its own clock — hole annealing, interface-trap buildup — so a fast irradiation followed by a room-temperature anneal of equal elapsed time reproduces a slow one. A true dose-rate effect depends on the rate itself. MOS gate oxides usually show only time-dependent effects: one ΔVit(t) curve from 6×10⁹ to 0.05 rad/s. Test: anneal high-rate samples for the same elapsed time — equal damage means time-dependent, less damage means a true dose-rate effect. Linear bipolar oxides show a true dose-rate effect. Decides whether a fast test can bound the space response. M08 §9, M09 §9, M15 §4
Space-charge model
ELDRS		 At high dose rate slowly transporting holes build a positive sheet of space charge in a thick, low-field oxide; above the sheet the net field reverses, and holes and H⁺ generated there never reach the interface. A critical dose rate separates the two regimes, giving the S-shaped dependence of degradation on rate. Explains why extra dose at high rate adds space charge rather than damage. Half of the lecture's account of ELDRS. M08 §9, M15 §7
Key to ELDRS
electron capture against H⁺ release		 A transporting hole either captures an electron and is neutralized, or releases H⁺ first; once the charge is on the proton, electron–hole recombination no longer removes it and the proton can form an interface trap. Trapped holes are neutralized 100–1000× more readily than protons. High rate → more free electrons → capture wins → fewer interface traps. Fleetwood et al. 2008. The other half of the lecture's account; ties ELDRS to hydrogen. M08 §9, M15 §9
Cryogenic freezing
77–80 K		 At 77–80 K the thermally activated steps stop on the timescale of a measurement: H⁺ drift, thermal emission, interface-trap buildup and annealing. Hole transport is frozen at low and moderate field but still field-driven at high field (79 K, 5 MV/cm: 40–96 nm crossed in ~0.015–0.5 s). Tunnelling continues. Interface-trap buildup is ~10³ smaller than at room temperature; frozen processes resume on warming. The 80 K thickness data, at +1 to +2 MV/cm, show the geometric tox² while tunnelling alone pulls thin oxides below it. M08 §8, M09 §§7, 9

Gate-oxide growth and processing

TermWhat it isKey factsWhy it matters
Thermal oxidation
dry, wet		 Conversion of silicon to SiO₂ by O₂ (dry) or H₂O (wet) that dissolves in the existing oxide, diffuses across it and reacts at the buried Si/SiO₂ interface. Si + O₂ → SiO₂; Si + 2H₂O → SiO₂ + 2H₂. Above ~750 °C. Wet is 10–20× faster because of solubility: 3×10¹⁹ against 5×10¹⁶ cm⁻³ at 1100 °C. Growing 1 nm of oxide consumes 0.45 nm of Si. Forms the interface fresh, below the original surface; the growth and the anneals that follow set the defect population. M10 §2
Deal–Grove model
linear–parabolic law		 Growth law x² + Ax = B(t + τ), from diffusion through the oxide in series with reaction at the interface. Dry O₂, 1000 °C, (111): B/A 0.071 µm/h, B 0.0117 µm²/h, A 0.165 µm, τ 0.37 h. Linear x ≈ (B/A)(t + τ) for x ≪ A; parabolic x² ≈ Bt for x ≫ A; both overestimate. (100): B/A ÷ 1.7. Sets oxide thickness, the variable the tox² law depends on. Not predictive below ~20 nm in dry O₂. M10 §3
Anomalous thin-oxide regime The first twenty-odd nanometres of dry oxidation, which grow faster than the Deal–Grove law predicts. Deal and Grove's dry data extrapolate to 230 ± 30 Å at every temperature from 700 to 1200 °C; wet data extrapolate to zero. At 1000 °C, τ absorbs it as an initial 23 nm. Every modern gate oxide lies inside it, so Deal–Grove is not predictive for gate oxides. M10 §3
Rapid thermal processing
RTP		 Single-wafer lamp heating for seconds, instead of a batch furnace for minutes to hours. Small thermal budget, for very thin oxides. One of Deck 07's growth-method choices. M10 §2
Nitrided oxide SiO₂ with a small amount of nitrogen placed at the interface by nitridation in N₂O or NO. 0.5–2% N leaves the SiO₂ constants and Fowler–Nordheim conduction essentially unchanged. NH₃ gives the same nitrogen but brings hydrogen, and the excess hydrogen leaves electron traps, so Deck 07 marks it not preferred. The modest form of the "alternative dielectrics" lever; changes near-interface trapping. M10 §§2, 9
Post-oxidation anneal and forming-gas anneal
POA; sinter		 The anneal after growth, often inert, and the final hydrogen-bearing anneal at 400–450 °C that passivates dangling bonds. A high-temperature inert POA removes excess silicon but appears to drive oxygen vacancies deeper, out of tunnelling range. Hardened oxides use a lower-temperature anneal: traps nearer the interface, faster tunnel annealing, more switching. Winokur: forming gas −2.67 / +2.59 / −0.08 V (ot / it / th) against N₂ −1.48 / +1.08 / −0.40 V. The anneal decides where the defects sit and how much hydrogen remains; a near-zero net shift can hide the worst components. M10 §§2, 5, 9
Atomic layer deposition
ALD		 Film growth by alternating self-limiting surface reactions, a fraction of a nanometre per cycle. How high-κ films are deposited. A high-κ film's radiation response depends on whether it was deposited by ALD or by chemical vapour deposition. M10 §§2, 4
High-κ gate stack Gate dielectric of a high-permittivity oxide on a thin SiO₂/SiOₓ interlayer, under a metal gate. HfO₂, HfSiON, ZrO₂; TiN, TaN, W gates; gate-first or gate-last. Hf-based high-κ from 45 nm (2007), EOT 1.0 nm. Traps more electrons and holes per EOT than SiO₂, and more border traps. Felix: silicate EOT 4.5 nm ΔVmg ≈ −0.4 V against 10 nm SiO₂ ΔVth ≈ −0.2 V at 1 Mrad(SiO₂) (not like quantities). Adopted for lower leakage at the same EOT; tolerable for dose because modern stacks are thin (EOT ≲ 1 nm), not because the material traps less. M10 §4
Interfacial transition layer Non-stoichiometric region of the oxide next to the interface, rich in excess silicon, left by incomplete oxidation and moving with the interface as the oxide grows. Positively charged process-induced defects within about 20 nm of the interface (Deal and co-workers): more after low-temperature oxidation, fewer after high-temperature annealing. Excess silicon and missing oxygen are the precursors of hole traps. M10 §§2, 5
Fowler–Nordheim tunnelling
FN		 Field-driven electron tunnelling through the triangular tip of the Si/SiO₂ barrier into the oxide conduction band. J = AFNE² exp(−BFN/E), SI units; 3.1 eV barrier; BFN ≈ 2.6×10¹⁰ V/m for m* = 0.5m0. Essentially temperature-independent. The high-field stress that creates radiation's defect set. Deck 07 p. 26's equation is Frenkel–Poole, not FN. M10 §7
Frenkel–Poole emission Thermal emission of trapped electrons into the conduction band over a barrier lowered by the field. Strongly temperature-dependent, which separates it from FN. The equation actually printed on Deck 07 p. 26. M10 §7
Anode hole injection
and impact ionization across the gap		 Hole generation during FN stress: hot electrons arriving at the anode inject holes back into the oxide, or ionize across the oxide gap. The holes drift toward the silicon and are trapped where radiation-generated holes are. Hot electrons above ~2 eV also break Si–H bonds. Why high-field stress and radiation leave the same trapped holes, border traps and interface traps. M10 §7
Hardening by process Making the oxide itself tolerant, rather than the layout. Four levers (Deck 06 p. 48): hydrogen control, optimized oxidation, alternative dielectrics, process modifications. History: Aubuchon 1971, Derbenwick and Gregory 1975. Controls trap number, trap location and the hydrogen available to make interface traps. M09 §10, M10 §9
Ferroelectric HfZrO₂
HZO		 Hafnium zirconium oxide in a ferroelectric phase whose remanent polarization stores a bit without power. Early tests show radiation-driven depolarization (Deck 07 p. 42). An emerging non-volatile memory for radiation environments. M10 §10

Device structures and measurements

TermWhat it isKey factsWhy it matters
Flat-band voltage
VFB		 Gate voltage at which the semiconductor bands are flat to the interface and there is no field in the substrate. At flat band all image charge returns to the gate, so the shift is the voltage dropped across the oxide between the gate and the charge. The reference point radiation-induced shifts are measured against. M06 §§1, 3
Charge centroid
x̄		 Charge-weighted mean distance of oxide charge from the gate. ΔVFB = −(Qox/Cox)(x̄/tox). Charge at the gate does nothing; charge at the interface does the most. Radiation-induced holes pile up near the interface under positive bias, so the weighting is close to 1. M06 §3, M09 §3
Quasi-static C–V Capacitance measured from the displacement current driven by a slow gate-voltage ramp, rather than by an AC bridge. The inversion layer is replenished by thermal generation, which is far too slow to follow a 100 kHz or 1 MHz excitation, so a conventional meter always reports the high-frequency branch. The difference between the quasi-static and high-frequency curves measures Dit across the gap. M06 §2
C–V hysteresis Separation between forward and reverse room-temperature C–V sweeps. After irradiation or high-field stress it measures border traps; it peaks in depletion. 17 nm oxide: ~37 pF after 10 mC/cm² FN injection, ~28 pF after 2 Mrad(SiO₂). Mobile-ion shifts need bias–temperature stress instead. The convenient measure of border-trap density. M06 §3, M10 §8
Subthreshold swing
S, mV/decade		 Gate voltage needed to change drain current by one decade below threshold. Ideal minimum S0 = (kT/q)ln10 = 59.5 mV/dec at 300 K. Cit = qDit with Dit per eV. The course form omits Cd. Its slope measures Dit directly; stretchout is the interface-trap signature. M06 §6
Stretchout Deformation of the subthreshold characteristic along the voltage axis, as opposed to a rigid translation of it. Comes from interface traps, whose occupancy changes as the surface potential sweeps, so they add a bias-dependent capacitance in parallel with the semiconductor. Measured as an increase in S. The one signature that separates ΔNit from ΔNot on a single curve. M08 §2
Midgap technique Separating ΔVot from ΔVit using one subthreshold sweep. ΔVot = ΔVmg; ΔVit = ΔVth − ΔVmg. Errors ±3% on ΔVth, ±5–10% on the components. Works because interface traps are neutral at midgap whatever their density. M07 §9
Charge pumping
ICP		 Pulsing the gate between inversion and accumulation and measuring the recombination current at the substrate. ICP = qAGfDitΔΨs. Gate area in cm². Needs no radiation, so it doubles as a pre-irradiation interface-quality check; hard to apply to FinFETs. Measures low Dit accurately and separates interface traps from border traps. M07 §9
EPR / ESR
electron paramagnetic (spin) resonance		 Resonant microwave absorption by an unpaired electron in a swept magnetic field. The g-tensor depends on the defect's local environment, so different defects resonate at different fields. Needs a large number of paramagnetic defects — thick, heavily irradiated oxides — and is not a working measurement on modern thin gate oxides. Identifies a defect's atomic structure rather than merely counting states — how E′ and Pb were established. M07 §6
Thermally stimulated current
TSC		 Heating a biased, irradiated capacitor at a steady ramp and integrating the current released as traps empty. ~0.1 °C/s to ~350 °C; ~40–350 °C maps to ~1.2–2.2 eV. After positive-bias irradiation, with the gate negative during the ramp, Q(+) = ∫ΔITSCdt counts all trapped holes; midgap C–V counts the net, Q(net) = −CoxΔVmg; Q(−) = Q(+) − Q(net) is the compensating electron charge (Deck 07 p. 35). Maps the energy distribution of trapped holes, which sets the thermal-emission annealing rate, and separates compensation: hardened oxides show TSC > C–V. M07 §6, M09 §4, M10 §8
Random telegraph noise
RTN		 Two-level switching of drain current as a single trap captures and emits a carrier. The emission-to-capture time ratio against gate overdrive locates the trap in depth: 0.25–1.50 nm in 14 nm devices (Gorchichko 2023) — electrostatic depths in a Hf-based stack, not SiO₂ tunnelling distances. Resolves border traps one at a time in scaled devices. M10 §10
Gummel plot Collector and base current plotted logarithmically against base–emitter voltage. Separates base-current components by ideality factor: n = 1 for neutral-base recombination, n = 2 for depletion-region recombination; radiation-induced current usually falls between 1 and 2. Deck 04 p. 34's bullets call the depletion-region component IB2, while its drawing and the 2001 Short Course call it IB1: identify it by ideality near 2 and its place at the emitter–base junction surface. Shows radiation-induced excess base current at low bias while IC is unchanged. M06 §7, M08 §9
Lateral PNP A bipolar geometry in which carriers travel laterally just below the surface. Typically very sensitive to total dose, because the transit runs adjacent to the oxide rather than crossing it once. Ordering: lateral pnp > substrate pnp > vertical pnp > vertical npn. In a pnp the oxide charge accumulates the n-type base surface and moderates the increase, so interface traps dominate; in an npn both charges raise the base current. The most radiation-soft bipolar geometry, and common at the inputs of linear ICs. M06 §7, M08 §9
Field oxide
FOX, isolation oxide; LOCOS or STI		 The thick oxide isolating one transistor from the next. LOCOS is grown through a mask; STI is an etched trench, filled over a thin grown liner. 200–1000 nm. Areal charge and oxide voltage both grow with thickness, so the penalty goes as t², two to four orders beyond a gate oxide. Isolation scaled far more slowly than gate oxide, roughly a micrometre of LOCOS to a few hundred nanometres of STI. LOCOS risk is the graded bird's beak at high field; STI risk is poor-quality deposited oxide. Trapped charge inverts the p-type surface beneath it, opening a leakage path: the primary total-dose failure mechanism in advanced CMOS. Commercial ICs can fail at 5 krad(SiO2); STI inverts at 50–100 krad(SiO₂). M06 §8, M08 §§5, 8
Guard ring
channel stop		 Heavily doped implant of the substrate type placed under the field oxide, drawn as a ring around a device. Raises the parasitic threshold through its depletion charge, so trapped positive charge that would invert a lightly doped surface no longer can — up to a dose: under 500 nm, inversion needs ~1.1×10¹¹ cm⁻² at 10¹⁵ cm⁻³ against ~1.1×10¹² cm⁻² at 10¹⁷ cm⁻³ (M08's arithmetic, not a slide value). Hardens the inter-device field-oxide leakage path, and separately lowers single-event latchup susceptibility. M06 §8, M08 §6
Enclosed layout transistor
ELT; edgeless		 Transistor whose gate is drawn as a closed ring, one diffusion inside and one outside. Effective W = perimeter through the middle of the ring (the course's rule; it overstates the corners), so W/L is set by geometry and there is a minimum device size. The two diffusions are not symmetric. The gate never crosses a thin-to-thick oxide edge, so no parasitic edge path exists. No leakage increase to 2 Mrad(SiO2) in 0.25 µm. M08 §6
Buried oxide
BOX		 The insulator under the silicon film of an SOI transistor. Partially depleted film: the neutral body screens the back interface from the front gate. Fully depleted film: no neutral body, front and back gates couple, and buried-oxide charge moves the front threshold directly, so FD is the more sensitive. Charge trapped in it inverts the underside of the body, connecting source to drain where the gate has no control. M06 §8, M08 §7
Back-channel leakage Source-to-drain conduction along the underside of an SOI silicon film, through an inversion layer formed by charge trapped in the buried oxide. In a partially depleted film nearly independent of the top gate voltage, unlike edge leakage, which the gate does control; the two together give a stepped characteristic. The total-dose penalty SOI pays for its single-event advantage. Broken by the BUSFET. M06 §8, M08 §7
BUSFET
body-under-source FET		 SOI transistor whose source is bottomed by body silicon rather than reaching down to the buried oxide. Interrupts the back-channel path at its source end, so it cannot connect source to drain whatever the buried oxide holds. Structural hardening against BOX leakage, without changing the oxide. M08 §7
Equivalent oxide thickness
EOT		 Thickness of SiO2 that would give the same capacitance per unit area as the actual gate dielectric. tEOT = tkεox/εk, summed over a stack. A capacitance equivalence only — the physical layer is thicker, generates more pairs per unit area, and traps by its own defect chemistry. Why total dose is still specified in rad(SiO2) even for high-κ stacks. M04 §8, M10 §4
Charge transfer inefficiency
CTI = 1 − CTE		 Fraction of a charge packet left behind in one CCD transfer. A packet making n transfers retains CTEn; CTE ≥ 0.9999 keeps the loss under 10% over 1000 transfers. A background charge (fat zero) fills traps and improves CTI, at the cost of noise. Multiplied by the length of the readout chain, so a tiny per-transfer loss smears an image. The CCD effect most directly tied to displacement damage. M06 §9, M12 §6
p–i–n photodiode Diode with a thick, almost undoped region between heavily doped contacts, fully depleted under reverse bias. Every pair generated inside is swept out before it can recombine, so collected charge equals generated charge and the current is the energy-absorption rate divided by Ep/q. The standard X-ray dosimeter in this field. M04 §8

Single-event mechanisms and rate prediction

TermWhat it isKey factsWhy it matters
Charge per unit length
Qcol		 Charge an ion deposits per micrometre of track, which Deck 03 p. 26 calls the total deposited charge. Qcol = 1.6×10⁻²·LET·ρ/Ep pC/µm. Si: 10.4 fC/µm per unit LET; LET 97 → 1 pC/µm. Qcol (pC/µm) is not Qcoll (collected, C). Multiplied by a collection depth, it gives the charge compared with Qcrit. M11 §1
Critical charge
Qcrit		 Minimum collected charge that flips a node. Qcrit = CnVcrit; 1 fF × 1 V = 1 fC. Can be below 50 fC (3×10⁵ electrons) in unhardened modern circuits. Set by the technology and circuit, never by the incident ion. M05 §4, M11 §4
Plasma wire The track's column of electron–hole pairs, far denser than the doping, acting as a conductor. Carries the node potential down the track for as long as its density exceeds the doping. Creates the field funnel and the fast drift component of the pulse. M11 §2
Field funnel
funnelling		 Transient extension of a reverse-biased junction's field down an ion track. Collects by drift from below the depletion width; lasts while track density exceeds the doping, so heavier doping shortens it. Hsieh, Murley and O'Brien 1981; McLean and Oldham 1982 model. Why a reverse-biased p–n junction on a high-impedance node is the structure most susceptible to single events. M11 §3
Double-exponential current pulse I(t) = I0(e−t/τα − e−t/τβ), the circuit-simulation model of a collected pulse. Rise tens of ps, fall 200–300 ps (fall time ≈ 2.2τα, so τα ≈ 90–140 ps); Q = I0(τα − τβ). Omits the diffusion tail. The standard stimulus when the node is slower than the pulse. M11 §2
Feedback–recovery race The SRAM upset contest between the opposite inverter latching the disturbed value and the restoring transistor pulling the struck node back. Feedback time ≈ RC of the inverter pair; feedback resistors or added capacitance slow it. The transient acts like a write pulse. Sets Qcrit of a static cell and the hardening options. M11 §4
Threshold LET
onset LET, L0		 Lowest LET that produces events. Measures Qcrit: Lth ≈ Qcrit[fC]/(10.4·d[µm]). Falls with scaling. Enters the rate steeply, as 1/L². M11 §§6, 8
Saturated cross-section
σsat		 The plateau of the cross-section against LET. Related to the total sensitive area; cm²/device or cm²/bit. Enters the rate linearly. M11 §6
Weibull fit σ(L) = σsat{1 − exp[−((L − L0)/W)s]} for L > L0, zero below. 63% of σsat at L0 + W; s sets the sharpness. Count a multiple-bit event once. The standard parameterization for rate calculations, and CREME96's input. M11 §6
Effective LET LET/cos θ for a track at angle θ from the normal through a thin sensitive layer. ×1.41 at 45°, ×2 at 60°; effective fluence Φ cos θ. Fails for SOI films, for funnel and diffusion collection in bulk, and for protons and neutrons. Lets one ion species map more of the cross-section curve. M11 §7
IRPP method
integral rectangular parallelepiped		 The rate calculation used by CREME96: the sensitive volume as a box of given depth, integrated over the chord lengths of an isotropic flux. Takes the Weibull cross-section and the box depth. The step idealization, σsatΦ(>Lth) at normal incidence, corresponds to old CREME's "critical charge" option, which Tylka calls inaccurate for space. How the rate integral R = ∫σ(L)Φ(L)dL is evaluated in practice. M11 §8
SEU figure of merit FOM = σsat/L0.25², multiplied by an orbit constant for errors per bit per day. L0.25 = L0 + W·0.2881/s, the LET at 25% of σsat. Petersen et al. 1983, in the 2024 Short Course form. A quick rate estimate that shows the threshold entering squared. M11 §8

Displacement damage

TermWhat it isKey factsWhy it matters
Maximum transferable energy
Tmax		 The largest recoil energy an elastic collision can give, in a head-on collision. 4mM/(m+M)²·E = 0.133E for protons and neutrons in Si: 1 MeV n → 133 keV, 10 MeV p → 1.33 MeV. Electrons, relativistic: 1 MeV → 155 eV; no displacement below ~0.22 MeV. Electrons cannot make cascades, so electron and Co-60 damage is point defects only. M12 §1
Recoil spectrum
PKA spectrum		 The distribution of energies given to struck lattice atoms by one particle species at one energy. Coulomb (Rutherford) scattering: dσ/dT ∝ 1/T², mean ≈ 200–300 eV. Neutrons: flat (hard-sphere) up to Tmax. In Si, Coulomb dominates below ~10 MeV protons and nuclear reactions above ~20 MeV. Decides whether the damage is isolated point defects or clusters, and so whether NIEL scaling holds. M12 §§1–2, 4
Cluster, cascade, subcascade Free point defects below El ≈ 1–2 keV; one dense cascade up to Eu ≈ 12–20 keV (Deck 03: 10–20); separate subcascades along the recoil path above that. 50 keV Si recoil: cascade ~80 nm long, 2–3 terminal clusters ~5 nm across. Proton markers: 6–10 MeV and >20 MeV. Point defects and clusters survive recombination differently — the origin of the departures from NIEL scaling. M12 §§2, 4
Damage energy
T·L(T), Ed		 The part of a recoil's energy spent on atomic collisions rather than on ionization. Ceiling ~300 keV for one Si recoil and ~2 MeV in GaAs, however energetic the recoil. NRT: Nd = κEd/(2Eth), κ = 0.8, Eth = 21 eV. What the NRT count uses and what NIEL integrates. M12 §2
E centre, A centre, divacancy Stable vacancy complexes in irradiated Si: V–P (E centre), V–O (A centre), V–V (divacancy). E centre and a divacancy level ~0.44 eV below Ec; A centre ~0.17 eV below. Stable to ~150 °C (E), ~350 °C (A), ~300 °C (V–V). The E centre is the dominant CTI trap in n-channel CCDs at moderately high temperatures. M12 §§2–3, 6
Lifetime damage constant
Kτ		 The constant in 1/τ = 1/τ0 + Φ/Kτ. cm⁻²·s. Depends on particle, energy, doping, injection, temperature and time after irradiation. Diffusion-length form 1/L² = 1/L0² + Φ/(DKτ). The base of the Messenger–Spratt gain law and of solar-cell degradation. M12 §§3, 6
Carrier removal Deep radiation-induced levels capture majority carriers from the shallow dopants, lowering the free-carrier concentration. Acts first on lightly doped regions such as a bipolar collector or a solar-cell base; appears after lifetime loss. Can fail silicon solar cells at high electron fluence. M12 §§3, 6
Short-term and long-term annealing
displacement damage		 Recovery of displacement damage after irradiation by recombination and rearrangement of defects. Frenkel pairs recombine in <1 ms and stable complexes form within seconds; short-term device recovery takes minutes to ~1 h, long-term runs slowly to ~1 yr. Si anneals at room temperature; unbiased GaAs needs ≥200 °C; forward bias enhances annealing. Every damage constant must carry its time and temperature. M12 §5
Universal dark-current damage factor
Kdark		 Mean dark generation per unit depleted volume per unit DDD (Srour and Lo). (1.9 ± 0.6)×10⁵ carriers·cm⁻³·s⁻¹ per MeV/g at 300 K, one week after irradiation. Predicts the mean imager dark current from DDD and depleted volume, not the pixel distribution. M12 §6
Hot pixel
field-enhanced dark current		 A pixel whose dark current is an order of magnitude or more above the mean, from a nuclear reaction or a defect in a high-field region (~10⁵ V/cm). Field-enhanced generation has an activation energy as low as ~0.4 eV against 0.63–0.65 eV, so it persists on cooling. TID gives a pedestal across the array; displacement damage gives this high-side tail. Sets the worst pixels in CCDs and active pixel sensors. M08 §9, M12 §6
Random telegraph signal
RTS		 A pixel's dark current switching at random between discrete levels, from a bistable displacement defect. Seen in all proton-irradiated CCDs and APS examined (1.5–60 MeV); switching times seconds to hours near room temperature. ~1.6×10⁻⁵ per incident 10 MeV proton, ~40% of 20 µm pixels after 10¹⁰ p/cm². Defeats dark-frame calibration. M12 §6
Trailing
CTI smear		 Charge captured from a packet by bulk traps and released into the packets that follow. Capture τc = 1/(σvthns); emission τe ∝ exp(Et/kT). Worst for small signals and low background. CCD02 at 7.2×10⁹ 10 MeV p/cm²: 150 e⁻ background → CTI ~5×10⁻⁴; 16,000 e⁻ → ~7×10⁻⁵. The image-level form of CTI. M12 §6
Fat zero A deliberately raised background charge in every CCD pixel. Keeps bulk traps filled, lowering CTI, at the cost of shot noise. Charge injection ahead of the signal does the same. An electrical countermeasure to displacement damage in CCDs, beside cooling. M12 §6

Testing and hardness assurance

TermWhat it isKey factsWhy it matters
Hardness assurance
piece-part		 The procedures, controls and tests that ensure a purchased part responds to radiation within known and acceptable limits; Pease drops the controls for commercial parts. Per part type and application: failure definition, measured mean failure dose, design-margin category, lot-test regime. Turns the oxide physics into an accept-or-reject decision on flight lots. M15 §§1–2
Radiation lot acceptance test
RLAT		 Destructive radiation test of a random sample from each purchased lot. Lot = date-code lot (same assembly week) at minimum; wafer or process lot preferred. The lecture's test: about 11 parts, zero failures, at the specification dose. Total-dose response varies lot to lot — four lots of one part differed more than 10× in supply current — so a result transfers only to the lot sampled. M15 §§1–2
Radiation design margin
RDM		 Geometric-mean failure dose of a part type divided by the specification dose at the part. Pease's breakpoints: <1–2 unacceptable; 1–2 to 10 HCC-1; 10–100 HCC-2; >100 HNC. RDM = 1 means half fail. Sets how much testing each lot needs. M15 §2
HCC-1, HCC-2, HNC Hardness-critical category 1 (test every lot), category 2 (periodic sample testing), hardness non-critical (no further radiation testing). For a 20 krad spec: HCC-2 needs a 200 krad mean, HNC 2 Mrad. For commercial parts Pease drops HCC-2 and places the HCC-1/HNC breakpoint at about twice the PCC. Most realistic margins land in HCC-1, the common case, which is why every lot of such a part is tested. M15 §2
Part categorization criterion
PCC		 PCC = exp(KTLs), a margin set by the measured spread s of ln(failure dose). Lognormal assumed. KTL ≈ 4.2 (n = 15, Ps 0.999, C 0.90); 3.1 (n = 18, 0.99, 0.90). Pease's categorization for commercial parts, and the variables lot-acceptance criterion. M15 §2
LTPD
lot tolerance percent defective		 The percent defective a sampling plan rejects at the stated confidence. Ps = (1 − C)1/n: 11 parts, 0 failures → Ps ≈ 0.81 at C = 0.90, stated as LTPD 20. Ps = 0.999 needs ~2300 parts (Pease: 2303). Shows how little an attributes test at the specification dose demonstrates, and why overtesting is used. M15 §2
Overtest factor Ratio of test dose to specification dose in a lot test. Pease (σln ≤ 0.5, Ps 0.999, C 0.90, zero failures): n = 5 / 10 / 11 / 15 / 20 → 3.97 / 3.11 / 3.02 / 2.75 / 2.53. Does not rescue ELDRS parts. Eleven parts at ~3× the specification give the assurance of ~2303 at it. M15 §§2, 7
Characterization and qualification Characterization maps the response over many doses, biases, rates and anneals and finds the worst case; qualification and lot acceptance run a fixed procedure at that worst case. Characterization's bar is understanding; qualification's is precision and documentation. Worst-case bias and accelerated-test conditions come from characterization. M15 §§2, 6, 8
Worst-case bias The bias during irradiation that produces the largest response in the parameter that fails. SiO₂ gate oxide: positive gate at the operating field (n-channel on). Grounded pins leave only the built-in potential and under-test; raising the field above operating does not overtest. SOI buried oxide: off-state or transmission-gate bias. High-κ pMOS: irradiate off, then bias-temperature stress on. The field sets yield, transport direction, trapping and interface-trap buildup, so a test at the wrong bias does not bound the part. M09 §3, M15 §6
MIL-STD-883 Test Method 1019
TM 1019		 US military total-dose test procedure. MOS: Co-60 at 50–300 rad(Si)/s, worst-case bias, test. Extended room-temperature anneal if required (3.11.1, after a failure); accelerated-anneal test if required (3.12.1), with a 0.5× overtest if required (3.12.2.a.2) and one of three anneals, routinely 168 h biased at 100 °C. Linear bipolar: ELDRS test needed (3.13)? No → Condition A; yes → C, D or E. Bounds each charge separately; neither step reproduces the orbital response. M08 §3, M15 §§5, 8
100 °C bake
rebound bake, 168 h		 Biased post-irradiation anneal at 100 °C for one week. Drives ΔVit to its plateau (~0.65 V, 32 nm oxide, 300 krad(SiO₂)) by ~4×10³ s, flat past a week, while removing most trapped holes. 100 °C is below the >125 °C interface-trap anneal. Bounds interface-trap failures (timing, drive, positive n-channel shift); cannot predict leakage. M09 §9, M15 §§4–5
Condition D
TM 1019 low-dose-rate test		 Linear-bipolar ELDRS test at ≤ 10 mrad(Si)/s to 1.5 × the specification dose. 100 krad spec → 150 krad at 10 mrad(Si)/s ≈ 174 days. Still at least ten times faster than average space rates, ~10⁻³ to below 10⁻⁴ rad/s. The default route for ELDRS-sensitive parts. M15 §8
ESCC 22900 European space total-dose test standard. Two dose-rate windows, 2–10 and 0.01–0.1 rad/s (Pease); the course textbook cites 100 °C for 168 h as its routine MOS rebound test too. Contrast with TM 1019's 50–300 rad(Si)/s. M15 §5
Elevated-temperature irradiation
ETI		 High-dose-rate irradiation with the part held hot, to speed hole transport and mimic low-rate damage. Accelerates but often does not bound: in-situ annealing competes, and the optimum temperature falls as dose rises. LM139 at 30 krad: ~40 nA high rate, ~90 nA at 100 °C, 300–600 nA low rate. ASTM F1892 revised to use it as a screen only. Why the standard's accelerated route, Condition E, needs characterization. M15 §8
Temperature-switching irradiation
TSI		 Irradiation at a fixed moderate rate while stepping the temperature down each time the response begins to saturate. LM833 at 5.8 rad(Si)/s, 120 → 100 → 80 °C at 20 and 80 krad(Si): 4.1 µA at 100 krad against 1.8 µA low rate and 0.56 µA high rate (Li et al. 2019). Tuned per part type. An accelerated test that bounded the low-rate response. M15 §8
Bias-temperature stress
BTS		 A gate held at elevated temperature under field, without radiation; its drift in service is bias-temperature instability. High-κ pMOS, 1 Mrad(SiO₂) at + bias, then −BTS (75 °C, 10 min, 2 MV/cm): −0.33 V against −0.11 V for the two separate shifts summed. Makes the worst case a sequence: irradiated off, annealed on. M15 §6
Hydrogen soak Exposure of a bipolar part to hydrogen before irradiation. 10 krad(SiO₂) at 40 rad(SiO₂)/s: excess base current 6 → ~40 nA after 6–43 h of soak, exceeding the unsoaked 20 mrad(SiO₂)/s response (~24 nA) after ~2 h. Evidence that hydrogen drives ELDRS; not a screen, because it gives good parts ELDRS. M15 §9
PETS
pre-irradiation elevated-temperature stress		 Any heating of a part before irradiation: burn-in, package sealing, solder reflow, rework, storage, deliberate bakes. MOS: 150 °C burn-in cut ΔVit 1.48 → 0.97 V at 1 Mrad(SiO₂); bias-independent effects point to H₂ diffusion. Bipolar LM111: 175 °C lowers the 0.1 rad(SiO₂)/s response; 450 °C for 200 s raises the 50 rad(SiO₂)/s response ×5–10. Test samples must share the flight parts' burn-in, preconditioning and packaging. M15 §10
Burn-in Reliability screen operating parts at elevated temperature under near-operating bias to remove early failures. Radiation test samples receive burn-in if and only if the flight parts do (TM 1019, as the lecture states it). Reduces interface-trap buildup, which can worsen leakage where interface traps had compensated trapped holes. M15 §10
