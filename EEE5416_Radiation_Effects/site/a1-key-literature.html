Key Literature: A Field Map

The published work this course is built on, arranged by field: who established what, the result the course's slides attach to each paper, and the module that teaches the physics behind it. A map for reading the primary literature and for placing a project topic against the work already done.

Prerequisites M02; read alongside any module Read time ~40 min Sources Deck 02 and the two lectures that walked through it; Dodd & Massengill (2003); Tylka et al. (1997); course textbook

Radiation effects in electronics is a research field whose results arrive as papers, and the course cites them by author name throughout. Deck 02 surveys the field through its most highly cited papers. It opens with two reviews published by D. M. Fleetwood in 2021 in the IEEE Transactions on Nuclear Science: one measures the citation impact of the outstanding conference papers of the IEEE Nuclear and Space Radiation Effects Conference, the other surveys the field as transistor scaling ends, which its title calls a post-Moore world. This appendix rearranges that survey into a map. Each entry gives the full citation, the claim the deck attaches to the paper, a sentence or two placing it, and a link to the module where the physics is taught, so that a name met in a lecture or a paper can be traced to its content.

Three institutions recur in the citations. The IEEE Nuclear and Space Radiation Effects Conference (NSREC) is the annual conference of the field; papers presented there are published, after review, in the IEEE Transactions on Nuclear Science (abbreviated IEEE Trans. Nucl. Sci. or TNS), which is why most entries below appear in that journal. Each NSREC opens with a short course whose notes are tutorial reviews of one area; several are in the course readings, and they are the efficient way into a literature too large to read paper by paper. The remaining entries are technical reports of US government laboratories — the Army's Harry Diamond Laboratories, the Naval Research Laboratory, and NASA's National Space Science Data Center and Goddard Space Flight Center — papers in electronics, physics and instrumentation journals, and a few books. Citations here take the form authors, journal, volume, first page (year). The citation counts printed on the slides are the deck's own snapshot and grow every year; this page quotes one only where the deck draws a conclusion from it.

Section 1 sets out how the field grew and how its parts connect. Sections 2 to 5 take the fields in the order the course teaches them — the environment, total ionizing dose, single-event effects and displacement damage — and section 6 the tools that join them into a prediction. Section 7 records where the deck places the frontier. Where the slides and the published record differ on a bibliographic detail, the page gives the published record and says so.

1 · How the field grew

Deck 02 p. 2 classifies high-impact radiation-effects work by the physical mechanism it studies, and names three. Total ionizing dose is the long-term effect of ionization in insulators: charge trapped in oxides and at their interfaces, accumulating over a mission. Displacement damage is lattice damage to semiconductors, atoms knocked from their sites by collisions. Single-event effects are the short-term collection, in a semiconductor, of the charge that one particle frees along its track. To these three the deck adds two subjects that cut across them: the effects on devices and integrated circuits, and the space environments that supply the radiation. The three mechanism classes are the ones M05 §1 derives by crossing two questions — does the energy go into ionization or into atomic displacement, and does the damage accumulate or arrive with a single particle — and the rest of this page is organized by them.

Deck 02 p. 3 compresses the history into five milestones. In 1979 May and Woods reported the first upsets caused by alpha particles. The deck's 1980s milestone is the space-environment models: the trapped-belt models AE-8 and AP-8, and models of the cosmic-ray flux (on the dates of the two belt models, see the note below). The 1990s brought single-event studies in avionics, associated with Normand, and single-event and total-dose standards from JEDEC, the microelectronics industry's standards body, and from the European Space Agency. The 2000s milestone is the effect of deep-submicron CMOS scaling, together with radiation testing of wide-bandgap power devices in gallium nitride and silicon carbide. The 2010s and 2020s extend the field to cryogenic operation, microelectronics for artificial intelligence, and reliability in extreme environments. Figure 1 places those milestones above the papers this page tabulates, by field.

AP-8 predates the 1980s. Deck 02 p. 3 lists AE-8 and AP-8 under its 1980s milestone. AP-8 was published in December 1976 (D. M. Sawyer and J. I. Vette, NSSDC/WDC-A-R&S 76-06, NASA Goddard Space Flight Center), and AE-8 is documented in Vette's NSSDC report of 1991 (§2). Both maps rest on satellite data of the 1960s and 1970s. The milestone stands as the slide gives it; the publication dates matter when either model is cited or compared with its successors.

Three features of Figure 1 carry through the rest of the page. The deck's total-dose entries begin with two process papers, of 1971 and 1975, which established that the growth conditions and the thickness of a gate oxide set its radiation response; the papers that explain why follow from 1980. The single-event entries begin with a prediction of 1962 and a single observation in orbit in 1975, and then multiply in 1978–79, when upsets were modelled for space memories, found in terrestrial dynamic memories, traced to protons and neutrons, and joined by the first report of latchup (Dodd and Massengill's history, §4). The displacement-damage entries are fewer and concentrate on one question, whether a single energy-loss quantity predicts damage across particles and energies (§5).

Deck 02 p. 2 reads the whole record as a progression from fundamental physics, to device-level effects, to modelling, to system reliability. The same deck, p. 3, names the four things the papers provide, and the four serve equally well to classify any new paper: a physics foundation (radiation spectra and damage processes), experimental baselines (measured total-dose and single-event data on microelectronics), modelling references (NIEL, Geant4, and TCAD reliability modelling), and system-level practice (avionics error rates, and JEDEC and NASA standards). Each term is explained where it is used: NIEL in §5, Geant4 and TCAD in §6. The endpoint of the progression is the chain of Deck 02 p. 30, drawn as Figure 6: environment models feed transport models, which feed device, circuit and system response, and every level contributes to a predicted error or failure rate.

One lecture recommendation governs how to use this map. The NSREC short-course notes summarize most of the papers listed here, so reading the short course for an area is the efficient first step; the individual papers are for the result a project depends on. The course readings include the NSREC short-course notebooks of 2001 (radiation effects in SOI, bipolar and CMOS technologies), 2018 (variability in environments, devices and radiation effects, including the space-environment notes that Deck 02 p. 4 draws on) and 2024 (basic mechanisms of total dose, displacement damage and single events, and sensors), Xapsos' 2006 short-course section on modelling the space radiation environment, the 2021 short course on image sensors, and Dodd and Massengill's 2003 review of single-event upset.

2 · The radiation environment

Every calculation in the course starts from a particle spectrum at the device, and the environment papers are where those spectra come from. M02 teaches the three natural populations of space — protons and electrons trapped in the geomagnetic field, galactic cosmic rays arriving from outside the solar system, and the protons and heavier ions of solar particle events — and M03 the neutrons that cosmic rays produce in the atmosphere. The papers in this section turned each population into a model a designer can run for a given orbit and mission. Deck 02 p. 4 states their collective result as having defined the background for all microelectronics radiation research. The sister course in space electronics treats the same populations from the mission side: EEL5385 M05 §5–§7 for solar particles and galactic cosmic rays, and EEL5385 M06 §6 for the trapped belts.

Three kinds of model appear. A trapped-particle model is an empirical map of flux against energy in the magnetic coordinates of M02 §6, built from satellite measurements; AE-8 and AP-8 are the long-standing pair for trapped electrons and protons, static maps for solar maximum and solar minimum that return long-term averages (M02 §11). A cosmic-ray flux model gives the spectrum of every nuclear species outside the magnetosphere as a function of the solar cycle, and the geomagnetic transmission that filters it down to a given orbit. A transport model follows a spectrum through matter — shielding, a spacecraft wall, the atmosphere — and returns the altered spectrum or the dose behind it. The code that brought cosmic-ray and solar-particle models, geomagnetic transmission and transport together for electronics is CREME, the Cosmic Ray Effects on Micro-Electronics code.

3 · Total ionizing dose

Total ionizing dose is the cumulative effect of ionization in the insulators of a device. Deck 02 p. 5 writes the chain as radiation, dose in the oxide, electron–hole pair generation, trapped charge, and a shift of the threshold voltage — the chain M07 §1 develops step by step, with the device consequence of M08 §1. The deck gives this field more pages than any other (pp. 5–15), and its literature follows a sequence of questions: which oxides survive, why, which defects are responsible, how the two trapped charges are told apart, and where the failure moved as the technology changed. Figure 3 groups the papers by those questions.

The mechanism and its standard references

Deck 02 p. 5 calls its band diagram, after McLean and Oldham's 1987 report, the original version of the famous total-dose mechanism. The diagram is of an n-channel transistor under positive gate bias. Ionizing radiation generates electron–hole pairs in the SiO₂; the electrons leave the oxide quickly; the holes move toward the silicon by hopping transport through localized states in the oxide bulk; some are trapped deep, near the Si/SiO₂ interface, as positive oxide-trapped charge; and the interaction of holes with the interface region creates interface traps, states within the silicon bandgap. Three documents carry that account, and Deck 02 p. 31 names the Oldham–McLean work as the framework for total dose in oxides that is still applied today.

Hardening by process, and the mechanism behind it

Hardening by process means choosing how an oxide is grown, annealed and sized so that it traps less charge per unit dose (M10 §9). Deck 02 p. 8 draws two conclusions from the earliest papers: oxide thickness plays a key role in radiation-induced degradation, and the fabrication process is very important. Its data are threshold and flat-band shifts rising steeply with oxide thickness after 10⁶ rad at +10 V, for etched-back and as-grown oxides, and a flat-band shift that is small at negative gate bias and rises sharply at positive bias. Deck 02 p. 9 then turns to interface traps. Switched bias is the experiment that exposed their mechanism: capacitors are irradiated under positive bias and the bias is reversed at a chosen time afterwards. On the deck's data (wet oxide, 96.5 nm, 0.6 Mrad from a LINAC, field 4 MV/cm), reversing to negative bias stops the growth of the interface-trap density during its second, slow stage, which identifies that stage as the drift of a positive ion to the silicon interface.

Naming the defects and separating the charges

Two measurement techniques turned the mechanism into numbers. Electron spin resonance (ESR) detects defects that carry an unpaired electron spin by their resonant absorption of microwaves in a magnetic field; the resonance position identifies the defect, and the signal strength counts it. The midgap technique rests on an experimental observation: when the silicon surface potential sits at midgap, the interface traps are net neutral, so the shift of the midgap point of a subthreshold current–voltage curve measures oxide-trapped charge alone, and the rest of the threshold shift is interface-trap charge (M07 §9). Border traps, the third category, are near-interfacial oxide traps that exchange charge with the silicon on the time scale of a measurement; the charges an oxide carries before any irradiation are classified in Advanced Microelectronics, EEE6338 M05b §1. Deck 02 p. 12 also names two results on the time behaviour of these charges, threshold-voltage rebound and the reversibility of oxide-trap annealing; the course textbook's reference lists supply the papers behind them.

Where the failure moved

Once the mechanism was established, the literature followed it into three structures the early papers did not consider. Isolation oxides — the field oxide or shallow-trench isolation (STI) that separates neighbouring transistors — are 200 to 1000 nm thick, far thicker than gate oxides, and positive charge trapped in them inverts the adjacent p-type silicon and opens a leakage path between the source and drain of a transistor (Deck 02 p. 12; M08 §5). At ultra-high doses, the regime of particle-accelerator electronics, charge trapped in the STI and in the spacer oxides beside the gate changes transistors in ways that depend on their width and length (Deck 02 p. 13). And in linear bipolar circuits the thick, low-field oxide over the emitter–base junction degrades more at a low dose rate than at a high one, which is enhanced low-dose-rate sensitivity (ELDRS; Deck 02 pp. 14–15; M08 §9).

4 · Single-event effects

A single-event effect is the response of a device to the charge that one ionizing particle frees along its track: the charge is collected at a junction, and if it exceeds what a circuit node can absorb, the node changes state or a parasitic structure turns on (M05 §4). Deck 02 pp. 16–17 draw the sequence the whole field studies — ion strike, charge deposition, funnelling, transient current, circuit upset — and its literature splits into two lines. Soft errors change the information a circuit stores or carries and leave the device undamaged; destructive events trigger a current or a field that damages the device or holds it in a state only a power cycle clears (M05 §5). Figure 4 follows both lines from the first prediction to the current work on power devices.

From prediction to a recognized effect, 1962–1982

Dodd and Massengill's 2003 review opens with the history, and it is the source for the sequence below. The first paper to treat upsets from single particles concerned terrestrial microelectronics. In 1962 Wallmark and Marcus, assessing how far terrestrial microelectronics could be scaled, forecast that terrestrial cosmic rays would cause upsets and that these would limit the minimum device to about 10 µm on a side. The first confirmed observation in space followed thirteen years later: Binder, Smith and Holman reported at the 1975 NSREC four upsets in 17 years of satellite operation in bipolar J–K flip-flops on a communications satellite, attributed to galactic cosmic rays. They located the sensitive transistors with scanning-electron-microscope exposures and, with a diffusion model of charge collection, predicted an upset rate within a factor of two of the one observed. Dodd and Massengill suggest that the count was too small to establish the importance of the effect at once: significant numbers of single-event papers did not appear at the NSREC until 1978–79.

Those years changed the field. Pickel and Blandford (1978) analysed cosmic-ray errors in MOS memory cells and formulated the first models for predicting system error rates. May and Woods (1979), at Intel, found soft errors in dynamic random-access memories (DRAMs) — in which a bit is a charge on a small capacitor — as integration reached 16 and 64 kbit, and traced them to alpha particles emitted by uranium and thorium present at parts-per-million levels in the packaging materials. An alpha particle crossing the die frees enough electron–hole pairs near a storage node to cause a single-bit error. Low-activity packaging materials and on-chip shielding coatings then suppressed the terrestrial problem for several years. In the same year two groups, Wyatt and co-workers and Guenzer, Wolicki and Allas, reported errors caused by protons and neutrons through nuclear reactions (indirect ionization, M01 §7); Guenzer's paper introduced the term single-event upset. Destructive single events

The destructive modes are separate mechanisms with separate literatures (M11 §10). In single-event latchup (SEL) the collected charge turns on the parasitic p-n-p-n thyristor inherent in bulk CMOS and a supply current flows until power is removed. In single-event gate rupture (SEGR) an ion crossing a gate oxide under a strong field triggers its dielectric breakdown. In single-event burnout (SEB) the charge collected in a power device, which holds a high reverse voltage across a high-resistance region, triggers a regenerative current that the device cannot dissipate and that destroys it. Single-event leakage current (SELC) is the permanent increase of leakage that ion strikes leave in a power device without burning it out. All four depend on the ion's LET and on the applied voltage. A latched thyristor stays on only if the supply exceeds its holding voltage, the lowest voltage that sustains the latched current, and the latchup cross-section rises with temperature. Gate rupture depends on the gate and drain voltages, and burnout and leakage current on the reverse voltage the device holds (Deck 03 pp. 30–33 for the last three).

5 · Displacement damage

Displacement damage is the lattice damage an incident particle causes by colliding with atomic nuclei. The struck atom, the primary knock-on atom (PKA), is driven from its site and, if it carries enough energy, displaces further atoms in turn; the vacancies, interstitials and clusters left behind introduce energy levels in the bandgap that shorten minority-carrier lifetime (M04 §6, M05 §3). Deck 02 p. 23 states where it matters: mainly in solar cells, charge-coupled devices, bipolar transistors and other minority-carrier devices. The literature the deck cites is compact and turns on two questions — what defect structure a recoil of given energy produces, and whether one energy-loss quantity predicts the damage done by different particles at different energies. That quantity is the non-ionizing energy loss (NIEL), the part of a particle's energy loss per unit mass thickness that goes into displacing atoms rather than into ionization (M04 §6; the sister course derives it in EEL5385 M11 §4).

6 · Tools and models

A predicted error or failure rate is the end of a chain of models, and Deck 02 p. 30 draws the chain: radiation-environment models feed radiation-transport models, which feed device, circuit and system response, every level contributes to the predicted rate, and experiments validate the device and circuit levels. The tools in this section each cover part of that chain. Two methods recur. A Monte Carlo calculation follows individual particle histories, drawing each interaction at random from its probability distribution, and accumulates statistics over many histories; it is needed because particle transport through a real geometry has no closed-form solution (M02 §11). TCAD (technology computer-aided design) device simulation solves the semiconductor transport equations numerically in a two- or three-dimensional device structure, which is how the collection of a track's charge is computed (M11 §2, §11). Deck 02 p. 31 credits CREME96, MUSCA-SEP and Geant4 with making statistical and physics-based predictions of single-event rates possible, and calls simulation a necessary complement to irradiation experiments, because access to test facilities is limited and scaling makes devices harder to test.

7 · Where the field is going

Deck 02 p. 31 closes with five take-aways. The first three summarize §§2–6: the classic papers, Oldham and McLean's on total dose in oxides and Messenger and Ash's on single events among them, established theoretical frameworks for charge generation, trapping, annealing and single-event charge collection that modern simulation tools still rest on; NASA, ESA and Department of Defense programmes supplied decades of benchmark data; and CREME96, MUSCA-SEP and Geant4 made statistical and physics-based rate prediction possible. The last two point forward, and with the deck's milestone for the 2010s and 2020s — cryogenic operation, microelectronics for artificial intelligence, reliability in extreme environments — they define the frontier. The table pairs each direction with the part of the course whose physics it builds on, which is the practical use of this section when a project topic is being chosen.

Two terms need definition. A wide-bandgap semiconductor has a bandgap several times that of silicon; silicon carbide (SiC), gallium nitride (GaN) and gallium oxide (Ga₂O₃) are used for high-voltage power switching, and the charge a particle frees in them and the fields they hold differ from silicon's. Combined stressors are exposures in which total dose, displacement damage and single events act on the same part during one mission, so that one effect changes the device's response to the next. The deck's first take-away names G. C. Messenger and M. S. Ash's work on single events, which is their monograph Single Event Phenomena (Chapman & Hall, 1997); their earlier The Effects of Radiation on Electronic Systems (Van Nostrand Reinhold, 1986) is a unified treatment of the whole field.

Where this leaves us

The literature of radiation effects divides by mechanism into total ionizing dose, displacement damage and single-event effects, with the environment and the tools running across all three. Each field has a small canon. For the environment it is the trapped-particle maps, CREME and its 1997 revision, and Normand's avionics and ground-level work. For total dose it is McLean and Oldham's 1987 report and their 2003 review, with the process, hydrogen, ESR, midgap and border-trap papers behind them and the isolation, ultra-high-dose and ELDRS papers after them. For single events it begins with Binder, Smith and Holman's 1975 report of upsets in orbit and May and Woods' 1979 report of alpha-particle upsets in memories, and runs through funnelling, the Dodd–Massengill review and the destructive modes of power devices. For displacement damage it is Summers' regimes and his NIEL correlation, with Srour's review.

The tools join these into a prediction: CREME96 for the cosmic-ray and solar environment and the rate integral, SRIM for ion stopping and range, Geant4 for detailed Monte Carlo transport, and MRED and MUSCA SEP3 for event-by-event prediction in device geometries. The frontier the deck names — wide-bandgap power devices, combined stressors, multiscale and machine-learning models, commercial space and automotive electronics — sits where those tools and those canons do not yet reach. The modules teach the physics each entry depends on; the glossary carries the shortest form of the most-cited entries.
