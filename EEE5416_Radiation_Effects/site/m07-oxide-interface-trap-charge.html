Module 07

Oxide-Trapped and Interface-Trap Charge

Where the charge behind total ionizing dose comes from: how many pairs a dose generates in an oxide, what fraction of the holes escapes prompt recombination, how those holes move to the silicon interface and what captures them there, how interface states are created by a second and slower process, and how the two densities are measured apart.

Prerequisites M04, M06 Read time ~50 min Sources Deck 05 pp. 19–56; Deck 03 pp. 18–19; Deck 04 pp. 22–25; Lectures 3, 5

M06 established what a charge inside an oxide does once it is there: it shifts the flat-band and threshold voltages by Qox/Cox, weighted by its distance from the gate, and states at the interface add a second contribution whose sign follows the channel type. Both statements begin with the charge already in place. This module supplies its origin.

The origin has four steps, and Deck 05 organises the whole lecture around them. Ionizing radiation generates electron–hole pairs throughout the oxide, at a rate per unit dose that is a material constant. Most of those pairs recombine within picoseconds of being made, and the fraction that does not — the charge yield — depends on the electric field in the oxide and on how densely the incident particle ionized. The surviving holes are then transported: electrons leave in picoseconds, but holes move by hopping between localized states and take from a tenth of a microsecond to of order a second to cross the same oxide, depending on temperature and field. When they arrive near the silicon interface, some are captured at deep traps and stay. Each step has its own dependence on field, thickness, temperature and time, and the device-level behaviour of the next module is the composition of all four.

Interface traps are a separate population with a separate history, and their creation is slower still: the states are not made by radiation striking the interface but by the arrival there of hydrogen released along the holes' path, which is why their buildup continues for thousands of seconds after the exposure ends and, in some oxides, resumes again a million seconds later. The module closes with the measurement that separates the two contributions, because a device only ever reports their sum.

1 · The four steps between dose and trapped charge

Deck 05 states the premise in one line: electron–hole pair generation in the oxide leads to almost all total dose effects. What happens between generation and a measurable threshold shift is a sequence of four processes, each of which removes most of what the previous one produced.

That asymmetry deserves stating precisely, because it is the physical fact the whole of Part III rests on. In silicon dioxide the electron mobility is of order 20 cm²/V·s, so an electron crosses a gate oxide in picoseconds and is collected at whichever electrode the field carries it to. The hole mobility is between roughly 10⁻⁵ and 10⁻⁷ cm²/V·s depending on temperature — six to eight orders of magnitude smaller — because a hole in SiO₂ is not a free carrier but a small polaron, localized on an oxygen site and moving only by hopping to a neighbour. The electrons leave, the holes stay long enough to be captured, and the oxide is left positive.

The generation constant itself was established in M04 §4: Neh = kgD, with kg = 8.1×10¹² pairs/cm³ per rad(SiO₂). It is fixed by the material alone: one rad deposits 100 erg per gram, so kg = (100 erg/g·rad)(ρ)/Ep, and with ρ = 2.2 g/cm³ and Ep = 17 eV per pair this returns 8.08×10¹², matching the tabulated value to better than half a per cent. Deck 05 writes this constant kg where Deck 03 writes g0; they are the same quantity.

2 · Charge yield

Immediately after an ionizing particle passes, the oxide holds a column of electrons and holes at the same positions. The electric field begins to separate them, but before separation is complete some electrons find holes and recombine. Deck 05 states both controlling variables: the fraction of holes that escapes depends on the oxide electric field and on the type of incident particle. That surviving fraction is the charge yield, fy, a dimensionless number between 0 and 1.

The field dependence follows directly from the competition. A stronger field pulls the two carrier populations apart faster, so fewer pairs recombine and the yield rises toward 1. The particle dependence follows from the geometry of the track. A sparsely ionizing particle leaves pairs far enough apart that each electron is unlikely to encounter a hole before the field removes it, and the yield is high; a densely ionizing particle leaves a column in which every carrier has neighbours of the opposite sign, and most recombine regardless of the field.

The ordering in Figure 2 is the ordering of ionization density along the track, which is the linear energy transfer of M04 §2. Co-60 gammas and 12 MeV electrons deposit sparsely and yield almost everything; 5 keV electrons are slower and deposit more densely; 700 keV protons more densely again; and a 2 MeV alpha, whose charge of +2e gives it four times a proton's energy loss per unit path at the same velocity, leaves so dense a column that most of its pairs recombine at any field the oxide can sustain.

This is why a heavy ion that easily causes a single-event upset contributes little to total dose per unit of energy deposited, and why total-dose qualification is done with photons. The same property of the track — high ionization density — makes it effective at the first and ineffective at the second.

Two test sources dominate practice, and Deck 05 devotes a slide to the fact that they do not agree. A cobalt-60 source emits gamma rays at 1.17 and 1.33 MeV, averaging 1.25 MeV, which interact in silicon principally by Compton scattering and produce energetic, sparsely ionizing secondary electrons. A 10 keV X-ray tube produces photons that interact by the photoelectric effect and release much slower electrons, which ionize densely. At the same field, the X-ray yield is therefore lower, and the gap narrows as the field rises: reading Deck 05 p. 33, the Co-60-to-X-ray yield ratio runs above 4 at 10⁵ V/cm, about 2.2 at 3×10⁵ V/cm, about 1.4 at 1 MV/cm and about 1.2 at 3 MV/cm. The ratio is not a constant, which is why a single conversion factor between the two sources does not exist.

The two sources also differ in a second way that acts in the opposite direction. A 10 keV photon's photoelectric cross-section is a strong function of atomic number, so high-Z gate and interconnect layers emit an excess of secondary electrons into the oxide — the dose enhancement of M04 §5, which is negligible for Co-60. The net ratio of X-ray to Co-60 response is consequently field-dependent: Deck 05 plots it for a 105 nm oxide rising from about 0.75 at low field to about 1.1 at several MV/cm, for both the oxide-trap and the interface-trap components, and reproduces the measurement with a dose enhancement factor of 1.4. A result obtained with one source converts to the other only through a correction that depends on the bias and on the layer stack.

3 · The net oxide-trap charge

The four steps of §1 compose into a single expression, and Deck 05 writes it as the definition of the hole trapping efficiency, fot — the fraction of the pairs generated that ends as trapped charge.

fot = ΔNottox kg fy fDE D ⟺ ΔNot = fot fy fDE kg D tox
(7.1)
ΔNot areal density of trapped holes (cm⁻²) · fot hole trapping efficiency, dimensionless — the quantity this equation defines and that a measurement returns · tox oxide thickness (cm) · kg pair generation constant, 8.1×10¹² pairs/(cm³·rad(SiO₂)), written g0 on Deck 03 · fy charge yield from §2, dimensionless, 0 to 1 · fDE dose enhancement factor, dimensionless, ≥1 for a low-energy X-ray source beside high-Z layers and ≈1 for Co-60 · D dose in rad(SiO₂). The professor's worked values for a 10 keV X-ray irradiation are fy = 0.6 and fDE = 1.55. On the formula sheet as (7.1).

Read left to right, the denominator is the chain of §1 in order: the oxide volume per unit area, the pairs made per unit volume per rad, the fraction of holes surviving recombination, the correction for the dose the oxide actually received rather than the dose stated, and the dose itself. The numerator is what survives all of it. Setting fot = 1 — every surviving hole trapped — gives an upper bound on the trapped charge, which is the form Deck 03 p. 19 states.

Combining (7.1) with the electrostatics of M06 §3 gives the voltage shift, which is what a device reports.

ΔVot = −q ΔNotCox = −q fot fy fDE kg D tox2εox
(7.2)
ΔVot threshold or flat-band shift from oxide-trapped charge (V), negative because the trapped charge is positive · q = 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ C · Cox = εox/tox from M06 (6.1) (F/cm²) · εox = 3.45×10⁻¹³ F/cm · every other symbol as in (7.1). Assumes the trapped charge sits at the silicon interface, so the centroid weighting of (6.2) is unity; a distributed charge gives less. The second form makes the tox² dependence explicit — one factor from the oxide volume that generated the pairs, one from the capacitance that converts them to a voltage. On the formula sheet as (7.2).

A conflict on the slides, worth knowing about. Deck 05 p. 33 carries an annotation reading fy ∼ E−0.55, placed beside a plot in which the fraction of unrecombined holes plainly rises with oxide field. A yield falling as E−0.55 would contradict the figure it sits on, and it would contradict p. 32. The −0.55 is the exponent of the capture process, which is what p. 34 plots and what (7.3) states; the label attaches it to the wrong quantity. Follow p. 34.

Every length in (7.1) and (7.2) is in centimetres, because kg is per cm³ and εox is per cm. A 45 nm oxide is 4.5×10⁻⁶ cm. Substituting nanometres directly changes the answer by fourteen orders of magnitude, and the result still looks like a voltage.

The squared thickness dependence is the single most consequential scaling result in total ionizing dose. Deck 04 states its consequence directly: MOSFETs with thin gate dielectrics are relatively insensitive to ionizing radiation, because the trapped hole density is roughly proportional to tox while Cox is inversely proportional to it. Halving the oxide thickness reduces the threshold shift by a factor of four at the same dose. Gate oxides that were 100 nm in the 1970s are now a few nanometres, and gate-oxide threshold shift has correspondingly stopped being the limiting mechanism in advanced CMOS — which is why M08 finds the surviving failure mode in the isolation oxides, which did not scale.

Below about 5 nm a second effect removes the remaining charge. Deck 04 records that in very thin oxides there is almost no net hole trapping, because electrons tunnel from the silicon into trap sites close enough to the interface and neutralize them as fast as they fill. The same slide adds the qualification that net positive oxide-trap charge can still appear in such oxides through hydrogen ions, so the mechanism is suppressed rather than abolished. Alternative gate dielectrics do not inherit the benefit: Deck 04 shows that high-permittivity replacements for SiO₂ contain more defects and trap more charge than thermal oxide of the same equivalent thickness.

4 · The field dependence, and why it is not the yield's

Charge yield rises with oxide field, so a naive reading of (7.2) predicts that a device biased harder during irradiation suffers a larger shift. Measurement shows the opposite above about 1 MV/cm, and resolving that is worth doing carefully because it separates two of the four steps.

Deck 05 plots the measured shift of a 45 nm poly-gate oxide irradiated to 500 krad(SiO₂) with 10 keV X-rays, alongside a second curve it labels adjusted. The adjusted curve falls monotonically as a clean power law while the measured one does not. Reading the two together, the adjustment removes step 2 and leaves steps 3 and 4 — transport and trapping — which is the reading this page takes; the slide labels the curve without saying what the adjustment was.

ΔVot ∝ Eox−1/2 (adjusted for charge yield, Eox ≳ 1 MV/cm)
(7.3)
Eox electric field in the oxide (MV/cm). Deck 05 p. 34 states the exponent as −1/2 and marks its fitted line −1/2; the companion interface-trap measurement on p. 54 gives −0.6 for the same quantity. The dependence is set primarily by the hole capture cross-section near the Si/SiO₂ interface, which falls as the field rises because a hole drifting faster past a trap is less likely to be captured by it. Applies to the yield-corrected shift; the measured shift is the product of this with fy(Eox), which rises.

The same experiment measures the interface-trap contribution on the same devices, and Deck 05 reports that its yield-adjusted field dependence is also close to Eox−1/2, with the fitted line marked −0.6 — and, as in §4, the measured curve on the same axes passes through a maximum rather than falling monotonically. Within experimental uncertainty the two exponents are equal, and equal to the field dependence of the hole capture cross-section near the interface. Deck 05 draws the inference explicitly: interface-trap buildup is linked to hole trapping. That is the evidence behind the two-stage model of §7 — the interface states are not made by radiation acting at the interface, but by the holes arriving there.

5 · Hole transport

Between escaping recombination and being trapped, a hole has to cross the oxide, and how long that takes governs how a device behaves in the seconds and hours after an exposure. Deck 05 states the result at the top: hole transport is much slower than electron transport in SiO₂.

The reason is that a hole in amorphous silicon dioxide is a small polaron. The positive charge localizes on an oxygen site and distorts the surrounding lattice, and that distortion is itself part of what binds it there. Moving requires the distortion to relax at one site and form at the next, so transport is a sequence of thermally assisted hops between nearest-neighbour oxygens rather than band conduction. The measured intrinsic hole mobility is of order 10⁻⁹ to 10⁻¹¹ m²/V·s and rises steeply with temperature, exactly as a thermally activated hopping rate should.

Because the hopping sites are randomly distributed in energy and position, hop times vary over many orders of magnitude, and the resulting motion is dispersive: a packet of holes released together does not arrive together, and there is no well-defined transit time in the ordinary sense. The standard description is a continuous-time random walk, in which each step's waiting time is drawn from a broad distribution rather than being fixed. Deck 05 gives the transit time across a 370 nm film at 4.7 MV/cm ranging from about 10⁻⁷ s at high temperature to of order 1 s at low temperature.

Two experimental signatures follow, and Deck 05 shows both on 95 nm wet-process oxides. The recovery of the flat-band voltage after a pulse of radiation — which tracks the number of holes still in the oxide — occurs over many decades in time rather than with a single time constant, which is the direct signature of dispersive transport. And the shift persists longer at low temperature: cooling slows every hop, so at long times a cold device still holds holes that a warm one has already cleared to the interface.

The thickness dependence is the most striking quantitative result. Deck 05 reports that the recovery time scales as tox⁴, which it identifies as characteristic of dispersive hopping transport. Boesch measured it at constant field, and at constant field ordinary drift would give a transit time proportional to tox/Eox — that is, linear in tox. A fourth power against a first is the signature that the transport is not ordinary drift; Oldham and McLean call the dependence superlinear for exactly this reason.

6 · What traps the holes

A hole that reaches the region near the silicon interface may be captured at a deep trap and held there. Deck 05 asks what those traps physically are, and answers with defect spectroscopy rather than inference.

The tool is electron paramagnetic resonance, also called electron spin resonance. An unpaired electron has a magnetic moment, and in an applied magnetic field its two spin orientations differ in energy by an amount proportional to the field. Sweeping the field until that splitting matches the energy of an applied microwave photon produces resonant absorption, and the field at which resonance occurs — expressed as the g-tensor — depends on the local environment of the unpaired electron. Different defects therefore resonate at different fields, so the technique identifies a defect's atomic structure rather than merely counting states. Deck 05 records the consequence: identification of the E′ and Pb centres led to models of oxide and interface charge, and to methods of reducing them.

The defect responsible for oxide-trapped charge is the E′ centre. Deck 05 gives its structure: an unpaired electron highly localized on a silicon atom bonded to three oxygen atoms. Its precursor is an oxygen vacancy — a site where two silicon atoms have bonded directly to each other because the bridging oxygen is missing. A hole arriving at that site breaks the weak Si–Si bond, leaving one silicon with an unpaired electron and the other positively charged. The defect is therefore both positively charged and paramagnetic, which is why it is simultaneously an electrostatic problem and an EPR signal. At least nine variants have been identified in thermally grown and bulk oxides, distinguished by how the two silicon atoms relax: Deck 05 names the dimer form and two puckered forms, one of which produces a dipole and one of which does not.

The correlation between the two observables is good but not universal, and Deck 05 is careful about it: for some oxides the number of E′ centres has been correlated with the amount of oxide-trap charge, while for other thermal oxides and for the buried oxides of SOI transistors a weaker correlation has been found. Oxygen vacancies remain important in every case. A weaker correlation means other defects also carry charge, not that the mechanism is wrong.

The energies of the trapped holes are measured by a different technique. Thermally stimulated current heats an irradiated capacitor at a controlled rate and records the current as traps release their carriers; a trap of a given depth empties at a characteristic temperature, so the current against temperature maps the energy distribution of the trapped charge. Deck 05 shows it for a set of oxides of differing quality, a 350 nm soft oxide among them.

7 · Neutralization, and traps that take electrons

Trapped holes do not stay indefinitely. Deck 05 gives two independent routes by which oxide-trap charge is neutralized, and they depend on different properties of the trap population.

  • Electron tunneling from the silicon into the trap. Tunneling probability falls exponentially with distance, so the rate of neutralization by this route depends on the spatial distribution of the traps — how close to the interface they sit.
  • Thermal emission of the trapped hole back into the oxide valence band. The rate depends on how deep the trap is, so this route depends on the energy distribution of the traps.

Both distributions are set during fabrication, which is why two oxides carrying the same trapped charge immediately after irradiation can recover at completely different rates. Annealing is therefore a property of the process, not only of the dose — the point M09 develops into rad-hard-by-process design.

A third mechanism removes charge without removing the trapped hole. Deck 05 records that thermal oxides also contain electron traps, and that for some gate oxides their number can exceed half the number of hole traps. An electron captured near a trapped hole compensates its charge electrostatically, so the net oxide charge falls while both carriers remain trapped. This compensation can occur during irradiation as well as after it, which means the measured ΔVot is a net quantity from the start and understates the number of holes actually trapped.

8 · Interface traps: a two-stage process

M06 §5 described what interface traps do: dangling silicon bonds at the Si/SiO₂ boundary, donor-like below midgap and acceptor-like above, neutral at midgap, negatively charged at an n-channel threshold. The unsatisfied silicon bond at the interface is the Pb centre, identified by the same EPR technique as the E′ centre and, like it, both paramagnetic and electrically active.

Radiation creates them, and the mechanism is indirect. Deck 05 makes the argument with a comparison of sources rather than of energies. It plots the interface-state distribution produced by two irradiations of the same oxide: 10 eV ultraviolet light, which is absorbed within the oxide and cannot deposit energy at the interface in any concentrated way, and 1 MeV Co-60 gammas, five orders of magnitude more energetic per quantum. The two produce the same distribution of states. A mechanism driven by the energy of the incident quantum could not do that, so the mechanism must be the one the two irradiations share — the generation and transport of charge in the oxide bulk. Deck 05 states the conclusion as its heading: interface traps result from a two-stage process, not direct interaction of radiation at the interface.

Deck 05 calls the accepted account the two-stage hydrogen model and decomposes the second stage into three steps. Holes transport through the bulk of the oxide. A hydrogen ion is released near either the Si/SiO₂ or the gate/SiO₂ interface — not along the whole path. That proton then drifts to the Si/SiO₂ interface and reacts there. Hydrogen is present in every thermal oxide, both as a deliberate passivant for interface bonds and as a processing residue, and the deck writes the reaction out:

H+ + H–Si≡Si3 → H2 + (+)Si≡Si3

The notation is the deck's. H–Si≡Si3 is a silicon atom bonded to one hydrogen and back-bonded to three silicons — a passivated interface bond. The arriving proton takes that hydrogen, leaves as molecular hydrogen, and leaves behind a silicon with a dangling bond, written •Si≡Si3. That defect is the Pb centre, and the reaction as written leaves it positively charged; capturing two electrons from the silicon returns it to the neutral, amphoteric state whose occupancy §5 of M06 describes. On (100) silicon the centres appear in two crystallographic variants, Pb0 and Pb1. The interface trap is created by the arrival of the proton, not by the radiation.

Four observations follow, and Deck 05 presents each as a separate measurement.

  • Buildup is slow. It takes thousands of seconds to saturate, because the proton has to travel. Oxide-trapped charge is at its maximum immediately after irradiation and decays; interface-trapped charge is at its minimum and grows. The two components therefore evolve in opposite directions over the same interval, which is why a measurement made an hour after an exposure differs from one made a minute after.
  • Buildup is field-dependent, following the Eox−1/2 law of §4 — the same exponent, within experimental uncertainty, as the hole capture cross-section.
  • Buildup is temperature-dependent, and less at low temperatures, because both the hole transport that releases the proton and the proton's own drift are thermally activated.
  • Buildup can resume much later. Deck 05 gives this as latent interface-trap buildup: a second rise occurring around 10⁶ s after irradiation, which can increase the number of interface traps by as much as four times. It is seen in oxides with high vacancy densities, is initiated by molecular hydrogen transport and reaction in or near the gate oxide, and is strongly thermally activated with an activation energy of about 0.41 eV. Deck 05 names it a possible concern for space systems, where a part must hold its parameters for years rather than for the duration of a test.

A test that ends when the beam does measures neither component at its final value. Oxide-trapped charge has not yet annealed, interface traps have not yet finished building, and in some oxides a second population will appear eleven days later. This is the reason hardness assurance specifies not only a dose but a post-irradiation measurement schedule and an anneal, and it is why Deck 05 presents time as a variable on the same footing as field and temperature.

9 · Separating the two densities

A transistor reports one number. ΔVth is the sum of a negative contribution from trapped holes and a contribution from interface traps whose sign depends on channel type, and since the two anneal differently, extrapolating a measured shift to mission end requires them separated. The standard technique exploits the midgap property of M06 §5.

At the gate voltage that places the surface Fermi level at midgap, the net interface-trap charge is zero whatever the interface-trap density. Any shift of that particular point on the characteristic is therefore due to oxide-trapped charge alone. The remaining shift, measured between midgap and threshold, is what interface traps contribute.

ΔVot = ΔVmg , ΔVit = ΔVth − ΔVmg
(7.4)
ΔVmg shift of the gate voltage at which the surface potential equals the bulk potential, Ψs = ΦB — read from a subthreshold I–V sweep at the drain current corresponding to midgap, of order 10⁻¹³ A (V) · ΔVth shift of the threshold voltage (V) · ΔVot, ΔVit the two components (V). For a capacitor measured by C–V rather than a transistor by I–V, the second reference point is inversion or flat band rather than threshold, and Deck 05 gives both: ΔVit taken from midgap to inversion is ΔVinv − ΔVmg, and from midgap to flat band it is ΔVfb − ΔVmg. These are not interchangeable — inversion lies above midgap and flat band below it, so the two count opposite halves of the gap and carry opposite signs. Always say which interval a quoted ΔVit spans. Deck 05 gives typical error bars of ±3% on ΔVth and ±5–10% on the separated ΔVot and ΔVit, for approximately straight subthreshold curves. On the formula sheet as (7.4).
ΔNit = Cox ΔVitq
(7.5)
ΔNit areal density of radiation-induced interface traps charged between midgap and threshold (cm⁻²) · Cox oxide capacitance per unit area (F/cm²) · ΔVit from (7.4) (V) · q = 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ C. The same relation applied to ΔVot returns ΔNot, which is the route from a measured shift back to the trap density that (7.1) predicts. Counts only the states charged over that part of the gap, not the whole distribution. On the formula sheet as (7.5).

A second, independent measurement of interface-trap density uses charge pumping, which counts recombination events instead of inferring a voltage shift. The source and drain junctions are held slightly reverse-biased while the gate is pulsed between inversion and accumulation. During the inversion half-cycle minority carriers flow in from source and drain and are captured by interface traps; during the accumulation half cycle majority carriers arrive and recombine with them. Each cycle therefore passes a net charge into the substrate, and the substrate current measures it.

ICP = q AG f Dit ΔΨs
(7.6)
ICP charge-pumping current measured at the substrate (A) · q = 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ C · AG gate area (cm²) · f gate pulse frequency (Hz) · Dit interface-trap density (cm⁻²eV⁻¹) · ΔΨs the range of surface potential swept by the pulse (eV, or V — the two are numerically equal for singly charged states). The base voltage is stepped from below flat band to above threshold at constant pulse amplitude, and the current rises to a plateau once the pulse spans the full range. On the formula sheet as (7.6).

Charge pumping measures low interface-trap densities accurately, and Deck 05 gives it a second use: it distinguishes interface traps from border traps. These are near-interface oxide traps that sit close enough to the silicon to exchange charge with it on the timescale of an I–V measurement. Electrostatically they behave like oxide traps, because they are in the oxide; kinetically they behave like interface traps, because they respond to bias. They occupy the boundary the two-population picture of this module draws, and a technique that can separate them is the reason the boundary can be drawn at all.

A 45 nm gate oxide is irradiated to 500 krad(SiO₂) with 10 keV X-rays, with the professor's values fy = 0.6 and fDE = 1.55. Taking a hole trapping efficiency of fot = 0.02, what threshold shift does (7.2) predict, and what would change if the same dose were delivered by Co-60?

Work in centimetres. tox = 4.5×10⁻⁶ cm, so Cox = 3.45×10⁻¹³ / 4.5×10⁻⁶ = 7.67×10⁻⁸ F/cm². The trapped density from (7.1) is ΔNot = 0.02 × 0.6 × 1.55 × 8.1×10¹² × 5×10⁵ × 4.5×10⁻⁶, which is 3.39×10¹¹ cm⁻². Then ΔVot = −(1.602×10⁻¹⁹ × 3.39×10¹¹)/7.67×10⁻⁸ = −0.71 V. The sign is negative because the trapped charge is positive, and the magnitude is the right order for a 45 nm oxide at this dose.

Both answers assume an oxide field near 1 MV/cm, which is where the yield figures below are read. With Co-60 two factors change and they act in opposite directions. The charge yield rises, because Co-60 secondaries ionize far more sparsely than 10 keV photoelectrons — Figure 2 puts fy near 0.9 at 1 MV/cm rather than 0.6. And the dose enhancement factor falls to about 1, because dose enhancement is a low-energy photoelectric effect and 1.25 MeV gammas interact by Compton scattering, which is almost independent of atomic number. The product 0.6 × 1.55 = 0.93 becomes roughly 0.9 × 1 = 0.9, so the two sources happen to give a similar answer here — but only because the two corrections nearly cancel at this field. At low field they do not, which is why Deck 05 plots the X-ray-to-Co-60 ratio against field rather than quoting a single number.

Where this leaves us

A dose delivered to an oxide generates 8.1×10¹² electron–hole pairs per cm³ per rad. A field-dependent and particle-dependent fraction of the holes escapes prompt recombination — near 0.9 for Co-60 gammas at 1 MV/cm, under 0.1 for alpha particles. Those holes cross the oxide by polaron hopping, dispersively, over microseconds to hours, with a recovery time scaling as tox⁴. A further small fraction is captured at oxygen-vacancy sites near the silicon interface, forming E′ centres that are positively charged and detectable by electron paramagnetic resonance. The resulting threshold shift is negative and scales as tox², which is why thinning gate oxides hardened them. Along the way the transporting holes release protons near an interface, and those drift to the Si/SiO₂ interface and depassivate silicon bonds there, creating Pb centres over thousands of seconds and, in some oxides, again at 10⁶ s.

The two populations behave oppositely in almost every respect. Oxide-trapped charge is positive, is maximum immediately after irradiation, and anneals by tunneling and thermal emission. Interface-trapped charge is negative at an n-channel threshold, is minimum immediately after irradiation, and grows. Their voltage shifts are separated by the midgap technique from a single subthreshold sweep, and their densities cross-checked by charge pumping.

M08 takes those two densities into working devices and integrated circuits: what the competing shifts do to an n-channel and a p-channel transistor as dose accumulates, why the surviving failure mechanism in scaled CMOS is leakage through isolation oxides rather than threshold shift, how the same interface states destroy bipolar gain, and why a bipolar part is often worse after a slow irradiation than a fast one.

Quick reference

  • Four steps: generation (kg = 8.1×10¹² pairs/cm³·rad(SiO₂), = g0 on Deck 03) → prompt recombination (yield fy) → hole transport → trapping (efficiency fot).
  • Why oxides charge positive: electron mobility in SiO₂ ≈ 20 cm²/V·s, hole mobility ≈ 10⁻⁵ to 10⁻⁷ cm²/V·s — six to eight orders of magnitude apart. Electrons leave in picoseconds; holes are small polarons and hop.
  • Charge yield rises with oxide field and falls with the ionization density of the particle. At 1 MV/cm: ≈0.9 for Co-60 and 12 MeV electrons, ≈0.55 for 5 keV electrons, ≈0.13 for 700 keV protons, ≈0.07 for 2 MeV alphas — a span of order thirteen.
  • Master equation: fot = ΔNot/(toxkgfyfDED), so ΔVot = −q fotfyfDEkgD tox²/εox. Professor's 10 keV X-ray values: fy = 0.6, fDE = 1.55. All lengths in cm.
  • tox² scaling — one power from generation volume, one from Cox. Halving the oxide quarters the shift. Below ~5 nm, tunneling from the silicon neutralizes traps and there is almost no net hole trapping; high-κ dielectrics trap more than thermal SiO₂.
  • Field dependence: yield-adjusted ΔVot ∝ Eox−1/2 above ~1 MV/cm, set by the hole capture cross-section near the interface. The measured shift peaks near 1–2 MV/cm because rising yield fights falling capture.
  • Transport: dispersive polaron hopping, described by a continuous-time random walk. Transit across a 370 nm film at 4.7 MV/cm runs from ~10⁻⁷ s hot to ~1 s cold; recovery of a device spans many decades in time, persists longer when cold, and scales as tox⁴.
  • The trap is the E′ centre — an unpaired electron on a silicon bonded to three oxygens, formed when a hole breaks the Si–Si bond of an oxygen vacancy. At least nine variants. Identified by EPR through its g-tensor; correlation with ΔNot is strong in some oxides and weaker in others, including SOI buried oxides. Energy distribution measured by thermally stimulated current.
  • Neutralization by electron tunneling (rate set by the spatial trap distribution) and thermal emission (set by the energy distribution). Electron traps can exceed half the hole-trap number in some thermal oxides and compensate charge without removing it.
  • Interface traps are two-stage: pair generation, then hole transport, H⁺ release near an interface, drift to the Si/SiO₂ interface and reaction there. Not direct interaction: 10 eV UV and 1 MeV Co-60 produce the same interface-state distribution, so the quantum energy cannot be what drives it.
  • Interface-trap kinetics: saturates over ~10³ s; the yield-adjusted buildup follows Eox−1/2, matching the hole capture cross-section, which is the evidence linking it to hole trapping; less at low temperature; latent buildup at ~10⁶ s up to 4×, in high-vacancy oxides, EA ≈ 0.41 eV.
  • Opposite time behaviour: trapped charge is at its maximum immediately after irradiation and then anneals, so |ΔVot| falls; interface-trap charge is at its minimum immediately after and then grows.
  • Midgap separation: ΔVot = ΔVmg, ΔVit = ΔVth − ΔVmg, ΔNit = CoxΔVit/q. Errors ±3% on ΔVth, ±5–10% on the components.
  • Charge pumping: ICP = qAGfDitΔΨs. Measures low Dit accurately and separates interface traps from border traps — near-interface oxide traps that exchange charge with the silicon on I–V timescales.
  • X-ray against Co-60: 10 keV X-rays give lower yield but larger dose enhancement. The response ratio is field-dependent — Deck 05 plots ≈0.75 at low field rising above 1 at several MV/cm for a 105 nm oxide, modelled with a dose enhancement factor of 1.4.
