Module 10

Gate Oxide Growth, Processing and Hardening by Process

Where the gate oxide comes from and what its making leaves inside it: the properties of amorphous SiO₂, thermal growth and the Deal–Grove law, the thinning that led to high-κ stacks, the defects and the trap vocabulary built around them, high-field stress as the electrical twin of irradiation, the separation of total from net trapped charge, and the four process levers by which an oxide is made radiation hard.

Prerequisites M07, M08, M09 Read time ~50 min Sources Deck 07; Deck 04 pp. 24–25; Deck 06 p. 48; Deck 02 pp. 8–9; Lecture 7

Three modules have now treated the gate oxide as given. M07 followed a dose into it and out again as trapped holes and interface traps; M08 turned those two densities into threshold shifts, leakage and lost drive; and M09 set them on a time axis and showed how field, bias and temperature move them. In every one of those calculations the number of hole traps, the number of interface bonds waiting to be depassivated and the amount of hydrogen available to do the depassivating were inputs. This module is about where those inputs come from. They are fixed when the oxide is grown and annealed, before the part ever sees radiation, and that is the whole premise of radiation hardening by process.

One fact organizes the material. The defects that radiation charges are processing defects: oxygen vacancies left by incomplete oxidation and by anneals, silicon dangling bonds at the interface that hydrogen has tied off, and the hydrogen itself. High electric field applied for long enough creates and charges the same defects, and so does a long service life at operating field. The radiation response of an oxide and its electrical reliability are therefore two readings of one defect population, which is why the measurement techniques, the vocabulary and much of the literature are shared between the two fields.

Sections 1 to 3 describe the oxide as a component and how it is grown, ending with the growth law that predicts its thickness. Section 4 follows the thickness down to the tunnelling limit and the high-permittivity replacements that came after it. Sections 5 and 6 describe the structure, where its defects come from and how they are classified by the speed at which they talk to the silicon. Section 7 shows that high-field stress builds the same defects radiation builds, section 8 how to count them when trapped charges of both signs coexist, section 9 what a process engineer can change, and section 10 where gate dielectrics are going.

1 · The gate oxide as a component

The gate dielectric of a MOS transistor has one electrical job, to couple the gate to the channel capacitively without passing current, and several material jobs that make the first possible. Deck 07 states them by contrasting an ideal gate oxide with a real one. The ideal oxide is a perfect insulator with zero gate leakage; it passivates the silicon surface perfectly, so it carries no interface-trap charge; it contains no mobile or trapped charge, and in particular no sodium or potassium; and it blocks dopant diffusion from the gate while containing no chemically or electrically active impurities of its own.

The real oxide falls short on each count, by amounts that are small but not zero. Its gate leakage increases with electric field and with decreasing thickness. It passivates the interface adequately, leaving an interface-trap density Deck 07 calls manageable, below about 10¹⁰ cm⁻². It contains few mobile or trapped charges, again below 10¹⁰ cm⁻². And it shields the silicon from the processing of the gate and the layers deposited above it — a role the ideal list does not name but that every later process step depends on.

Those targets are small in the units that matter. An areal charge qN in an oxide of capacitance Cox per unit area moves the threshold by qN/Cox, by the same argument as M07 §3. At N = 10¹⁰ cm⁻² in a 5 nm oxide, with Cox = 6.9×10⁻⁷ F/cm² from (6.1), that is 2.3 mV. How that compares with the charge a dose adds depends on thickness, because the trapped density grows with the oxide thickness by (7.1). The trapped-hole densities of M07's worked examples, for oxides tens of nanometres thick, run to 10¹¹–10¹² cm⁻², one to two orders of magnitude above the as-processed level. In a 5 nm oxide, the same (7.1) with fot = 0.02 and a Co-60 yield of about 0.9 gives about 7×10⁹ cm⁻² at 100 krad(SiO₂), comparable to the as-processed level, and that before any neutralization by tunnelling (§4). The as-processed charge is present before irradiation and forms part of the pre-irradiation baseline against which a shift is measured. What processing determines for the radiation response is how many empty traps are waiting.

Why a commercial process cares about radiation

Deck 07 p. 31 gives four reasons why manufacturers who never sell into space still study the radiation response of their oxides. Some are paid to, for space and the growing commercial space market. The process itself is increasingly radiative: plasma etching and deposition and deep-ultraviolet, electron-beam and X-ray lithography all ionize the gate oxide during manufacture. The defects radiation creates are the ones high-field stress and long-term operation create, so radiation is a fast probe of reliability. And, historically, oxide materials problems have usually surfaced in radiation studies before reliability studies found them. Oldham records the same point from the process side: the most severe radiation environment most parts ever see is the process line itself.

The gate oxide is a component with a specification, and every item on that specification is a defect density set by processing. Radiation hardness is one more line on the same list, controlled by the same process variables that control leakage and reliability.

2 · Growing the oxide

Thermal oxidation does not deposit silicon dioxide on silicon; it converts silicon into it. The oxidant diffuses inward through whatever oxide already exists and reacts with the silicon at the buried Si/SiO₂ interface, so the interface on which every device depends is formed fresh, below the original surface, at every instant of growth. That is why a thermally grown oxide has the best interface of any dielectric on silicon, and it is why the conditions of growth and of the anneals that follow it — chemistry, temperature, ambient, the cooling ramp — are where an oxide's defect population is decided.

Deck 07 p. 12 separates the choices into method and chemistry. The method is either a furnace, in which batches of wafers sit in a heated quartz tube for minutes to hours, or rapid thermal processing (RTP), in which a single wafer is heated by lamps for seconds, giving the small thermal budget that very thin oxides need (EEE6338 M06 §8.2–8.3). The chemistry is either pure — dry O₂ or wet H₂O — or deliberately modified: lightly nitrided in N₂O or NO, with NH₃ possible but not preferred; fluorinated; deposited and then annealed; or, for the high-permittivity films of §4, grown by atomic layer deposition (ALD), which builds the film from alternating self-limiting surface reactions a fraction of a nanometre at a time.

The two pure chemistries are

dry: Si + O₂ → SiO₂      wet: Si + 2H₂O → SiO₂ + 2H₂

and Deck 07 marks the wet reaction as oversimplified. Both run at temperatures above about 750 °C. The oxidant is the molecule, O₂ or H₂O, which dissolves in the oxide, diffuses across it and reacts at the silicon. Wet oxidation is ten to twenty times faster at the same temperature, and the reason is solubility rather than diffusivity: at 1100 °C silicon dioxide holds about 3×10¹⁹ cm⁻³ of water against about 5×10¹⁶ cm⁻³ of O₂, so the concentration gradient that drives oxidant across the film is far steeper for water (Plummer, Deal and Griffin; EEE6338 M01 §7.1). Water enters the network by reacting with it to form Si–OH groups, so a wet oxide also carries hydroxyl — hydrogen that becomes significant in §5 and §9. Dry oxidation is the slower, cleaner process and the one used for gate oxides; sequences that combine dry and wet steps in one film are possible.

Because the oxide occupies more volume than the silicon it replaces — about 2.2 times as much — a known fraction of every oxide lies below the original surface.

The modified chemistries matter for radiation because each changes the region next to the interface. Nitridation in N₂O or NO places a small amount of nitrogen at the Si/SiO₂ interface, which improves resistance to boron penetration, breakdown and hot-carrier damage without altering the constants of §1. Nitridation in ammonia achieves the same nitrogen but brings hydrogen with it — NH₃ is a hydrogen carrier — and the re-oxidation used to drive that hydrogen back out is hard to control; excess hydrogen leaves electron traps behind, which is why Deck 07 marks NH₃ as not preferred (EEE6338 M06 §9). A deposited oxide, grown by chemical vapour deposition rather than by consuming silicon, has no freshly formed interface and a poorer network; it must be annealed, and even then it traps more charge than thermal oxide. A shallow trench is filled this way, over a thin liner oxide grown on its walls to give the sidewall a thermal interface (EEE6338 M05 §1). The poorer fill is one of three reasons trench-isolation oxides dominate the total-dose failures of M08 §5, with the low field across them and, dominant, their much greater thickness, which enters squared.

The last lines of Deck 07 p. 12 carry the radiation-relevant point: the ramp-down at the end of oxidation is critical, and the temperature and ambient of the post-oxidation anneal, and any radiative processing, are important to oxide quality. The reason is structural. Silicon at the interface cannot be fully oxidized because of the lattice mismatch, so a non-stoichiometric transition layer rich in excess silicon forms near the interface and moves with it as growth proceeds (Oldham, after Deal and co-workers). Lower oxidation temperatures leave more excess silicon there; high-temperature annealing reduces it; and an anneal in an inert ambient can also drive oxygen out of the film. Excess silicon and missing oxygen are the precursors of the hole traps of M07 §6, so the thermal steps after growth set the trap density, a connection §5 makes precise and §9 turns into a hardening lever.

3 · The Deal–Grove law

An oxidation recipe is a temperature, an ambient and a time, and the thickness that results is predicted by the linear–parabolic model of Deal and Grove (1965). It is the standard description of thermal oxidation for films thicker than about 20 nm, and it matters here for two reasons: the oxide thickness is the variable to which the total-dose response is most sensitive, through the tox² dependence of M07 §3, and the model's own failure at small thickness is a statement about the defective, fast-growing layer next to the interface.

The model treats growth as three fluxes in series in the steady state. Oxidant crosses from the gas to the oxide surface, a step fast enough to neglect. It then diffuses across the oxide, with flux D(C0 − Ci)/x by Fick's law, where D is its diffusivity in SiO₂, C0 and Ci its concentrations just inside the outer surface and at the silicon, and x the oxide thickness. Finally it reacts at the interface with flux ksCi, where ks is the interface reaction rate constant. In the steady state the fluxes are equal, and each oxidant molecule that reacts adds a fixed volume of oxide, so the growth rate is the flux divided by N1, the number of oxidant molecules incorporated per unit volume of oxide — 2.2×10²² cm⁻³ for O₂ and twice that for H₂O. Integrating from an initial thickness gives the growth law.

The two constants have separate physical origins, and their temperature dependences show it. Both follow Arrhenius laws. The linear rate constant has an activation energy close to 2 eV in every ambient and depends on the orientation of the silicon surface, which marks it as a property of the reaction at the silicon; the parabolic constant has an activation energy of 1.23 eV in dry O₂ and 0.71–0.78 eV in wet ambients, matching independently measured diffusivities of O₂ and H₂O in silica, and does not depend on orientation (Plummer, Deal and Griffin, Table 6-2). Deck 07 gives the constants for dry O₂ at 1000 °C: B/A = 0.071 µm/h and B = 0.0117 µm²/h. They are the values in Table II of Deal and Grove's paper, measured at one atmosphere of oxygen on (111)-oriented silicon, where the same row gives A = 0.165 µm and τ = 0.37 h. The characteristic time is then A²/4B = 0.58 h, about 35 minutes, and the characteristic thickness A is 165 nm. On (100) silicon, the orientation of modern wafers, the linear rate constant is lower by a factor of about 1.7 (Plummer, Deal and Griffin divide its prefactor by 1.68), while B is unchanged.

The offset τ has two roles, and the second is the one that matters for gate oxides. Its first role is the one Deck 07 names: a correction for oxide already present at the start, such as a native oxide or a film left by an earlier step. Its second is empirical. Deal and Grove found that their dry-oxygen data did not extrapolate to zero thickness at zero time but to 230 ± 30 Å at every temperature from 700 to 1200 °C, while their wet data did extrapolate to zero. Something grows the first twenty-odd nanometres of a dry oxide faster than the linear law allows, and the model absorbs it by treating that layer as an initial thickness: at 1000 °C, an initial 23 nm corresponds through τ = (xi² + Axi)/B to exactly the tabulated 0.37 h. This is the anomalous thin-oxide regime, and every gate oxide of the last three decades lies inside it. The law is therefore not predictive for the films this course is most concerned with; several physical models for the fast initial growth exist, and EEE6338 M06 §8.1 surveys them.

Two examples on Deck 07 p. 13 sit in the wrong regime. The slide attaches an example to each law. Under the linear law it gives 125 Å of dry oxide in 35 minutes at 900 °C; under the parabolic law, 320 Å of wet oxide in 15 minutes at 850 °C. Evaluating the constants at those temperatures with the Arrhenius parameters of Plummer, Deal and Griffin shows that the second example belongs to the linear law, not the parabolic one. In steam at 850 °C, A is about 1.2 µm on (111) silicon, so a 32 nm film has x/A ≈ 0.03 and is deep in the reaction-limited regime; the linear law gives about 26 nm there, the same order as the slide's value, while the parabolic law alone would give about 175 nm. The first example is linear-regime as the slide says, but at 900 °C the linear law gives only about 9 nm on (111) silicon; the slide's 12.5 nm is larger because a 12 nm dry oxide lies in the anomalous regime just described. Use the linear law for thin films and short times — as the slide's headings say — and read the parabolic example as an illustration of wet oxidation's speed rather than of the parabolic limit.

A bare (111) silicon wafer is oxidized in dry O₂ at 1100 °C for 20 minutes. Deal and Grove's constants at that temperature are B/A = 0.30 µm/h and B = 0.027 µm²/h. What does the linear law predict, can it be trusted here, and what does the full law give?

The linear law gives (0.30 µm/h)(1/3 h) = 0.100 µm = 100 nm. To judge it, find A = B ÷ (B/A) = 0.027/0.30 = 0.090 µm, so A²/4B = 0.0081/0.108 = 0.075 h, or 4.5 minutes. Twenty minutes is four and a half times the characteristic time, and the predicted 100 nm is larger than A: the linear regime is left well behind.

The full law with τ = 0 reads x² + 0.090x = 0.009, so x = (−0.090 + √(0.0081 + 0.036))/2 = (−0.090 + 0.210)/2 = 0.060 µm = 60 nm. The linear law overestimates by 1 + x/A = 1 + 0.060/0.090 = 1.67, which is exactly 100/60; the parabolic law, √(0.027/3) = 95 nm, overestimates as well, by √(1 + A/x) = 1.58. With Deal and Grove's dry-oxygen offset at this temperature, τ = 0.076 h, the full law gives 69 nm — the anomalous initial layer adds almost ten nanometres.

4 · Scaling, leakage and high-κ dielectrics

The gate oxide has thinned by a factor of about fifty since 1980, because the gate must keep control of a channel that keeps getting shorter, and control is capacitance per unit area. Thinning helped the total-dose response at every step — the tox² law of M07 §3 and the tunnelling neutralization of M08 §8 — until it ran into a limit that has nothing to do with radiation: below about 2 nm, electrons tunnel straight through the oxide. The industry's answer, a physically thicker film of higher permittivity, reverses part of the radiation benefit, and the tension between those two facts is the subject of this section.

Deck 07 p. 14 gives the trajectory: a gate oxide of about 600 Å in 1980, 200 Å in 1990, 40–50 Å in 2000 and 11 Å in 2010, the last described as the physical limit of SiO₂ set by direct tunnelling. The driver is geometric. A gate shorter than the distance over which the drain's field penetrates loses control of the channel to the drain, and the ratio the industry held between gate length and oxide thickness was about 45 (EEE6338 M02 §4). Planar transistors then gave way to fins and stacked nanosheets, which wrap the gate around the channel to recover control by geometry rather than by thickness alone (EEL5385 M02 §3). The 11 Å limit is a physical thickness of SiO₂, and production reached it: Intel's 65 nm process of 2004 kept the 1.2 nm physical gate oxide, a lightly nitrided SiO₂, of the node before it rather than thin it further and raise the gate leakage (Bai et al.). The next reduction came from a new material, not a thinner film. Hafnium-based dielectrics entered production at the 45 nm node in 2007 at an equivalent thickness of 1.0 nm (Mistry et al.), and from then on the leading-edge figure is an equivalent oxide thickness, defined below, rather than a physical one.

Ultrathin SiO₂: the problem is leakage

Deck 04 p. 24 compresses the thin-oxide problem into one line — for ultrathin SiO₂ the main issue is leakage, with or without radiation — and supports it with two measurements. The first, from Suñé and co-workers, is a ladder of current–voltage characteristics for one ultrathin oxide in successively worse states. A fresh oxide passes current below the measurement floor until the field is high enough for Fowler–Nordheim tunnelling, the field-driven tunnelling of electrons through the triangular tip of the oxide barrier treated in §7. After high-field stress the same oxide conducts at much lower voltage: this stress-induced leakage current, SILC, flows by tunnelling in steps through neutral traps the stress created. Further stress produces a localized conducting path across the film, first as soft breakdown, SBD, in which the path carries a limited, noisy current, and then as hard breakdown, HBD, in which thermal runaway along the path shorts the oxide.

The second measurement, from Ceschia and co-workers, shows that ionizing radiation produces the same low-field rise. Gate current density in an ultrathin oxide, measured against oxide field after doses from zero to 50 Mrad(Si), climbs with dose through the range of a few MV/cm and rejoins the unirradiated curve at high field, where Fowler–Nordheim conduction dominates every state. This is radiation-induced leakage current, RILC, and it is the radiation analogue of SILC: irradiation creates neutral traps in the oxide as high-field stress does, and electrons tunnel through them in steps. It follows that the two failure modes of an irradiated ultrathin oxide are not the same as those of a thick one. A thin oxide loses little threshold voltage, because it traps little and tunnelling neutralizes what it traps, but its gate current can rise by orders of magnitude.

The reason to accept a new material is leakage at fixed capacitance. Direct tunnelling current falls exponentially with barrier width, so a film four times thicker at the same EOT — M04's example, 4 nm of relative permittivity 15.6 for 1 nm EOT — leaks far less. The high-κ materials have smaller bandgaps and smaller barriers to silicon than SiO₂ (Robertson), so the gain comes entirely from the added physical thickness, not from a better barrier. Deck 07 pp. 9–10 describe the stacks that resulted. Every one keeps a thin interlayer of SiO₂ or SiOₓ against the silicon, because the Si/SiO₂ interface of §2 is the best available and the high-κ film is deposited on it rather than grown from the silicon. The high-κ layer itself is mostly HfO₂ or a nitrided or silicate variant — HfO₂, HfSiON and ZrO₂ are the materials the deck names — deposited by ALD. Polysilicon gates gave way to metal gates of TiN, TaN or W. Stacks are made either gate-first, with the metal gate in place before the high-temperature source/drain anneal, as in the TiN/LaO/HfO₂/interlayer n-channel and TiN/AlO/HfO₂/interlayer p-channel structures of Yeh et al. reproduced on p. 9, or gate-last, where a sacrificial gate is removed after the hot steps and replaced by metal on HfO₂ and the interlayer.

How high-κ stacks respond to radiation

Deck 07 lists the radiation issues. High-κ films trap charge at higher rates than SiO₂, and they trap both electrons and holes, where thermal SiO₂ traps mainly holes. They carry a higher density of border traps — the near-interface traps of §6 that exchange charge with the silicon during a measurement. Their response is sensitive to processing, including whether the film was deposited by ALD or by chemical vapour deposition, and it depends strongly on bias and field. Deck 04 p. 25 shows what that means for a device, with data from Felix and co-workers.

In the left panel of p. 25 a hafnium silicate film of 4.5 nm EOT reaches a midgap shift of about −0.4 V at 1 Mrad(SiO₂), while SiO₂ controls of 6.0 to 10.0 nm show no measurable shift below about 500 krad and the 10 nm control reaches only about −0.2 V at 1 Mrad. The two curves are not the same quantity: the silicate curve is a midgap shift, which counts oxide-trapped charge alone, and the SiO₂ curve a threshold shift, which also contains the interface-trap contribution (M07 §9), so the ratio between them is indicative rather than exact. The ordering is still the reverse of what the tox² law predicts for oxides of these EOTs, which would put the 4.5 nm film at about a fifth of the 10 nm shift. The right panel shows an alumina film of 8 nm EOT reaching about −1.2 to −1.3 V at 1 Mrad(SiO₂), with nearly the same result whether irradiated at +1 or −1 MV/cm. The slide's title states the conclusion: alternative dielectrics have more defects and trap more charge than thermal SiO₂. Two mechanisms make it so. The physical film is thicker than its EOT and so absorbs more energy per unit area at the same dose, and its defect density is higher, since it is deposited rather than grown from the silicon. The response also depends on bias history more than SiO₂'s does: the dense border-trap population exchanges charge with the silicon when the bias is switched, so part of the measured shift is reversible, the switched-bias behaviour M09 §3 describes for SiO₂ at larger amplitude.

The course's sources disagree on whether high-κ is a hardening route. Deck 04 p. 25, Deck 07 p. 9 ("higher trapping rates for both electrons and holes") and Lecture 7, where high-κ was described as good for physical thickness but not for radiation tolerance, all say that a high-κ film traps more charge than SiO₂. Deck 06 p. 48 lists alternative dielectrics among the ways radiation hardening is achieved, and Deck 07 p. 9 adds that thin high-κ "can be rad-hard enough". The statements refer to different quantities and are consistent. Per unit of equivalent thickness a high-κ film traps more charge than SiO₂, and at equal EOT it is the softer material. But a modern stack is thin in absolute terms — an EOT near 1 nm and below in FinFETs and nanosheets (Deck 07 p. 41) — so the total charge is small and sits within tunnelling range of an electrode, where much of it is neutralized or exchanges charge reversibly (§6). A thin high-κ stack is tolerant because it is thin, not because the material is hard. The course counts alternative dielectrics as a hardening route in that sense — the route that lets the gate dielectric keep scaling without paying for it in leakage — and §9 places it among the four process levers.

5 · Structure and the origin of defects

The two defects that carry radiation-induced charge, the E′ centre of M07 §6 and the Pb centre of M07 §8, exist in precursor form before any irradiation, and their precursors are consequences of how the oxide is built and heated. This section describes the structure of the film and its interface, lists where Deck 07 locates the origin of each defect, and gathers the several roles hydrogen plays, since hydrogen is both the cure for one defect and the agent that later re-creates it.

In crystalline silicon each atom is bonded to four silicon neighbours arranged tetrahedrally. In silicon dioxide each silicon is bonded instead to four oxygen atoms at the corners of a tetrahedron, and each oxygen bridges two silicons, so the tetrahedra share corners and form a continuous network. Thermal oxide is amorphous: the tetrahedra are nearly perfect but the angles at which they join vary, so the network has short-range order and no long-range order (EEE6338 M05a §3). Where this network meets the crystal, the two cannot match. The oxide occupies more volume than the silicon it came from and has no lattice to register with, so not every silicon atom at the interface finds four bonding partners, and the unsatisfied bonds that remain are dangling bonds (Deck 07 p. 15). Deck 07 names the two remedies: dangling bonds are passivated by hydrogen, for example during the metal sinter — the final anneal in a hydrogen-bearing ambient, often forming gas, at 400–450 °C (EEE6338 M05 §6.3 covers it) — and they can be mitigated by incorporating nitrogen at the interface.

The mismatch also leaves a composition gradient. Silicon right at the interface cannot be fully oxidized, so a thin, non-stoichiometric transition layer containing excess silicon forms next to the crystal, and it moves with the interface as the oxide grows. Deal and co-workers found positively charged, process-induced defects associated with that excess silicon within about 20 nm of the interface, more after low-temperature oxidation and fewer after high-temperature annealing; Oldham points out that this process-induced charge is the same kind of defect that later holds radiation-induced charge, and that radiation-hardened oxides have often used low-temperature oxidation and low-temperature anneals, the two conditions that increase the excess silicon. That apparent paradox is resolved in §9.

Oxygen vacancies and hydrogen-related centres

Deck 07 p. 16 names the principal defects of MOS oxides and where they come from. Oxygen vacancies — sites where a bridging oxygen is missing and two silicons bond directly — form during processing by four routes: incomplete or inefficient oxidation; mechanical strain; out-diffusion of oxygen during the post-oxidation anneal; and implantation damage. The vacancy and its complexes form the dominant MOS oxide trap: a hole that reaches one breaks the weak Si–Si bond and leaves an E′ centre (p. 17; the mechanism is M07 §6). The silicon dangling bond that results from incomplete passivation of the interface is the dominant interface trap, the Pb centre. Hydrogen-related centres form either during processing or during later thermal, irradiation or electrical stress, and the deck gives four reasons hydrogen is involved so often: it is ubiquitous in processing; it is light and mobile; it bonds readily to both silicon and oxygen; and the bonds it forms are relatively weak.

Hydrogen's roles are worth listing together, because they pull in opposite directions. It is a passivant: bonded to an interface dangling bond as Si–H, it removes the state from the gap, and without it no oxide would meet the 10¹⁰ cm⁻² target of §1. That same Si–H bond is the target of the two-stage process of M07 §8: a proton released by a transporting hole takes the hydrogen and leaves the Pb centre behind, so the hydrogen put in to passivate becomes the reactant that depassivates. It is a reservoir: hydrogen left in the oxide, introduced by wet oxidation, by ammonia nitridation or by the sinter, and hydrogen supplied later by the package, sets how many protons the holes can release — which is why package hydrogen alone changes the low-dose-rate response of a bipolar part by up to two orders of magnitude (M08 §9). And hydrogen-related species may act as border traps themselves, or change their density (Deck 07 p. 22). The passivation reaction also does not run to completion. Oldham reports oxides 35 nm thick with about 1.3×10¹² cm⁻² neutral electron traps, annealed in successively more hydrogen: even after 50 atm of forming gas, more than 10¹¹ cm⁻² remained, which he reads as passivation and depassivation standing in equilibrium. Hydrogen control therefore means controlling the amount and where it sits, not removing it.

6 · Oxide, interface and border traps

A trap is classified less by what it is than by how quickly it exchanges charge with the silicon, measured against the speed of the measurement being made. That definition decides what each technique on this site counts: the midgap separation of M07 §9, charge pumping, C–V hysteresis, low-frequency noise. Deck 07 builds the vocabulary in two steps, from a four-charge model to a three-trap one, and gives a rule for converting a trap's depth into its response time.

The older classification is the four-population scheme M06 §3 starts from, before adding border traps as a fifth: fixed oxide charge, oxide-trapped charge, mobile ionic charge and interface-trapped charge, standardized by a committee chaired by Deal in 1980. In that scheme a state either follows the silicon Fermi level during a measurement, and is an interface trap, or does not, and is oxide charge. Fleetwood pointed out in 1992 that the scheme has no place for traps that sit in the oxide but close enough to the interface to exchange charge with the silicon by tunnelling on the timescale of a measurement, and named them border traps (IEEE Trans. Nucl. Sci. 39, 269; the nomenclature is set out in J. Appl. Phys. 73, 5058, 1993). Deck 07 p. 18 gives the resulting three classes. Oxide traps — trapped holes and "neutral" electron traps — are essentially fixed charge during a measurement. Interface traps, the Pb defects, switch state during the measurement. Border traps lie between, electrostatically oxide traps and kinetically able to respond.

Deck 07 p. 22 turns the classes into frequency bands. Bulk oxide traps switch slower than about 0.01 Hz; border traps between about 0.01 Hz and 10 kHz; interface traps faster than about 10 kHz. In the Deal-committee model the middle band is empty. In the distribution the slide shows for a soft oxide, the border-trap band holds more states than the interface-trap band, which is the practical reason the distinction matters: a technique that counts everything responding faster than its own sweep as an interface trap assigns border traps to the wrong class. The same slide gives candidate structures. The interface trap is Si₃≡Si•, the Pb centre. A fast border trap is a silicon dangling bond back-bonded to a mixture of oxygen and silicon, (O/Si)₃≡Si•, in the transition layer of §5. A slow border trap is an E′-type defect related to an oxygen vacancy, O₃≡Si⁺ or O₃≡Si•, a little deeper in the oxide. The slow border trap is the oxide-trap charge of M07 made reversible: Oldham's review identifies the dipole formed when an electron tunnels to the neutral side of a charged E′ centre as the dominant switching oxide trap, and notes that hardened oxides show this switching while many soft commercial ones do not.

Whether a given trap behaves as a border trap depends on its distance from the silicon, because electron tunnelling falls off exponentially with distance. Deck 07 p. 19 states the rule of thumb.

7 · Radiation and high-field stress make the same defects

A strong electric field applied to an oxide, with no radiation present, leaves behind trapped holes, border traps and interface traps next to the silicon — the defect set of M07. The route is different and the endpoint is the same, which is the physical basis of the overlap between radiation effects and oxide reliability. This section develops the high-field route: how current crosses the oxide at high field, how the oxide charges while it does, and how the damage accumulates through stress-induced leakage to breakdown. The conduction physics is derived in EEE6338 M06 §§1–5; what follows takes the results and connects them to the radiation response.

Deck 07 p. 25 sets the two routes side by side for an n-channel device under positive gate bias. Under irradiation, electron–hole pairs are generated throughout the oxide; the electrons are swept to the gate within picoseconds, and the holes hop slowly toward the silicon, where some are trapped as oxide charge, some become border traps, and some release the protons that create interface traps. Under Fowler–Nordheim stress, electrons are injected from the silicon into the oxide conduction band and accelerate across it to the gate, the anode. Those that arrive hot deposit their energy near the anode, which injects holes back into the oxide — anode hole injection — and at high enough energy they create electron–hole pairs within the oxide by impact ionization across its gap. The holes so produced travel toward the silicon under the same field that moves radiation-generated holes, and are trapped in the same places. Hot electrons also break Si–H bonds once heated above about 2 eV (Oldham, after DiMaria), a second route to interface traps.

Fowler–Nordheim conduction

At low field an electron in the silicon faces the full rectangular barrier of the oxide, 3.1 eV high at the Si/SiO₂ conduction-band offset and as wide as the film. At high field the oxide conduction band slopes steeply, so the barrier seen by an electron at the silicon band edge becomes a triangle whose width, φB/qE, is shorter than the film. The electron tunnels through the triangular tip into the oxide conduction band and drifts the rest of the way. The current depends on field alone, and because tunnelling needs no thermal activation it is essentially independent of temperature (Sze & Ng §4.3.4).

The oxide charges while it conducts

Current through an oxide at high field changes the oxide, and a constant-current stress makes the change visible as a drifting voltage. Fowler–Nordheim current depends on the field at the injecting electrode, the cathode, so any charge trapped in the film changes the voltage needed to hold the current fixed. Positive charge near the cathode steepens the barrier there and lets the same current flow at a lower applied voltage; negative charge trapped in the bulk of the film shields the cathode and requires a higher one. Deck 07 p. 27 shows both in one trace.

The trace belongs to a 17 nm thermal oxide held at 2.8 µA through a 0.0012 cm² capacitor, which is 2.3×10⁻³ A/cm²; at about 15.2 V across 17 nm the field is 8.9 MV/cm, and the slide's 10 mC/cm² of injected charge takes 4.3 s. The plotted trace runs to 5 s, about 12 mC/cm², so the slide's injected charge is the nominal figure for the stress. In the first 0.6 s the voltage falls from about 15.18 to 15.13 V, then rises nearly linearly to about 15.28 V at 5 s. Positive charge near the injecting interface dominates first and saturates quickly; electron trapping in the bulk continues for as long as current flows and takes over. Both are defect-filling processes, and the positive component is the one irradiation also produces. At constant voltage the same two processes act as negative feedback (electron trapping lowers the cathode field and the current) and positive feedback (hole trapping raises them), the form in which EEE6338 M06 §3 describes them; the constant-current trace is the same physics read as voltage.

Stress-induced leakage and breakdown

Stress that stops short of breakdown still leaves the oxide leakier, and the extra current at low field is the most sensitive electrical sign that traps have been generated. Deck 07 p. 29 shows it for a 3.3 nm SiO₂ capacitor stressed for 10 s at each of a series of gate voltages from −4.0 to −4.8 V (Massengill et al.). After stress the current at low gate voltage rises by two to three decades above the pre-stress curve, while at high voltage all the curves meet in the Fowler–Nordheim region, entered at a kink near an oxide voltage of 4.5 V; the oxide voltage is the gate voltage less a −1.2 V offset from the polysilicon gate. The extra low-field current flows by tunnelling in steps through the neutral traps the stress created — traps that carry no charge, and so shift no threshold, but that act as intermediate states. In thicker films SILC decays as those traps fill; in thin ones tunnelling out is as easy as tunnelling in, and the leakage becomes a steady current path (EEE6338 M06 §4). Its radiation twin, RILC, is §4's.

Continued generation of traps ends in breakdown. Deck 07 p. 30 states the two fields that bracket the process: breakdown typically above 10 MV/cm for thin oxides, but degradation — the trap generation that leads to it — above about 6 MV/cm. Its histograms of breakdown field are labelled E0 = 14.3 MV/cm for 12 nm oxides and 13.4 MV/cm for 18 nm oxides. The slide does not define E0, and for the thinner film it is not where the devices break: the 12 nm histogram peaks near 15.4 MV/cm, with nearly every device between about 14.9 and 15.9 MV/cm, while the 18 nm histogram peaks near 13.6 MV/cm, close to its label. Each distribution is narrow, with most devices breaking within about a megavolt per centimetre of the peak. Breakdown proceeds in two stages: a slow wear-out in which defects accumulate until a chain of them spans the film, then a fast runaway in which current concentrates in that path and destroys it (EEE6338 M06 §5). The slide's closing question points at time-dependent dielectric breakdown: an oxide held at a field well below its ramped breakdown field still breaks down eventually, after a time that falls steeply as the field rises, because the wear-out stage proceeds at any field above the degradation threshold; the operating-field limits that follow are in EEE6338 M05 §4.2. The radiation counterpart is abrupt: a single heavy ion crossing a gate oxide under high field can trigger breakdown at once, the single-event gate rupture of M11 §10.

8 · Net against total trapped charge

Every electrical measurement used so far reads a net quantity. The midgap shift of M07 §9 is the net charge in the oxide, trapped holes minus any electrons trapped near them, and M07 §7 and M09 §6 showed that the compensating electrons can be numerous. An oxide with a small net shift may therefore hold a large number of trapped holes and a nearly equal number of trapped electrons, and the two populations anneal, switch and fail differently. Deck 07 closes on the distinction: C–V and I–V methods sense the net oxide-trap and interface charge, while thermally stimulated current senses the total oxide-trap charge. This section combines the two to separate the signs, and adds the room-temperature measurement that counts border traps.

Thermally stimulated current, TSC, was introduced in M07 §6 as a way to map the energy distribution of trapped holes. Deck 07 p. 35 uses it as a counting method. A capacitor is irradiated or high-field stressed under positive bias, which leaves net positive charge trapped near the Si/SiO₂ interface together with electrons in compensating traps. It is then heated at a controlled rate with an ammeter in series. As the temperature rises, trapped charge is released, and the current, after the leakage current of the undamaged capacitor is subtracted, integrates to the total trapped positive charge — the trapped holes, compensated or not. A C–V measurement of the same capacitor before heating gives the net charge from the midgap shift, and the difference is the negative charge.

C–V hysteresis measures border traps

A border trap exchanges charge with the silicon on the timescale of a slow voltage sweep, which gives a simple way to count them. Sweep a capacitor's gate voltage from accumulation to inversion and back. Border traps fill as the surface potential moves one way and empty as it returns, lagging the gate each time, so the two sweeps give displaced curves; the difference between them, reverse minus forward, is a convenient measure of slow-state or border-trap density (Deck 07 p. 39). Interface traps respond too fast to lag a slow sweep, and deep oxide traps too slowly to respond at all, so the hysteresis isolates the middle band of §6.

Deck 07 pp. 39–40 apply it to a 17 nm thermal oxide. After 10 mC/cm² of Fowler–Nordheim injection the hysteresis peaks at about 37 pF near −0.2 V; after 2 Mrad(SiO₂) of X-rays, at about 28 pF near −0.55 V. The slides draw the pre-stress curve and the curve measured after the TSC anneal as one, so the heating of the TSC measurement removes the stress-induced charge. The slide's conclusion is that radiation and high-field stress create similar border traps. The result agrees with M06 §3, which already assigns room-temperature hysteresis to border traps. Mobile sodium and potassium drift only under bias at elevated temperature, so they show up as a flat-band shift after a bias–temperature stress; hysteresis in a room-temperature sweep of an irradiated or stressed oxide is the border-trap signature.

9 · Hardening by process

Deck 06 closes, and Deck 07 opens, with a one-line statement of how an oxide is made hard: radiation hardening is achieved via hydrogen control, optimized oxidation, alternative dielectrics and process modifications. Each of the four is a way of changing the inputs listed at the start of this module — the number of hole traps, where they sit, and the hydrogen available to turn trapped holes into interface traps — and each follows from the physics of the preceding sections. The kinetic levers, bias and temperature in service and in test, belong to M09 §10; the layout levers to M08 §6. This section takes the process levers in turn, after the history that established them.

Hardness is made in the process line

The idea that a process can be tuned for radiation hardness dates from the first hardened MOS technologies. Deck 02 pp. 8–9 mark the two landmark papers: Aubuchon's radiation-hard p-channel MOS with an SiO₂ gate dielectric (IEEE Trans. Nucl. Sci., December 1971) and Derbenwick and Gregory's radiation-hardened CMOS (December 1975). The data Deck 02 p. 8 reproduces carry both of the conclusions the slide draws. Threshold and flat-band shifts at 10⁶ rad under +10 V gate bias fall steeply as the oxide is made thinner — oxide thickness plays a key role — and at a given final thickness an oxide grown thick and etched back shifts more than one grown to that thickness, so the growth history matters as well as the thickness: the fabrication process is very important. The same slide shows the bias dependence M09 §3 develops, a flat-band shift of a 700 Å oxide at 10⁶ rad of about 0.1–0.2 V under negative gate bias rising to about 1.2 V under +10 V. Deck 02 p. 9 then shows the hardened wet oxides of that era serving as the vehicle for the hydrogen model of interface-trap buildup: switching the field across a 965 Å hardened wet oxide between polarities during the buildup, after 0.6 Mrad of LINAC irradiation, showed negative bias inhibiting the second stage (McLean, 1980). Process hardening and the hydrogen mechanism were established on the same samples.

Hydrogen control

The interface trap is made in two stages, and the second consumes hydrogen: a proton released near an interface by a transporting hole drifts to the Si/SiO₂ interface and depassivates an Si–H bond (M07 §8). Fewer hydrogen species in the oxide therefore means fewer protons, fewer Pb centres and a smaller interface-trap density, with everything that follows from it in M08 — less mobility and drive loss, less timing degradation and less rebound. This is the first lever: radiation hardening by hydrogen control. Its instruments are the hydrogen content of every ambient the gate oxide sees after growth — the post-oxidation and post-metallization anneal ambients, wet against dry oxidation, the avoidance of NH₃ nitridation — and, after fabrication, the hydrogen supplied by the package. It cannot mean removing hydrogen altogether, because the sinter needs hydrogen to reach the 10¹⁰ cm⁻² interface-trap target of §1; it means supplying enough to passivate and no more to leave behind.

Deck 07 p. 33 puts the lever in numbers, from Winokur and co-workers: two processes, one finished with a forming-gas anneal — a thermal anneal in a hydrogen-bearing forming gas — and one with an anneal in nitrogen, irradiated with 10 V on the gate. The forming-gas device shows ΔVot = −2.67 V and ΔVit = +2.59 V, summing to a net ΔVth of −0.08 V. The N₂ device shows −1.48 V and +1.08 V, summing to −0.40 V. By the single number a threshold measurement reports, the forming-gas process looks five times better. By its components it is worse on both counts: through (7.5), and for equal oxide thickness, it carries 2.4 times the interface-trap density and 1.8 times the oxide-trap charge. The hydrogen-rich anneal supplied the reactant for interface-trap formation. The consequence arrives with time: trapped holes anneal and interface traps do not (M08 §3), so the forming-gas device is headed toward a positive shift approaching +2.59 V, with the mobility and timing loss of 2.4 times as many interface traps, while the N₂ device approaches +1.08 V. The slide asks which process is best; the physics answers that the N₂ anneal is, and that a near-zero net shift can conceal the worst component values of all.

Optimized oxidation

The second lever is the oxidation itself: thickness, growth temperature and chemistry, and the post-oxidation anneal and cooling ramp that Deck 07 p. 12 singles out. Thickness was the first variable understood, through the tox² law and later the tunnelling limit, and it is now set by the device rather than by radiation. The thermal steps are subtler, and Oldham's review gives the clearest single case. Texas Instruments produced hardened and unhardened gate oxides from one split lot, identical except for one step: the hardened oxide received its post-oxidation anneal at a lower temperature. Measured a hundred or a thousand seconds after a pulse of radiation, the hardened oxide showed about an order of magnitude less threshold shift per unit thickness. Extrapolated back to the end of the pulse the difference shrinks and may vanish, which suggests the two oxides trap about the same number of holes but that the hardened oxide traps them closer to the interface, where electrons tunnelling from the silicon neutralize them faster.

This resolves the paradox of §5. A high-temperature anneal in an inert ambient at the oxidation temperature, standard in commercial processing since Deal's early work, removes excess silicon from the interface region and cures a process-induced instability — but it also appears to drive oxygen vacancies deeper into the oxide, out of tunnelling range, where the holes they capture persist. Omitting it leaves more defects near the interface but places them within reach of the silicon, so their trapped charge is short-lived. The hardened oxide pays for this: the same process change that speeds its annealing also makes its oxide traps switch charge reversibly with the silicon under bias, the border-trap behaviour of §6 and the compensation §8 detected. Oldham notes that radiation-hardened oxides have often used low-temperature oxidation and low-temperature anneals, and Deck 07 p. 16 adds the chemical warning on the other side: oxygen out-diffusion during the post-oxidation anneal is itself a source of vacancies. Optimized oxidation means choosing the thermal history for the location of the defects, not only their number.

Alternative dielectrics

The third lever replaces or modifies the SiO₂. Light nitridation in N₂O or NO is the modest form: it leaves the constants of §1 and the conduction of §7 essentially unchanged, adds nitrogen at the interface where Deck 07 p. 15 says it mitigates dangling bonds, and changes the near-interfacial trapping that TSC detects (§8). High-κ stacks are the radical form, and §4 set out their terms: per unit of equivalent thickness they trap more charge, of both signs, than SiO₂, but a stack with an EOT near 1 nm is thin enough that its total trapped charge is small and largely within tunnelling range of an electrode, so the Deck 07 p. 9 judgement — thin high-κ can be rad-hard enough — holds. In the course's list, alternative dielectrics are a route to radiation-hard gate dielectrics in that sense: thin nitrided oxides and thin high-κ stacks that let the gate keep scaling, with their tolerance coming from thinness.

Process modifications

The fourth lever covers every step after gate oxidation that can add defects or charge them. The radiative steps of §1 — plasma etching and deposition, electron-beam and X-ray lithography — ionize the finished gate oxide, and their damage must be annealed out; implantation near the gate creates oxygen vacancies (Deck 07 p. 16); and the hydrogen-bearing sinter is placed last so that nothing after it undoes the passivation. In scaled technologies the lever extends beyond the gate oxide to the oxides that now fail first — trench isolation and the buried oxide of silicon on insulator (M08 §§5–8) — where a channel-stop implant or a modified buried oxide is a process change rather than a layout one. Deck 07's summary gives the rule that governs all four levers: every oxide process is different and requires characterization and control, and published results should be trusted, mostly, but verified.

Deck 07 p. 34 frames the decision as a question about four oxides after irradiation: (a) holds a few trapped holes and nothing else; (b) more trapped holes and interface traps; (c) trapped holes, electrons compensating them in border traps, and interface traps; (d) trapped holes and compensating electrons, with no interface traps. A threshold or midgap measurement ranks them by net charge, and on that ranking (d) and (c) can look better than (b) because compensation cancels part of their positive charge. Ranked by what each defect does, they sort differently. Interface traps cost mobility, drive and timing and drive rebound (M08 §§3–4); compensating electrons in border traps make the charge reversible under bias, a source of threshold instability, hysteresis and low-frequency noise (§§6, 8); trapped holes shift the threshold and anneal. Oxides (a) and (b) carry no compensating electrons, so their net (midgap) oxide-trap charge equals their total (TSC) charge by (10.9). Oxide (a) also has no interface traps and so holds the fewest kinds of defect; oxide (c) holds all three. Whether (b) or (d) is preferable depends on whether an application is limited by drive and timing or by threshold stability. The forming-gas and N₂ anneals above are a measured instance of the same lesson: the component values, not the net shift, decide which process is better.

All four levers act on the same three quantities: how many traps the process leaves, how far they sit from the silicon, and how much hydrogen is available to convert trapped holes into interface traps. A process is hard when those are controlled, and the only reliable way to know whether they are is to measure the components — midgap and threshold, TSC and C–V — rather than the net shift.

10 · Where gate dielectrics are going

Deck 07 ends with the questions the field is working on now, and most of them follow from pushing §§4–6 further. The gate dielectric keeps thinning and changing material, the channel beneath it changes shape and material, and the metals and dielectrics around it multiply. Each change moves where the radiation-induced charge sits and how much dose reaches it.

The equivalent thickness of production gate stacks is now below 1 nm in FinFETs and nanosheets, and in gate-all-around nanosheet transistors — stacked horizontal channels with the gate wrapped around each one — tunnelling dominates (Deck 07 pp. 41–42). For total dose the consequence extends §4 and M08 §8: charge trapped in so thin a film is largely neutralized or exchanges reversibly with the channel, so the classical threshold shift of the gate dielectric shrinks, and what remains are leakage and the charge exchange of individual border traps. That exchange is now measured one trap at a time. When a single trap captures and emits a carrier, the drain current switches between two levels — random telegraph noise — and the ratio of the mean emission and capture times, followed as the gate overdrive changes, locates the trap in depth. Deck 07 p. 41 reproduces such a measurement on 14 nm bulk silicon transistors (Gorchichko et al., 2023), placing individual traps between 0.25 and 1.50 nm from the interface. They are border traps on kinetic grounds: each switches slowly enough to appear as discrete levels in a time record of the drain current, which places it in the border-trap band of §6, and they are resolved one by one. Their depths are not the tunnelling distances of (10.6). They are electrostatic depths, found from how the ratio of emission to capture time changes with gate overdrive, and the gate dielectric of a 14 nm transistor is a hafnium-based high-κ film on an SiO₂ interlayer (§4). Equation (10.6) is a rule for SiO₂, 25 Å for about 1 s, and does not carry over to that stack: applied to these depths it would give exchange times of 10⁻⁹ to 10⁻⁴ s, too fast to resolve as telegraph switching.

The metals around the dielectric raise the dose it receives. Secondary electrons liberated in high-atomic-number gate and back-end metals deposit extra energy in the adjacent dielectric under X-ray and γ irradiation — the dose enhancement of M04 §5 — which matters most in low-energy X-ray testing and in space, and grows in importance for three-dimensional integration and packaging, where metals and oxides are interleaved. Deck 07 p. 41 gives 2–8× enhancement observed in HfO₂/TiN capacitors. M04 §5, from the calculations of Deck 03 p. 22 for a hafnium-oxide gate stack, gives roughly 2 to 4. The two ranges describe different things: M04's comes from calculations for particular layer thicknesses and overlayers, and M04 §5 shows the factor falling as the dielectric thins and changing as back-end metal is added, while Deck 07's is a span of observations across capacitor structures and irradiation conditions, for which the slide gives no single source. In every case the factor multiplies the dose: the dielectric absorbs DEF times the dose an equilibrium reference would, by (4.8), and generates charge in proportion to that enhanced dose. The factor is near one for Co-60, which is one reason gamma rays remain the qualification standard (EEL5385 M10 §9).

The channel is changing too. Two-dimensional semiconductors such as MoS₂ and WS₂ form channels a few atoms thick, so every carrier sits next to the dielectric, and Deck 07 p. 42 records their sensitivity to charge trapped at the oxide/2D interface. Hafnium zirconium oxide in its ferroelectric phase holds a remanent polarization that can store a bit without power, which makes it a promising non-volatile memory; early tests show radiation-driven depolarization, a loss of the stored polarization. Cryogenic operation, for quantum and deep-space electronics, alters the total-dose response because the thermally activated processes stop: annealing slows and charge freezes in place, as Deck 07 puts it. Hole hopping, thermal emission from traps and proton transport all slow sharply, while tunnelling, which needs no thermal activation, continues; M09 §9 works through what that does to the balance of oxide-trap and interface-trap charge.

The open questions on Deck 07 p. 42 are about oxides that were never gate dielectrics: the oxide liners of through-silicon vias and interposers in three-dimensional integrated circuits, mixed stacks of SiO₂, HfO₂ and silicon carbonitride in the back end, and all of these in harsh environments — space, cryogenic and nuclear. Predicting them needs modelling across scales, from density-functional calculations of individual defects through device simulation to system behaviour. The slide's closing line is the module's: oxides remain the weak link for scaling, reliability and radiation hardness.

Where this leaves us

The gate oxide is a manufactured component whose radiation response is fixed, to first order, before it is irradiated. Growth converts silicon to SiO₂ at a buried interface, at a rate the Deal–Grove law predicts above about 20 nm and fails to predict below it; the thermal history that follows — anneal temperature, ambient, ramp — sets how many oxygen vacancies remain, where they sit, and how much hydrogen the film holds. Scaling drove the oxide to the tunnelling limit and then to high-κ stacks that trap more per unit of equivalent thickness but, being thin, hold little charge in total. The defects are the same whether radiation or high field creates them, and they are classified by the speed at which they exchange charge with the silicon, which is why border traps, hysteresis, noise and compensation belong to one picture and why net measurements must be paired with total ones.

Hardening by process follows directly: control hydrogen, choose the oxidation and anneal for the location as well as the number of defects, use thin or modified dielectrics, and keep the rest of the process from adding damage. The next module leaves the oxide for the silicon beneath it. M11 follows a single ion through a device, where the charge that matters is collected within nanoseconds rather than trapped for years — and where the gate oxide returns once more, as the dielectric a heavy ion can rupture.
