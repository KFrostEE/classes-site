Module 02

The Space Radiation Environment

The three natural particle populations of near-Earth space, the geomagnetic field that creates one of them and filters the other two, and the way orbit and solar activity fix what a spacecraft actually receives.

Prerequisites M01 Read time ~45 min Sources Deck 01 pp. 8–24, 29–41; Lectures 1–2; Xapsos, NSREC 2018 Short Course

M01 established how each incident species couples to matter: which particles ionize directly, which do so only through secondary products, which are stopped by a spacecraft wall and which are not. That is half of a radiation requirement. The other half is arrival — which species reach a given orbit, at what energies, in what numbers, and how steadily. This module supplies that half.

Near-Earth space carries three natural populations of energetic particles, and they are physically unrelated. Galactic cosmic rays come from outside the solar system and arrive continuously from all directions. Solar particle events are episodic bursts from the Sun. The trapped belts are a population held in place by the Earth's own magnetic field. Each has a different composition, a different energy range, a different time behaviour, and a different dominant effect on electronics. The geomagnetic field ties them together: it creates the trapped belts by confining charged particles, and it partially excludes the other two, so the exposure of any Earth-orbiting spacecraft depends as strongly on the geometry of its orbit relative to that field as on the populations themselves. Which populations dominate at a given orbit follows from the two together. Converting an arriving flux into an absorbed dose is the subject of M03.

1 · The populations, and where they come from

Radiation environments relevant to microelectronics divide into three groups by origin. The natural space environment contains trapped electrons and trapped protons in the Van Allen belts, galactic cosmic rays, and solar particle events. The atmospheric environment is a secondary environment: it consists of the particles produced when the first group strikes the upper atmosphere, and its dominant component at aircraft and ground level is the neutron. Man-made environments are those produced deliberately — particle accelerators, nuclear reactors and their accident conditions, and nuclear weapons. The Van Allen belts belong firmly to the first group; they are a natural consequence of the Earth having a magnetic field and a supply of charged particles, and they existed long before anything was launched into them.

The outer boundary of the region this module describes is set by the Sun. The Sun continuously emits a plasma — a gas of free ions and electrons — called the solar wind, and that plasma carries the Sun's magnetic field outward with it. The wind inflates a magnetized cavity in the interstellar medium, the heliosphere, which extends far beyond the planets. Where the outflowing wind slows abruptly from supersonic to subsonic speed there is a termination shock; where its pressure finally balances that of the interstellar medium there is the heliopause, the boundary at which the Sun's influence ends. Voyager 1 crossed the termination shock at about 94 astronomical units in 2004 and the heliopause at about 122 astronomical units in August 2012; Voyager 2 crossed the heliopause at about 119 astronomical units in November 2018.

The heliosphere matters here because it is a filter. Charged particles arriving from the galaxy must diffuse inward against an outward-flowing magnetized plasma, and the lowest-energy ones largely fail to do so. Everything said below about galactic cosmic rays being modulated by solar activity is a statement about how effectively this cavity excludes them, and the effectiveness changes as the Sun's output changes.

Why the first spacecraft losses mattered

The subject exists because early satellites failed. Price catalogued the first cases in 1965: Explorer XIV lost its power supply, Explorer XV a transistor, Ariel 1 (UK-1) its solar power system, TRAAC and Transit IV B their solar cells, and Telstar I a transistor — Telstar failing in November 1962 and being partially restored in the following weeks. Several of these losses followed the Starfish Prime high-altitude nuclear test of 9 July 1962, which injected a large artificial population of energetic electrons into the trapped-particle region, where the same magnetic confinement described in §6 held it for years. A man-made source and a natural one populate the same drift shells and produce the same degradation.

2 · Galactic cosmic rays

Galactic cosmic rays are fully ionized atomic nuclei that originate outside the solar system. They arrive as a nearly isotropic flux — the same in all directions — because their long journey through the tangled magnetic fields of the galaxy has erased any memory of where they started. Those below roughly 1015 eV are generally attributed to acceleration in supernova remnants within the Milky Way. The origin of the very highest-energy particles is not established, and those above about 1018 eV are usually taken to be extragalactic.

The composition follows the abundance of the elements, with hydrogen dominating. The course uses approximately 89% ionized hydrogen, approximately 9% ionized helium, and the remaining 1–2% heavier ions together with electrons. Xapsos' 2018 short course tabulates 90% protons, 9% alphas and 1% heavier ions, but that table is of the hadron composition alone and excludes electrons, which is where most of the difference lies. Every naturally occurring element up to uranium is present, but the abundance falls steeply above iron (Z = 26), which makes iron the heaviest element that appears in appreciable numbers.

The energies are extreme. The flux peaks between 100 and 1000 MeV per nucleon, so a typical galactic cosmic ray carries kinetic energy near 1 GeV per nucleon and travels at a large fraction of the speed of light. The distribution extends to about 1020 eV, though the acceleration mechanism capable of reaching those energies is unknown. The integral flux over the whole distribution is only 1 to 10 particles per square centimetre per second.

In Earth orbit, galactic cosmic rays cause single-event effects and essentially nothing else; only on a multi-year interplanetary cruise, where neither the belts nor frequent solar events contribute, does their accumulated dose stop being negligible. Their flux is far too low to dominate ionizing dose here, but a single iron nucleus at 1 GeV per nucleon deposits energy along its track densely enough to upset or destroy a circuit node in one passage. The quantity used to describe that capability is linear energy transfer, introduced in M01 and quantified in M04. Because heavy ions have larger linear energy transfer than protons at the same velocity, the 1–2% heavy-ion component dominates the single-event hazard even though it is a small fraction of the flux, and the destructive events — latchup, burnout, gate rupture — are almost exclusively its work. That is why accelerator time is spent on heavy ions rather than on the protons that make up most of the population.

The energy spectrum of each species is therefore usually converted into an integral linear-energy-transfer spectrum: the flux of particles whose linear energy transfer exceeds a given value, summed over all species from hydrogen to uranium. That single curve, combined with the dimensions of a sensitive volume, gives the rate at which a device is struck by particles capable of depositing more than a given charge. This is the standard route from environment to error rate and it returns in M11 §8.

Why galactic cosmic rays cannot be shielded

A 1 GeV proton has a range in aluminium of order a metre, and heavier ions at the same energy per nucleon penetrate comparably far. Spacecraft shielding is measured in millimetres because every gram must be launched. Shielding therefore removes the low-energy end of the distribution and leaves the peak untouched, and adding thickness produces rapidly diminishing benefit. Worse, the material that stops a heavy ion does so by fragmenting it, producing lighter secondaries that continue forward. The galactic cosmic ray requirement is met by hardening the circuit, not by shielding it. M03 quantifies the saturation of shielding; M14 covers what hardening the circuit means.

The one respect in which galactic cosmic rays are not constant is their coupling to solar activity. The flux of ions below about 20 GeV per nucleon is modulated by the magnetic field carried in the solar wind. When solar activity is high, the heliosphere is more turbulent and more strongly magnetized, incoming particles are scattered more effectively, and fewer reach 1 AU. Galactic cosmic ray flux is therefore anti-correlated with solar activity: it is highest at solar minimum. The effect is largest at the low-energy end, where the deck notes that cosmic rays near 100 MeV are substantially deflected by the heliosphere, and it vanishes at the high-energy end where the particles are too rigid to be turned.

3 · Solar particles

The Sun is the most intense radiation source in the solar system, emitting more than 60 MW per square metre from its surface. That surface is the photosphere, the visible layer, at a temperature near 6,000 K. Above it lies the corona, a far more tenuous region of plasma at about 106 K. Almost all of the emitted power is electromagnetic and thermal, and it produces essentially no ionizing dose in shielded electronics, although solar ultraviolet and X-rays degrade exposed optical coatings and solar-cell coverglass. The hazard comes from a small, episodic fraction of the Sun's output in which magnetic energy stored in the corona is converted into particle kinetic energy.

Three distinct things are involved, and the terms are often used interchangeably in conversation even though they name different objects.

  • The solar wind is the steady, continuous outflow of plasma — protons, alpha particles and electrons at energies of electronvolts to kiloelectronvolts — carrying an embedded magnetic field. It is far too low in energy to penetrate a spacecraft wall, so it causes no bulk radiation damage. It matters because it is the medium that modulates galactic cosmic rays, because it shapes the magnetosphere, and because it charges exposed spacecraft surfaces.
  • A solar flare is a sudden, localized brightening: a burst of electromagnetic radiation released when stored coronal magnetic energy is converted through magnetic reconnection. Flares are frequently but not always accompanied by energetic particles. When they are, the particles are electron-rich, the event lasts hours, and it carries an unusually high 3He content relative to 4He.
  • A coronal mass ejection is a large eruption of magnetized plasma from the corona. If it moves fast enough it drives a shock wave ahead of it, and that shock accelerates particles continuously as it propagates. Coronal mass ejections are proton-rich, last for days, and have a low 3He content. They eject on the order of 1015 to 1017 grams of plasma at speeds from about 50 to 2,500 km/s, averaging near 450 km/s, and take from a few hours to a few days to reach the Earth.

Solar particle event is the collective term for the energetic-particle enhancement produced by either mechanism. Coronal mass ejections produce the large events: they are responsible for the major disturbances in interplanetary space and for the major geomagnetic disturbances when they strike the magnetosphere.

The composition of a solar particle event is overwhelmingly protons. The course gives 90–95% protons with alpha particles making up most of the remainder and a much smaller heavy-ion flux; the modern characterization of coronal mass ejection events is 96.4% protons, 3.5% alphas and about 0.1% heavier ions. Peak fluxes above 10 MeV per nucleon can exceed 5×105 protons per square centimetre per second, reaching about 106 in the largest events, and integral fluences for a single large event can reach 1010 particles per square centimetre. These are extreme-event ceilings; a typical event peaks orders of magnitude below them. In a large flare, the proton and alpha fluxes rise by a factor of order 104 above background, and the heavy-ion flux can approach 50% of the background galactic cosmic ray flux.

Because protons dominate, solar particle events are the principal episodic source of accumulated ionizing dose and of displacement damage, and they are why proton testing is a standard part of a qualification programme rather than an optional extra. The heavy-ion component is small but not negligible: at 50% of the galactic background during a large event, it raises the heavy-ion single-event rate by roughly half for its duration, on top of the proton-induced upsets. That figure is an upper bound on the rate change, because upset rate follows the integral linear-energy-transfer spectrum rather than the raw ion flux, and solar heavy ions are softer in energy than galactic ones.

Three species, three distinct consequences

Protons in a solar particle event deposit dose and displacement damage across the whole exposed volume, and cause single-event effects indirectly through nuclear reactions. Alpha particles behave similarly with higher linear energy transfer. Heavy ions cause single-event effects directly and are the only component capable of the destructive modes. A design margin computed from proton fluence alone does not bound the heavy-ion risk, and a heavy-ion test alone does not bound the accumulated dose. Both are required.

Event magnitudes are statistical rather than deterministic. The distribution of event sizes follows a power law over most of its range, with a rapid falloff for the very largest events. That shape means small events are frequent and dominate the count while rare large events dominate the accumulated fluence, so a mission is designed against a probability of exceedance rather than against a typical event. The October 1989 event serves as the conventional worst case in CREME96.

4 · The solar activity cycle

All three populations vary with solar activity, and they do not vary in phase. Understanding the cycle is what allows a mission to be planned against the correct worst case, and it is the reason a single "worst case" does not exist.

The Sun's activity varies with a period of approximately 11 years. At the end of each 11-year cycle the magnetic polarity of the Sun reverses, so the full magnetic cycle is approximately 22 years, with intensity peaks every 11 years. For design purposes the cycle is conventionally divided into about 7 years of solar maximum and about 4 years of solar minimum, although the real transition is continuous. Activity is tracked by two indicators: the sunspot number, whose record extends back to the 1600s with cycles numbered from 1749, and the solar radio flux at 10.7 cm wavelength, denoted F10.7, recorded since 1947.

Trapped proton flux at low altitude is therefore anti-correlated with F10.7, with a phase lag that depends on the shell being observed. A mission that must survive both the galactic cosmic ray heavy-ion environment and the solar proton fluence is designed against solar minimum for one and solar maximum for the other. Long-term forecasting of cycle amplitude remains unreliable, which is why environment models for design are probabilistic and return a spectrum at a stated confidence level rather than a prediction.

5 · The geomagnetic field

Everything from here on depends on the Earth's magnetic field, so it is worth establishing precisely what that field is and how a charged particle behaves in it.

A charged particle moving in a magnetic field experiences the Lorentz force. The force is perpendicular to both the velocity and the field, so it does no work: the particle's speed and kinetic energy are unchanged and only its direction is altered. A particle moving exactly along the field feels no force at all. A particle moving exactly across the field is bent into a circle. The general case is the sum of the two: uniform motion along the field combined with circular motion around it, tracing a helix whose axis is the field line.

The Earth's field has two parts. The internal, or geomagnetic, field originates within the Earth and is approximately a dipole out to about 4 or 5 Earth radii. The dipole axis is tilted about 11.5° from the geographic rotation axis, and its origin is displaced more than 500 km from the Earth's centre. The displacement is what produces the South Atlantic Anomaly; the tilt only fixes where on the globe the resulting feature falls. The 11.5° figure is the one the course's references carry, and it is a mid-twentieth-century value: the current International Geomagnetic Reference Field puts the tilt nearer 9.4°. The difference changes no argument here, but it is worth noticing in a section that is itself about the field changing. The external field is the distortion imposed by the solar wind. The wind compresses the field on the sunward side, where the magnetosphere terminates at roughly 10 Earth radii under moderate conditions and can be pushed inward to about 6 Earth radii during a magnetic storm. On the anti-sunward side the wind drags the field into a tail that may extend 1,000 Earth radii.

Because the trapped populations organize themselves around the field rather than around geography, they are mapped in a coordinate system built from the field itself: McIlwain's (B, L) coordinates. L labels a field line by the distance, in Earth radii, at which that line crosses the magnetic equator. B is the local magnetic field strength, which is minimum at the magnetic equator and increases toward the poles, so it says where along the line a point sits. A single (B, L) pair therefore locates a point within the magnetic field, and the flux of trapped particles is a function of that pair alone. Two spacecraft at very different latitudes, longitudes and altitudes see the same trapped environment if they share the same (B, L).

6 · How magnetic trapping works

A trapped particle is one whose motion is confined to a bounded region of the magnetosphere indefinitely, or at least for far longer than the time it would take to cross that region in a straight line. Confinement is produced by three superimposed motions on three very different timescales: a fast gyration about the field line, a slower bounce along it between reflection points, and a slower still drift around the Earth. The separation of the timescales is what makes the motion regular and predictable.

The gyration is the helical motion of equation (2.1) and requires no further explanation. The bounce is the essential part. As the particle moves along a field line away from the magnetic equator, the field strength it experiences rises according to equation (2.2). The magnetic force does no work, so the total kinetic energy — and therefore the total momentum — is fixed. What is also fixed, provided the field changes little over one gyration, is the magnetic moment of the gyrating charge, the first adiabatic invariant. Holding the magnetic moment constant while B increases forces the perpendicular momentum up, and because the total is fixed, the parallel momentum must fall to compensate. Where the parallel momentum reaches zero the particle has stopped advancing along the field line, and the force turns it around. It then travels to the conjugate point in the opposite hemisphere and reflects again.

Holds while the field changes little over one gyroradius and one gyroperiod, which is well satisfied in the belts. The mirror condition needs two separate facts: the constancy of μ, which involves only the perpendicular motion, and the constancy of p, which follows from the magnetic force doing no work.

Equation (2.3) also decides which particles are not trapped. A particle whose equatorial pitch angle is small mirrors only at a field strength that is reached below the top of the atmosphere. It never arrives at its mirror point; it collides with atmospheric constituents and is lost. The set of small equatorial pitch angles for which this happens is the loss cone, and it is why the belts have a sharp lower edge near 200 km altitude and why any process that scatters a particle's pitch angle toward the field direction removes it from the belt.

The third motion arises because the field is not uniform across one gyration orbit: it is stronger on the side of the orbit nearer the Earth. The gyroradius is therefore smaller on that side than on the far side, the circle does not close, and the guiding centre creeps sideways. The direction of that creep depends on the sense of gyration, which depends on the sign of the charge, so protons drift westward and electrons drift eastward. After a complete circuit the particle has traced a closed toroidal surface, the drift shell — the surface labelled by L. The drift period depends on energy and shell — about 16 seconds for the 100 MeV proton used above, but about 2.6 minutes for a 10 MeV proton on the same shell and about 16 minutes for a 1 MeV electron at L = 4. In every case it is long compared with the bounce, so a spacecraft sampling the environment at one longitude sees a population that has already been averaged around the Earth.

Trapping needs a supply and a sink

The three motions explain why a particle already inside the field stays there. They do not explain why there is a population at all. A steady belt requires a source that injects particles onto trapped orbits and a loss process that removes them, balancing at some equilibrium intensity. The inner proton belt is supplied by neutron decay, described in §7. The outer electron belt is supplied by injection and acceleration during geomagnetic storms. Losses are dominated by scattering into the loss cone, principally through collisions with the upper atmosphere and through interaction with plasma waves. When the balance is disturbed — by a storm, or by an artificial injection such as Starfish Prime — the belt takes months to years to return to its previous state.

7 · What is trapped, and where

Two populations occupy the same region, and they are not arranged the same way. The protons form a single continuous belt with no gap in it, densest close to the Earth. The electrons form two zones separated by a slot in which the flux falls to a local minimum. The familiar three-part picture — inner zone, slot, outer zone — is the electron structure, and applying it to protons is what makes the published L ranges look contradictory. The belts were first detected by Explorer I in 1958, whose Geiger counter returned count rates that saturated at altitude.

The proton belt begins at an inner edge near L = 1.14, set by the top of the atmosphere, and extends outward past geosynchronous orbit to about L = 10, thinning and softening as it goes. Its energetic part is confined: protons above 10 MeV are found only below about 20,000 km, and the course quotes the belt's damaging extent as altitudes of roughly 1,000 km to 12,000 km. The flux of protons above 10 MeV peaks near L = 1.7 at slightly more than 105 particles per square centimetre per second. Energies reach several hundred MeV — the course slide quotes protons in excess of 400 MeV, and the 2018 short course puts the tail in the GeV range. This population is stable over years: its losses are slow, by Coulomb scattering and charge exchange with the residual neutral atmosphere and by gradual radial diffusion, so lifetimes at low L run to years or decades. It is the dominant total-dose source for low Earth orbit.

The electron zones are two. The inner zone runs from L = 1 to about L = 2. The outer zone runs from about L = 3 out to L = 10, with energies generally below 10 MeV; the course quotes a maximum trapped electron energy near 7 MeV, and the slides put outer-zone fluxes about ten times those of the inner zone, though the inner-zone figure is itself uncertain for reasons given below. Outer-zone flux above 1 MeV peaks between L = 4.0 and 4.5, with a long-term average near 3×106 per square centimetre per second. It is extremely dynamic: one-day averaged differential fluxes at 1 MeV measured before and after a geomagnetic storm differ by about three orders of magnitude over nine days. A long-term average therefore describes the outer zone's climate but not its weather, and the standard long-term model should not be applied over intervals shorter than about six months.

The slot, near L = 2 to 3, separates the two electron zones. It is a local minimum in electron flux during quiet periods; storms inject electrons into it and they decay away with a time constant near ten days. Its low quiet-time flux and its wide ground coverage compared with low Earth orbit make it attractive for some missions, but its variability is severe. There is no corresponding gap in the proton belt, which passes straight through this range of shells.

Altitude and L are measured from different origins

L, and every distance quoted in Earth radii, is measured from the centre of the Earth. Altitudes are measured from the surface. The course slide gives the outer belt as "~3–10 Earth radii (~13,000–64,000 km)", and the two ends of that kilometre range are quoted on different baselines: 13,000 km is the altitude corresponding to L = 3, while 64,000 km is the geocentric distance corresponding to L = 10, whose altitude is 57,400 km. The inner-belt figures on the same slide, 1,000 to 12,000 km, are both altitudes and convert cleanly to L = 1.16 and L = 2.88. One line of that table deserves to be said explicitly. The GPS constellation crosses the magnetic equator at L = 4.2, which is the peak of the outer electron belt. Equation (2.4) holds only at the equator, and the orbits are inclined 55°, so a GPS satellite sweeps out to L ≈ 12 near the extremes of its latitude range and never reaches shells below about L = 4. It therefore crosses the belt's peak four times a day, every day, for a design life measured in decades. That geometry sets the total-dose requirement for navigation payloads and explains why they are built with hardened parts and extensive redundancy.

The source of the inner proton belt is neutron decay. Galactic cosmic rays strike the upper atmosphere and produce showers of secondaries, among them neutrons. Some of those neutrons travel upward out of the atmosphere. A free neutron is unstable, with a mean lifetime of about 880 seconds, and decays into a proton, an electron and an antineutrino. A neutron that decays while inside the trapping region leaves a proton carrying almost the neutron's kinetic energy, and if that proton's pitch angle places it outside the loss cone it is trapped. This is the Cosmic Ray Albedo Neutron Decay process. Because its input is the galactic cosmic ray flux, it delivers more protons at solar minimum, reinforcing the anti-correlation between trapped proton flux and solar activity that atmospheric expansion already produces.

The belt structure is not static either. During the Combined Release and Radiation Effects Satellite mission, a large geomagnetic storm in March 1991 produced a second proton belt between L = 2 and 3, most pronounced in the 20 to 70 MeV range. Its flux began to decay immediately, but CRRES still saw it six months later when the mission was lost, and it remained measurable on METEOSAT for about two years. Xapsos' 2006 short course calls this belt stable and the 2018 edition calls it transient; the observation is the same, and the disagreement is only about which timescale to compare it against. A separate observation by the Van Allen Probes in 2012 identified a transient third electron belt that persisted for about four weeks. Both make the same point: the three-zone structure is the quiet-time average, and a single event can add a zone that then persists on its own timescale.

One further caution about the inner zone. Older data reported significant fluxes of electrons above about 1.5 MeV there, and the long-standing models include them. Measurements from the Van Allen Probes, which for the first time could subtract the detector background produced by energetic protons, found essentially no such electrons in the inner zone after 2012. Some of the older data were proton contamination, and the remainder may require extreme storms that have not occurred recently. The practical consequence is modest — for a Hubble-like low Earth orbit behind 2.5 mm of aluminium, electrons contribute under 20% of the total dose with the older model and under 2% with the newer one — but it illustrates clearly that environment models are measurements subject to revision, not physical constants.

8 · The South Atlantic Anomaly

For any spacecraft below about 1,000 km, one geographic feature dominates the trapped-particle exposure. Its cause is entirely geometric.

The trapped populations are organized around the magnetic dipole, but a spacecraft's altitude is measured from the Earth's surface, and the two centres do not coincide: the dipole centre is displaced more than 500 km from the geocentre. A given drift shell therefore sits at a constant distance from the dipole centre but at a varying altitude above the ground, and on one side it comes much closer to the surface than on the other. The displacement alone produces that asymmetry; a tilted dipole centred on the geocentre would put the belt at the same altitude at every longitude. What the 11.5° tilt does is fix where the feature lands. That region lies over the South Atlantic, south and east of Brazil, and there the inner proton belt reaches down into the altitudes that low Earth orbits occupy. A spacecraft at a fixed 500 km altitude flies below the belt for most of its orbit and through the belt's lower fringe when it crosses this region.

The consequence for a low Earth orbit is that exposure is not uniform in time. A spacecraft accumulates most of its trapped-proton dose and observes most of its single-event upsets during the small fraction of each orbit spent inside the anomaly. On-orbit event data show exactly this: a ten-minute record of upsets taken during anomaly crossings is dense, while a forty-eight-hour record away from it holds only a few scattered events. Because the anomaly is fixed in geographic coordinates and the orbit is predictable, this is one of the few radiation hazards that can be scheduled around, and sensitive instruments are commonly placed in a safe state for the crossing.

The anomaly is also moving. The geomagnetic dipole centre drifts away from the geocentre at about 2.5 km per year and the dipole moment is decreasing. The net effect is that the anomaly drifts westward, broadens, and rises in peak flux. A model built on 1960s data therefore places it in the wrong location for a mission flying today, which is one of the main reasons the older trapped-proton models have been superseded.

9 · Orbit determines exposure

The populations of §§2, 3 and 7 do not reach all orbits equally. Two effects select what arrives: geometry, which decides which parts of the trapped structure an orbit passes through, and geomagnetic filtering, which decides how much of the external populations penetrates.

Low Earth orbit, from 0 to 2,000 km, is the most benign of the common orbits. The geomagnetic field shields it effectively from solar protons at low inclination, and below about 1,000 km it lies under the equatorial belts altogether. Its trapped-particle exposure comes from South Atlantic Anomaly crossings and, at higher inclination, from the outer electron belt where that belt reaches low altitude at high latitude. Total dose is moderate.

Polar and sun-synchronous low Earth orbits receive more. A high-inclination orbit crosses the full range of drift shells every revolution, so it collects the high-latitude portion of the outer electron belt on each pass, and the polar regions are where the geomagnetic field admits external particles most freely, so solar protons reach the spacecraft during events.

Medium Earth orbit, which includes the 31-satellite GPS constellation in circular orbits at 20,200 km, crosses the peak of the outer electron belt on every revolution, and eccentric transfers through it pass the slot region. It is the harshest of the routine Earth orbits for trapped electrons.

Geostationary orbit, at 35,800 km, lies in the outer part of the electron belt and beyond effective geomagnetic shielding, so it receives solar protons unattenuated and galactic cosmic rays essentially unattenuated. Trapped protons are present there but at low energy, and light shielding removes them: typical spacecraft shielding stops protons below about 10 MeV, which is why trapped protons at altitudes greater than 3.8 Earth radii — the slide's wording, so L ≈ 4.8 — are normally ineffective at producing damage. This is one place where the course quotes Earth radii as an altitude rather than a geocentric distance, so convert before combining it with an L value.

Geomagnetic filtering is quantified by rigidity. A particle's resistance to being deflected depends on its momentum per unit charge rather than on its energy alone, so that combination is the quantity the field responds to.

R = pcZe      rg = RcB
(2.5)
R magnetic rigidity, conventionally quoted in volts and usually in GV · p relativistic momentum, most easily handled as pc in MeV from (pc)² = T² + 2T m0c², with T the kinetic energy and m0c² the rest energy, 938.3 MeV for a proton · Z charge number, dimensionless · e elementary charge, 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ C · c speed of light (m/s) · B field (T) · rg gyroradius (m) for a particle moving perpendicular to the field, the same quantity as in (2.1) rewritten in terms of rigidity. A 100 MeV proton has R = 0.445 GV and a 1 GeV proton R = 1.70 GV. Two particles of equal rigidity follow the same trajectory in the same field whatever their mass or species.

A particle reaches a given point in the magnetosphere only if its rigidity exceeds the local cutoff. The cutoff depends strongly on magnetic latitude, because near the equator the field lines lie parallel to the surface and an incoming particle must cross them, whereas near the poles the field lines point toward the surface and an incoming particle can travel along them.

Rc ≈ 14.9 · cos⁴λ(r / RE)²   GV
(2.6)
Rc Störmer vertical cutoff rigidity for a positively charged particle arriving vertically (GV) · λ magnetic latitude · r geocentric distance · RE Earth radius, so the denominator is dimensionless and equals 1 at the surface. The constant 14.9 GV is set by the Earth's present dipole moment and decreases slowly as that moment weakens. Valid for a pure dipole and vertical incidence; real cutoffs depend on arrival direction and are lowered during geomagnetic storms, which is why disturbed conditions admit particles that quiet conditions exclude.

Equation (2.6) accounts for a set of facts that would otherwise have to be remembered separately. Equatorial low Earth orbits are shielded from solar protons because those protons have rigidities far below the equatorial cutoff. Polar orbits are not, because the cutoff there falls to near zero. Geostationary orbit has a formal dipole cutoff of only 14.9/6.61² = 0.34 GV, which galactic cosmic rays exceed by an order of magnitude and pass undiminished; solar protons do not exceed it, and they reach geostationary orbit anyway because the field at 6.6 Earth radii is no longer dipolar and the magnetopause is close, so equation (2.6) has stopped describing the situation. And a geomagnetic storm, which distorts the field and lowers cutoffs, briefly admits particles to orbits that normally exclude them, so a storm raises the single-event rate in low Earth orbit by changing the filter as well as by changing the source.

10 · Planetary belts beyond the Earth

Magnetic trapping is a property of any magnetized planet with a supply of charged particles, and the Earth's belts are not the most severe example. Jupiter has the strongest planetary magnetic field in the solar system and the most intense radiation belts. Its inner belts are further supplied by its moon Io, whose volcanic activity feeds a torus of plasma into the magnetosphere that is then accelerated. The result is an electron environment far more intense, and far harder in energy, than anything near the Earth.

This is the design driver for missions to Europa, which orbits within Jupiter's radiation belts — well outside the inner belt, but in a severe electron environment nonetheless. The course quotes a total ionizing dose of 2.8 Mrad for the Europa Clipper spacecraft. That figure is a mission total rather than an annual rate, and the slide gives it without a shielding depth; a total ionizing dose is not a specification until the depth behind which it is accumulated is stated, because the number changes by more than an order of magnitude across the range of thicknesses a spacecraft actually uses. At that level no realistic uniform shielding suffices. The mission response is to place the vulnerable electronics inside a dedicated shielded vault, to select parts qualified well beyond the requirement, and to design the trajectory so that time spent in the highest-flux region is minimized. M03 §5 develops why shielding saturates, and M15 §2 covers how a total-dose requirement is demonstrated by test.

11 · From environment to numbers: models

A design requires a spectrum: particles per square centimetre per second as a function of energy and species, for a specific orbit over a specific interval. That spectrum cannot be measured at the orbit before the mission flies, and it varies over the solar cycle and from storm to storm. Environment models fill the gap. They are empirical constructions built from decades of satellite measurements, and knowing what data underlies each one is part of using it correctly.

A trapped-particle calculation runs in a fixed sequence. An orbit generator produces latitude, longitude and altitude against time. Those geographic coordinates are transformed into the magnetic coordinates (B, L) in which the particle population is mapped. The model returns the flux at each point, and integrating along the trajectory gives the orbit-averaged or peak spectrum outside the spacecraft. Only then is transport through the structure computed, to give the spectrum and dose at the device.

What a Monte Carlo calculation is

Particle interaction with matter is probabilistic: a given proton entering a given slab has a probability distribution over how far it travels, whether it undergoes a nuclear reaction, and what fragments emerge. For all but trivial geometries there is no closed-form solution for the resulting energy deposition. A Monte Carlo calculation instead simulates individual particle histories one at a time, drawing each step and each interaction at random from the governing probability distributions, and accumulates statistics over a very large number of histories. The result converges as roughly the inverse square root of the number of histories, so accuracy is bought with computer time. That is why detailed transport is Geant4's principal advantage, and why rare events — a nuclear reaction producing an energetic recoil inside a small sensitive volume — need either enormous numbers of histories or specialized variance-reduction techniques.

Trapped particles. The long-standing standard models are AP-8 for protons and AE-8 for electrons: static flux maps for solar maximum and solar minimum, built on data from the 1960s and 1970s. They give long-term mean values, and because they are means, designs using them apply a radiation design margin. Their known weaknesses — obsolete data, no account of the secular drift of the field, and a discontinuous jump between solar maximum and minimum — drove a generation of replacements including PSB97, LATRM, CRRESPRO and TPM-1. The current comprehensive models are AP9 and AE9, now called IRENE, built on data from 1976 to 2016. Their important change is methodological: alongside the mean they return the environment at a stated percentile, and they add a perturbed mode carrying measurement uncertainty and a Monte Carlo mode carrying space-weather variation, so a designer chooses a confidence level explicitly instead of applying an arbitrary margin. Model disagreement is not small — AP-8 and TPM-1 differ substantially in spectral hardness for a space-station-like orbit — so the model used is part of the requirement, not an implementation detail.

Galactic cosmic rays. Adams' Cosmic Ray Effects on Micro-Electronics code began this line of work, and its current version, CREME96, is hosted at Vanderbilt and uses the Moscow State University model of galactic cosmic ray flux. The other widely used model is Badhwar–O'Neill, developed at NASA Johnson Space Center. Both start from an assumed local interstellar spectrum outside the heliosphere and apply diffusion–convection theory to describe how solar modulation transports it inward to 1 AU; they differ in how the modulation level is tied to observed solar activity. CREME96 also produces the integral linear-energy-transfer spectra used directly for upset-rate prediction, which is why it is the tool named when cosmic-ray upset rates are being estimated.

Solar particle events. Because events are stochastic, these models are explicitly probabilistic and return a fluence at a chosen confidence level for a chosen mission duration. King's analysis of solar cycle 20, dominated by the August 1972 event, was the first. The JPL model established that event magnitudes form a continuous distribution from small events to the largest. NASA's ESP and PSYCHIC models apply maximum entropy theory and extreme value statistics, and the European Space Agency's SAPPHIRE model uses a different waiting-time distribution.

Transport. Once the external spectrum exists, Geant4 — a general-purpose Monte Carlo toolkit for transporting particles through matter — propagates it through the spacecraft structure and into the device. MRED, developed at Vanderbilt, is built on Geant4 and specialized for energy deposition in microelectronic geometries. Both return in M04 §3 and §5, for heavy-ion test planning and dose in a gate stack, and in M11 §11.

Where this leaves us

Three natural populations reach near-Earth space, and they differ in more than name. Galactic cosmic rays are a low, steady, isotropic flux of fully ionized nuclei at energies near 1 GeV per nucleon that cannot practically be shielded, and whose small heavy-ion component sets the single-event requirement. Solar particle events are episodic, proton-dominated bursts that raise the flux by four orders of magnitude for hours to days, delivering accumulated dose and displacement damage on a schedule that is statistical rather than predictable. The trapped belts are a resident population held by the geomagnetic field: protons in the inner zone, supplied by neutron decay and stable over years, and electrons in the outer zone, injected by storms and variable by orders of magnitude within days.

The geomagnetic field is the organizing principle. Three superimposed motions — gyration, bounce between mirror points, and azimuthal drift — confine particles onto drift shells labelled by L, and the same field excludes external particles whose rigidity falls below the local cutoff. Every consequence in this module follows from those two facts: the three-zone structure of the belts, the existence of the South Atlantic Anomaly and the episodic exposure it produces in low Earth orbit, the admission of solar protons to polar orbits and their exclusion from equatorial ones, and the coincidence of the GPS altitude with the harshest trapped electron environment.

What remains is conversion. A flux spectrum is not yet a dose, a shielding thickness or an upset rate. M03 converts environment into absorbed dose, shows why adding shielding stops helping, and follows these populations into the atmosphere, where the cascade they produce dominates avionics. M04 supplies the energy-deposition physics that turns any of these spectra into charge in a device, and M11 §8 turns that charge into an error rate.

Quick reference

  • The three natural populations are galactic cosmic rays, solar particle events and the trapped belts. The atmospheric environment is secondary to these; accelerators, reactors and weapon environments are the man-made ones.
  • GCR: ~89% protons, ~9% helium, 1–2% heavier ions and electrons; flux peaks at 100–1000 MeV/nucleon and extends to ~10²⁰ eV; total flux 1–10 cm⁻²s⁻¹. Effectively unshieldable; in Earth orbit its small heavy-ion component sets the single-event requirement and little else. Highest at solar minimum.
  • Solar particle events: 90–95% protons; peak flux above 10 MeV can exceed 5×10⁵ cm⁻²s⁻¹, reaching ~10⁶ in the largest events; a large event raises proton flux by ~10⁴ and heavy-ion flux to ~50% of the GCR background. Cluster at solar maximum. October 1989 is the CREME96 worst case.
  • The solar cycle is ~11 years in intensity and ~22 years in magnetic polarity, tracked by sunspot number and F10.7. No single phase is worst for every population.
  • Trapping is gyration (ms) + bounce between mirror points (a few tenths of a second) + drift around the Earth (tens of seconds to tens of minutes, falling with energy; protons west, electrons east). Mirroring follows from constant μ = p⊥²/2mB at constant total momentum; particles inside the loss cone are absorbed by the atmosphere.
  • The two species are arranged differently. Protons: one continuous belt from L = 1.14 outward with no slot, energies to several hundred MeV, peak >10 MeV flux ~10⁵ cm⁻²s⁻¹ at L ≈ 1.7, stable over years. Electrons: inner zone L = 1–2, slot L ≈ 2–3, outer zone L = 3–10 to ~7–10 MeV, ~3×10⁶ cm⁻²s⁻¹ above 1 MeV at L = 4–4.5, variable by orders of magnitude.
  • The inner proton belt is fed by CRAND: cosmic rays make atmospheric neutrons, which escape upward and decay (mean life ≈ 880 s) into protons that the field traps.
  • The South Atlantic Anomaly exists because the dipole is tilted 11.5° and offset more than 500 km, bringing the belt to low altitude over the South Atlantic. It dominates exposure below ~1,000 km and drifts westward as the field changes.
  • Orbits: LEO to 2,000 km, anomaly-dominated; GPS at 20,200 km, crossing the equator at L = 4.2 — the outer electron belt peak — and sweeping to L ≈ 12 at 55° inclination; GEO at 35,800 km, L = 6.6, with unattenuated solar protons and GCR. 1 RE = 6,380 km and L = 1 + heq/RE.
  • Rigidity R = pc/Ze decides penetration. The vertical cutoff falls as cos⁴λ from ~14.9 GV at the equator to near zero at the poles, so inclination controls access to the external populations.
  • Models: AP9/AE9 (IRENE) for trapped particles, CREME96 and Badhwar–O'Neill for GCR, ESP/PSYCHIC and SAPPHIRE for solar protons, Geant4 and MRED for transport. The environment models are empirical and statistical; only Geant4 and MRED sample individual particle histories through matter. Keep the environment calculation and the transport calculation distinct.
