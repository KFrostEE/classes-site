Module 09

Oxide Charge in Time, Temperature, Field and Bias

How fast, how much and under what conditions: the measured kinetics of hole transport, trapped-hole neutralization and interface-trap buildup, their dependence on temperature, oxide field, bias polarity and oxide thickness, and the two results — one response curve across eleven decades of dose rate, and an interface-trap anneal that needs more than 125 °C — on which total-dose testing of MOS parts rests.

Prerequisites M06, M07, M08 Read time ~45 min Sources Deck 06; Deck 02 pp. 5–9, 15; Deck 04 p. 22; Lecture 7

M07 set out what happens inside an irradiated oxide: electron–hole pairs form, a field-dependent fraction of the holes escapes recombination, the survivors hop toward an interface, some are captured at oxygen vacancies near the silicon, and hydrogen released along the way creates interface traps. Each step was stated with its qualitative dependences — on field, thickness, temperature and time. This module supplies the numbers. It measures how long each step takes, how the rate of each responds when the oxide is heated, cooled, thinned or biased the other way, and how much charge survives each of those conditions.

The organizing fact is that the steps run on clocks separated by many orders of magnitude, and that each clock responds to temperature and field in its own way. Electrons leave the oxide in picoseconds. Holes cross it in microseconds to milliseconds at room temperature and over far longer times when it is cold. Hydrogen ions reach the interface over seconds to about a day. Trapped holes are neutralized over the entire lifetime of a part, and interface traps, once formed, anneal only above about 125 °C. Heating, cooling, thinning or re-biasing an oxide moves each of these clocks by a different amount, so the same dose can leave a transistor shifted negative, unchanged or shifted positive, depending only on when and under what conditions it is measured. That is the physical content of the rebound of M08 §3, and it is the reason every total-dose test procedure specifies a bias, a temperature and a schedule as well as a dose.

1 · The irradiated oxide on one time axis

The clearest way to see the whole sequence is to deliver the dose in a single short pulse and watch the threshold of a transistor afterwards. A pulse separates generation, which is over when the pulse ends, from everything that follows it. Deck 06 p. 27 draws the result, after Benedetto et al. and Oldham, on a time axis running from 10−6 to 108 s — from a microsecond to about three years — and divides it into four stages. Each stage is a different step of the chain in M07 §1, dominating the response over a different window of time.

Stage 1, the initial hole yield. By the end of a microsecond the electrons generated in the oxide have been swept out — their mobility is six to eight orders of magnitude higher than that of the holes — and recombination is over. The shift at this instant reports every hole that escaped recombination, distributed through the oxide where it was generated. Its magnitude is set by the dose, the thickness and the charge yield of M07 §2.

Stage 2, hole transport, or short-term recovery. The holes hop toward the interface that the field points them at. Most of them reach it and are removed there, at the silicon or at the gate electrode; only the fraction captured at deep traps on the way stays behind. The shift therefore recovers, and at room temperature most of the recovery is complete within milliseconds. It is slower in a thick oxide, at low field and at low temperature, by amounts §2 quantifies.

Stage 3, trapped-hole annealing, or long-term recovery. What remains after transport is the oxide-trap charge ΔNot. It is not permanent: electrons tunnel into the traps from the silicon, and trapped holes are thermally emitted, at rates that fall steeply with a trap's depth in the oxide and its depth in energy. The shift decays roughly in proportion to the logarithm of time, a decade of time removing about the same increment of charge as the decade before it, and on the slide it is still decaying at 108 s. §5 derives this behaviour.

Stage 4, interface-trap buildup. Protons released near the interfaces drift to the Si/SiO₂ interface and depassivate Si–H bonds, creating interface traps. In an n-channel transistor at threshold these are negatively charged, so the shift they add is positive and opposes the trapped holes. Buildup begins within a second, is largely complete by 104–105 s, and in some oxides resumes near 106 s as latent buildup (§8). The accent curve in Figure 1 carries both contributions; it crosses zero and settles positive, which is rebound.

The figure also makes precise a statement that recurs throughout total-dose work. The total positive charge in the oxide is greatest at the end of an exposure, because generation stops then and every later process only removes charge. The deeply trapped part stops growing once the last holes have finished their transport, and from then on it can only be neutralized — so for any exposure lasting longer than the hole transit time, which is every practical irradiation at room temperature, oxide-trap charge is at its maximum immediately after irradiation. Interface-trap charge is at its minimum at that same moment. Two populations that change in opposite directions from the end of an exposure make time after irradiation a variable on the same footing as dose.

The four stages overlap in time but not in cause, and each responds to a different control. Transport depends on field and temperature; trapped-hole annealing on trap depth, temperature and bias history; interface-trap buildup on hydrogen, bias polarity and temperature. A measurement made at one time after an exposure samples the sum at that one time. It cannot be converted to any other time, or to any other temperature or bias, without knowing which stage dominates there — which is what the rest of this module supplies.

2 · Hole transport, quantitatively

M07 §5 established how a hole moves in amorphous SiO₂: as a small polaron, by thermally assisted hops between localized sites whose waiting times are spread over many orders of magnitude, so that a packet of holes released together arrives over many decades of time. This section measures how fast that transport is, and how its speed changes with temperature, field and thickness. The measurements are Boesch's, on wet-process oxides pulsed with radiation and watched afterwards, and they appear on Deck 06 pp. 20, 21 and 23 (Deck 05 pp. 36, 37 and 39).

The quantity plotted in all three is the flat-band shift at time t after the pulse, divided by the shift at the end of the pulse, ΔVfb(t)/ΔVfb(0+). The normalization is the point of the experiment. Numerator and denominator come from the same pulse in the same oxide, so the dose and the charge yield cancel, and the ratio reports only how much of the initial hole population is still in the oxide. The shift falls because holes arriving at the silicon are removed there; these wet oxides trap little of what arrives, so the ratio falls nearly to zero once transport is complete.

Temperature

Deck 06 p. 20 repeats the measurement on a 95 nm wet oxide at eight temperatures from 293 K down to 124 K. At 293 K about 60% of the initial shift is gone by the first reading, less than a millisecond after the pulse, and essentially all of it by 10 s. Each successive cooling step in the series delays the recovery by one to two decades of time, and at 124 K about 85% of the initial shift is still present after 103 s. Every curve, at every temperature, stretches over many decades rather than completing within one, which is the dispersive signature of hopping transport. The slowing on cooling is the thermal activation of each hop: the lattice distortion that binds the hole must be overcome at every step, and the rate at which that happens falls steeply with temperature.

Field

Deck 06 p. 21 holds the temperature fixed and varies the oxide field from 3 to 6 MV/cm. Higher field speeds transport, and far more steeply than a field-independent mobility would allow. Half of the initial shift has recovered about 0.02 s after the pulse at 6 MV/cm, about 1 s after it at 5 MV/cm, and only after several hundred seconds at 4 MV/cm. At 6 MV/cm about 93% of the shift has recovered by 103 s; at 3 MV/cm, about 20%. A factor of 1.5 in field, from 4 to 6 MV/cm, therefore shortens the half-recovery time by about four decades, where drift at a constant mobility would shorten it by a factor of 1.5. The field changes the hop rate itself, not merely the direction the hops accumulate in.

That makes three separate field dependences acting on three separate steps, and they point in different directions. The charge yield of M07 §2 rises with field, because a stronger field separates a pair before it can recombine. The transport speed of this section rises with field, so the holes reach the interface sooner. And the fraction of arriving holes that is captured near the interface falls roughly as Eox−1/2 (M07 §4), because the capture cross-section of the trap falls with field. The net trapped charge is the product of the first and the third, which is why it passes through a maximum near 1–2 MV/cm. Transport speed does not enter that product: it decides when the holes arrive, not how many are captured.

Deck 06 p. 22 (Deck 05 p. 38) is headed "Hole transport is E-field dependent", repeating the heading of p. 21, but its plot is the 45 nm poly-gate, 500 krad(SiO₂), 10 keV X-ray measurement of p. 18: the yield-adjusted oxide-trap shift falling as E−1/2, which the slide's own bullet attributes to the hole capture cross-section near the Si/SiO₂ interface. That is a trapping result — the one M07 §4 develops — and not a transport one. Pages 22 and 23 also carry the caption "Wet-process oxide with thickness of 95 nm", which describes the oxide of pp. 20–21: the p. 22 plot is of a 45 nm oxide, and p. 23 shows four oxides from 21.8 to 96.3 nm. Read p. 22 as the field dependence of trapping and p. 23 as a thickness series.

Thickness

Deck 06 p. 23 varies the oxide thickness. Its recovery curves for oxides of 21.8, 37.6, 56.6 and 96.3 nm show the thinner oxide recovering first: ten milliseconds after the pulse the 21.8 nm oxide has lost about three-quarters of its initial shift, the 96.3 nm oxide about a third. The same slide plots the transit time against thickness on logarithmic axes for two series of oxides, and both are straight lines of slope close to four — 4.0 for as-grown oxides measured at 220 K and 1 MV/cm, 4.2 for etched-back oxides at 79 K and 5 MV/cm. Doubling the thickness lengthens the transit time about sixteenfold.

The two series answer different questions. An as-grown series consists of oxides each grown to its final thickness, so the thinner ones also spent less time in the furnace and may differ in structure. An etched-back series thins a single thick oxide chemically to several thicknesses, so every sample shares one growth history and thickness is the only variable. Both give a slope of four, under temperatures and fields that differ by factors of nearly three and five, so the tox⁴ law is a property of the transport rather than of the growth. Ordinary drift at constant field would give a transit time proportional to tox, so a fourth power is the quantitative signature of dispersive hopping that M07 §5 describes. This transit-time law is distinct from the tox² law of the threshold shift in M07 §3, which comes from geometry and capacitance and applies once transport is over.

These dependences decide where transport matters in practice. In a thin gate oxide at room temperature and operating field, holes clear the oxide within milliseconds at most and transport is invisible to any measurement made later. Three conditions stretch it out. A thick oxide, such as the field and trench isolation of M08 §5, lengthens it as the fourth power of thickness. A low field, such as the fringing field in an oxide with no electrode over it, slows it by decades. And cooling slows it by a decade or more for every few tens of kelvin (§9). In each case holes remain in transit long after the exposure, the oxide holds positive charge a room-temperature gate oxide would already have cleared, and the hydrogen that holes release near the interface arrives late, which delays interface-trap buildup as well (§8).

3 · Bias during and after irradiation

The voltage on the gate during an exposure sets the oxide field, and the field has both a magnitude and a direction. Its magnitude acts on the yield, the transport and the capture of §2. Its direction decides which interface the holes travel to, and therefore where the survivors end up trapped, and it decides whether the hydrogen ions they release drift toward the silicon or away from it. After the exposure the bias goes on acting on everything that is still moving. Deck 06 p. 8 (Deck 05 p. 24) poses the question of whether bias needs to be considered at all; the measurements below show it matters more than any other operating condition.

It is worth stating what bias competes with. The amount of oxide-trap charge a dose produces is set by the oxide thickness, the field during irradiation, the temperature, the time since the exposure and the density of hole traps the process built in, and Deck 06 p. 48 names field, thickness and bias as its dependences. Substrate doping is not among them. It fixes the Fermi potential and the depletion charge, which set the threshold voltage before and after irradiation alike (M08 §1); it alters the oxide field at a given gate voltage only through the surface potential and the gate–substrate work-function difference, together a correction of less than a volt beside gate biases of several volts; and it takes no part in generating, transporting or trapping holes in the oxide.

Polarity during irradiation

Under positive gate bias the field drives electrons to the gate and holes to the silicon. The holes are captured, if at all, close to the Si/SiO₂ interface, in the oxygen-deficient transition region where the vacancy precursors concentrate. By the centroid relation of M06 (6.2), charge at x̄ ≈ tox shifts the flat-band voltage by the full −Qox/Cox. The protons released near the interface drift the same way and reach the silicon, where they create interface traps. Every mechanism of M07 acts at full strength.

Under negative gate bias the holes travel to the gate. Whatever is trapped there sits at x̄ ≪ tox, so by (6.2) it moves the flat-band voltage by only a small fraction of Qox/Cox, and the protons are driven away from the silicon, so few interface traps form there. At zero bias the only field is the built-in one set by the gate–substrate work-function difference: the yield is low because little separates the pairs, transport is slow, and the holes move in whatever direction the built-in field points.

Deck 02 p. 8 shows the size of the effect. For a 70 nm oxide irradiated to 10⁶ rad at gate biases from −10 to +10 V, the flat-band shift is 0.15–0.2 V from −10 to −5 V, falls to about 0.07 V at −2 V and to its minimum of about 0.05 V near −1 V, is back to about 0.2 V at zero bias, rises steeply once the gate turns positive, and saturates near 1.2 V from +5 to +10 V. The minimum lies where the applied voltage cancels the built-in potential of the zero-bias case, so that the oxide field, and with it the charge yield, is close to zero. At zero applied bias the built-in field remains, and the shift is as large as at any negative bias on the plot. Deck 02's own calculation recipe (p. 7) records the consequence by treating the effective yield of trapped charge as larger at positive gate bias.

The worst-case bias

A total-dose test is only useful if it bounds what the part will do in service, and the measurements above say what that requires of the bias. The part must be irradiated under the bias that produces the largest response it will meet in operation. For the gate oxide of a MOS transistor that is positive gate bias at the operating supply — VDD on the gate of an n-channel transistor — because it sends the holes to the silicon, where they weigh fully in (6.2), and sends the hydrogen there too. Grounding every pin during the exposure does not produce a conservative test. It leaves only the built-in potential across the oxide, which on the plot above gives about 0.2 V against 1.2 V at positive bias — a sixth of the response — and the part then fails in service at a dose it survived in the laboratory. The bias is also not a quantity to raise beyond the operating value in the hope of overtesting: the net trapped charge passes through a maximum at intermediate field (M07 §4), so a field above the operating one can trap less. Other structures have other worst cases. For the buried oxide of silicon on insulator it is the off-state bias (drain at the supply, every other terminal grounded) or the transmission-gate bias (source and drain at the supply, gate grounded) rather than the on state, and which of the two depends on whether the transistor body is tied to a contact and on the ratio of gate length to buried-oxide thickness. The worst case is therefore established by characterization rather than assumed, which is the practice M15 §6 develops.

Bias after irradiation

The field goes on acting after the exposure, and the experiment that shows it most directly is the switched-bias measurement. Deck 02 p. 9 reproduces Winokur, McLean and Boesch's version on hardened 96.5 nm wet-oxide capacitors irradiated to 0.6 Mrad with a linear accelerator (LINAC), under a field of 4 MV/cm whose polarity was reversed at chosen times. The slide plots the density of interface states — written NSS, an older symbol for the interface-trap density — for five capacitors, A to E, starting from a pre-irradiation level of about 0.17×10¹¹ cm⁻². Capacitors A–D were biased positive during the exposure and the first second after it, and all four build identically, to about 0.45×10¹¹ cm⁻² by 0.8 s. At that time the bias on B, C and D was reversed to negative. A, kept positive, goes on to about 2.3×10¹¹ cm⁻² by a few thousand seconds. D, held negative from then on, stays at 0.45×10¹¹ cm⁻². B and C, returned to positive bias at about 20 s and about 200 s, resume building only at the moment of that second switch, and finish lower — about 1.3×10¹¹ and 1.0×10¹¹ cm⁻². E, biased negative throughout, stays at the pre-irradiation level from the first reading onward.

Deck 06 p. 36 (Deck 05 p. 52) repeats the experiment on a 42 nm oxide, after Saks, Dozier and Brown. The devices receive +8.4 V on the gate at a switching time tsw after the exposure; until then the buildup stays close to zero, and the later the switch the lower the final value. The slide does not print the bias applied before the switch; in the analogous Deck 02 experiment the capacitors returned to positive bias had been held negative from 0.8 s until the switch. The final densities, per 50 krad, are about 2.65×10¹⁰ cm⁻²eV⁻¹ with no switch and about 2.3, 1.3, 0.95 and 0.65×10¹⁰ cm⁻²eV⁻¹ for switches at 0.1, 25, 100 and 300 s.

Both experiments point to one agent. Something is released in the oxide during and just after the exposure; it is positively charged, because a positive gate drives it to the silicon and a negative gate holds it off; it creates an interface trap when it arrives; and it is used up or lost if the field keeps it away, since a late switch never recovers the full buildup. The hydrogen ion released by transporting holes near the interfaces — the second stage of the model in M07 §8 — is the species that fits, and the switched-bias results are the evidence Deck 06 p. 36 cites for its heading: interface-trap formation is a two-stage process involving proton transport to the interface. The component that forms within the first second on Deck 02 p. 9 is gated by polarity as well: capacitors A–D, positive during that second, all show it, and E, negative throughout, shows none. The bias applied after the exposure acts on the trapped holes too, through the electron exchange that §6 describes, and it gates interface-trap formation after a cold irradiation in exactly the way shown here (§8).

4 · How deep the holes sit

A trapped hole is neutralized by one of two routes, and one of them — thermal emission — depends on how deep in energy the trap lies (M07 §7). The distribution of those depths therefore decides how fast an oxide anneals when it is warm, and it is measured with thermally stimulated current. The same measurement, set beside a capacitance–voltage measurement on the same sample, also counts the electrons that compensate trapped holes, which is how the electron traps of §6 were quantified. Deck 06 p. 25 (Deck 05 p. 41) shows the method and a set of results, after Fleetwood et al.

Thermally stimulated current (TSC) heats an irradiated MOS capacitor at a steady rate while a bias holds a field across the oxide, and records the current in the external circuit. As the temperature rises, trapped carriers acquire enough thermal energy to escape their traps, the field sweeps them out of the oxide, and each carrier moving through the oxide induces current in the circuit. A released carrier delivers to the external circuit its charge times the fraction of the oxide thickness it crosses, so the integral of the current over the ramp is the released charge weighted by the distance each carrier travels. The polarity of the ramp bias therefore decides what is counted. Holes trapped near the silicon after positive-bias irradiation count in full only under a negative gate bias, which sweeps them across the whole oxide to the gate; under a positive gate bias they cross only the few nanometres to the silicon, and Fleetwood, Reber and Winokur observed very little current in that configuration. The IEEE NSREC 2001 Short Course gives the working conditions: ramps of about 0.1 °C/s, up to about 350 °C, above which the leakage current of the capacitor swamps the detrapped charge, and an applied bias throughout, without which the released charge is not collected.

The temperature scale is an energy scale. A trap empties when its thermal emission rate becomes comparable to the rate at which the temperature is being swept, and the emission rate falls exponentially with the depth of the trap level above the SiO₂ valence-band edge, so a deeper trap needs a higher temperature before it releases its charge. Deck 06 p. 25 maps roughly 40–350 °C onto trap depths from about 1.2 eV to about 2.2 eV; its energy axis begins at 1.2 eV, behind an axis break. The mapping is of the same kind as the thermal-emission front of §5, (9.3): deeper levels empty at higher temperature, roughly in proportion to absolute temperature. It is not (9.3) itself. The published scale comes from the generalized activation analysis that Fleetwood and co-workers applied to the ramp, and at 350 °C it lies about 8% below a scale proportional to absolute temperature anchored at 1.2 eV, which would reach about 2.4 eV there.

The results on p. 25 cover five oxides: a radiation-soft 350 nm oxide irradiated to only 20 krad, and four gate oxides between 18 and 45 nm — labelled hard, wet, dry and AT&T on the slide — irradiated to 1–5 Mrad. Every one releases its charge over a broad band of temperature, with its main peak between about 200 and 270 °C, or about 1.7–1.95 eV. The NSREC 2001 Short Course reports peaks near 1.2 and 1.9 eV in the buried oxide of a SIMOX (separation by implanted oxygen) silicon-on-insulator structure, and notes that the same peaks appear in thermally stimulated current from thermal oxides. Two consequences follow. The trap levels form a broad distribution rather than a single level, so thermal emission empties them over many decades of time rather than with one time constant, which is the subject of §5. And the soft oxide, after a dose a hundred times smaller than the others received, releases the largest current of all, more than 10 pA at its peak against a few picoamperes or less for the gate oxides. A soft oxide is one with a high density of hole traps, and the measurement shows how much higher.

The second use of the measurement is counting. The course's analysis, on Deck 07 p. 35 after Reber and Fleetwood, applies to a capacitor irradiated under positive bias and heated with the released holes driven toward the gate, as the slide draws them. It takes the current integrated over the ramp, after the leakage baseline of the capacitor is subtracted, as the total trapped positive charge Q(+): every trapped hole, whether or not an electron is compensating it. The midgap voltage shift of M07 §9, measured before the ramp, gives the net charge Q(net) = −CoxΔVmg, holes minus electrons. The compensating electron charge is the difference, Q(−) = Q(+) − Q(net). If QTSC exceeds QCV, electrons have been captured and are compensating trapped holes without removing them. Oldham's review records the outcome across three independent groups: hardened gate oxides show QTSC > QCV, and soft oxides show the two equal within uncertainty. The ratio of trapped electrons to trapped holes then follows as Q(−)/Q(+) = (QTSC − QCV)/QTSC, which is the fe/fh of §6. M10 §8 writes the separation as an equation and extends it to capacitance–voltage hysteresis.

A gate oxide irradiated under positive bias releases 3×10¹² cm⁻² of positive charge in a thermally stimulated current sweep made with the gate negative, while its midgap shift before the sweep corresponds to 2×10¹² cm⁻² of net positive charge. How many holes and electrons were trapped, and what is fe/fh?

Q(+)/q = Nh = 3×10¹² cm⁻², and Nh − Ne = 2×10¹² cm⁻², so Ne = 1×10¹² cm⁻² and fe/fh ≈ 0.33 — within the range of the dry gate oxides of §6. The voltage shift alone reports only two-thirds of the holes actually trapped; one hole in three is hidden behind a compensating electron.

5 · Neutralization kinetics

Stage 3 of Figure 1 is the slow removal of trapped holes, and M07 §7 named its two routes: electrons tunnelling into the trap from the silicon, at a rate set by how far the trap is from the interface, and thermal emission, at a rate set by how deep the trap level lies. This section turns the two routes into kinetics. It starts with the elementary case of a single trap population, which gives the exponential decay the course's calculations use, and then lets the traps spread in depth and in energy, which turns each route into a front that advances as the logarithm of time. Deck 06 p. 26 (Deck 05 p. 42) gives both fronts, after McWhorter et al.

One trap population

Suppose every trapped hole sits in an identical trap: same distance from the silicon, same energy. Each then has the same probability per unit time, 1/τ, of being neutralized, and each is neutralized independently of the others. The number remaining falls at a rate proportional to the number present, which is first-order kinetics, and its solution is an exponential.

ΔNot(t) = ΔNot,0 e−t/τ   ⟺   ΔVot(t) = ΔVot,0 e−t/τ

Describes a single population of identical traps, and oxide-trap charge only — interface traps do not anneal on this timescale below about 125 °C (§9). On the formula sheet as (9.1).

Traps spread in depth: the tunnelling front

Real traps are not identical. The near-interface region that holds them has a finite thickness, and the probability per unit time that an electron tunnels from the silicon into a trap falls exponentially with the trap's distance x from the interface, as αe−2βx. Each trap still obeys (9.1), but with its own time constant, and the time constants of traps a nanometre apart differ by orders of magnitude. At a time t, traps whose rate times t is much greater than one have almost certainly been neutralized, and traps whose rate times t is much less than one have almost certainly not. Because the rate changes by a factor of e over every 1/(2β) of depth, the change from one population to the other is abrupt — about 0.2 nm wide, in Oldham's account — and it defines a boundary, the tunnelling front, at the depth where the rate times the elapsed time equals one.

xm(t) = 12β ln(αt)
(9.2)

Trapped holes closer to the silicon than xm have been neutralized; those farther in remain. The front advances by ln10/(2β) per decade of time — about 0.2 nm per decade in Oldham's model calculations, which corresponds to 2β ≈ 11.5 nm⁻¹. The course's rule of thumb for the same process, Deck 07 p. 19, fixes where the front stands as well as how fast it moves: a trap 2.5 nm from the silicon exchanges charge in about 1 s, and each 0.25 nm moves that time by a decade — M10 (10.6), which traces the difference in rate to the barrier and effective mass assumed. Nearly independent of temperature, because tunnelling needs no thermal activation. A second front advances from the gate electrode in the same way. On the formula sheet as (9.2).

Traps spread in energy: the thermal-emission front

The same argument applies in energy. A hole trapped at a level a depth φ above the SiO₂ valence-band edge is neutralized when an electron is thermally excited from the valence band into the trap — equivalently, when the hole is emitted into the band — at a rate proportional to AT²e−qφ/kT. The rate falls by a factor of e for every kT/q of depth, so again the traps divide sharply into emptied and filled, and the boundary is the thermal-emission front.

φm(t) = kTq ln(AT²t)
(9.3)
φm energy depth of the thermal-emission front above the SiO₂ valence-band edge, in volts (numerically equal to its value in eV) · k = 1.381×10⁻²³ J/K with q = 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ C, or directly k/q = 8.617×10⁻⁵ V/K · T absolute temperature (K, never °C) · A emission prefactor (s⁻¹K⁻²) · t time at temperature (s). Trapped holes shallower than φm have been emptied. The front advances by (kT/q)ln10 per decade of time: 59.5 mV at 300 K, 74 mV at 100 °C and 15 mV at 77 K. At a fixed time it lies deeper in proportion to T, which is why heating accelerates annealing and why cooling stops it. On the formula sheet as (9.3).

Why annealing goes as the logarithm of time

Both fronts advance in proportion to ln t. The charge neutralized by a given time is the number of traps the fronts have passed, so if the traps are spread roughly uniformly over the depths the tunnelling front sweeps, the charge removed grows in proportion to the distance the front has moved, and the remaining oxide-trap charge falls linearly in log time — each decade removing about the same amount as the decade before. Oldham describes the tunnelling front as the analytical basis for exactly this ln t annealing, which is observed empirically in many MOS oxides, most cleanly in unhardened commercial ones. Where the trap population is concentrated at the interface, as in hardened gate oxides, the front runs out of traps as it moves inward and the slope of the annealing curve falls with time. The emission front gives ln t annealing only if trap levels are spread uniformly in energy; the measured distributions of §4 have peaks of finite width, so that description holds over a limited range of time and temperature.

The two routes differ most in their response to temperature. Tunnelling needs no thermal activation, and Oldham records that the annealing of every oxide studied is essentially independent of temperature at room temperature and over some range above it — the tunnelling front dominates there. The emission front lies deeper in proportion to absolute temperature by (9.3), so at 100 °C it sits about a quarter deeper than at room temperature after the same time, and its prefactor rises as T² besides. The temperature at which emission takes over varies from oxide to oxide: in some it matters below 100 °C, in others the response stays temperature-independent well above 100 °C, and by 200 °C every oxide undergoes significant thermal annealing in a short time. Isochronal anneals of thermal oxides produce significant removal by about 300 °C. Both distributions — how close the traps sit to the interface and how deep their levels lie — are fixed during fabrication, which is why Deck 06 p. 26 closes with the statement that the distribution of traps is fabrication dependent, and why §10 treats annealing as a lever of the process.

One exponential describes one trap level; real oxides anneal as ln t. The course's calculations use (9.1), and it is exact for what it describes: a population of identical traps, all at one depth and one energy, emptying with one time constant at one temperature. A real oxide holds traps spread over nanometres of depth and about an electronvolt of energy (§4), whose time constants spread over many decades, and its trapped charge anneals roughly linearly in log time — still falling at 108 s in Figure 1. (9.1) takes the charge from 90% to under 1% of its initial value between one-tenth of τ and five times τ, a span of less than two decades, whereas a measured oxide at room temperature goes on annealing across eight decades or more. Use (9.1) whenever a calculation supplies a single time constant; reason with the fronts of (9.2) and (9.3) when predicting how a real part behaves over months or years, and never extrapolate a single fitted τ far beyond the times it was fitted over.

By the rule of Deck 07 p. 19, the tunnelling front stands 2.5 nm from the silicon about one second after the holes are trapped and advances 0.25 nm per decade. When does it reach 3.0 nm, and what does that imply for a 5 nm gate oxide with fronts advancing from both interfaces? How does the first answer change at Oldham's 0.2 nm per decade from the same starting point?

Half a nanometre at 0.25 nm per decade is two decades, so the front reaches 3.0 nm at about 102 s, under two minutes. In a 5 nm oxide, fronts from the silicon and the gate each need to cover only 2.5 nm to meet in the middle, which the rule places at about 1 s. Within about a second of trapping, then, no region of the oxide is beyond the reach of tunnelling electrons, which is the mechanism behind the thin-oxide behaviour of §7. At 0.2 nm per decade the same half nanometre takes two and a half decades, about 300 s; the 5 nm answer does not change, because it depends only on the starting point. The two rates are rules of thumb and differ by less than a decade here; both say that each further nanometre of depth costs four to five decades of time.

6 · Electron traps and compensation

M07 §7 ended with a third way for the net oxide charge to fall: electrons captured near trapped holes compensate them without removing them. This section measures how much compensation there is, shows that it depends on how the oxide was grown, and establishes the property that makes it matter — much of it can be undone. The sources are Deck 06 pp. 28, 32 and 33 (Deck 05 pp. 44, 48 and 49), the heading "reversibility of oxide-trap charge annealing" on Deck 02 p. 12, and Oldham's review of switching oxide traps in the course textbook.

Deck 06 p. 28 reports fe/fh, the ratio of trapped electrons to trapped holes obtained from thermally stimulated current and capacitance–voltage measurements as in §4, for eleven thermally grown gate oxides. Oxides are grown either in steam (wet) or in dry oxygen (dry), a choice M10 §2 describes. The four wet oxides, 23 to 348 nm thick, lie between about 0.1 and 0.25. The seven dry oxides, 18 to 104 nm thick, lie between about 0.3 and 0.65, and every dry oxide lies above every wet one. The chart is drawn in three dimensions, so individual values read from it are approximate; the separation between the two groups is not. The slide draws two conclusions: in some thermal gate oxides the number of electron traps exceeds half the number of hole traps, and electron traps can compensate oxide-trap charge quickly, during irradiation as well as after it. The first has a direct quantitative consequence. The net shift reports Nh(1 − fe/fh), so in an oxide with fe/fh = 0.5 the voltage shift counts only half of the holes actually trapped.

Compensation that can be undone

The defect behind both kinds of trapping is the E′ centre of M07 §6: an oxygen vacancy whose strained Si–Si bond breaks when it captures a hole, leaving a positively charged silicon on one side and a neutral silicon carrying an unpaired electron on the other. When an electron later tunnels in from the substrate, the outcome depends on how far the two silicon atoms have relaxed apart. If they remain close, the electron allows the Si–Si bond to re-form and the defect returns to a neutral vacancy — true annealing. If the positive silicon has relaxed far from its partner, the electron settles on the neutral side instead, producing a dipole: a positively charged silicon beside a negatively charged one. The dipole is net neutral, so the voltage shift disappears, but the defect is intact, and under a reversed field the extra electron can tunnel back into the silicon and restore the positive charge. This is the dipole model of Lelis et al., and defects that behave this way are called switching oxide traps — the border traps of M07 §9, which exchange charge with the silicon on the timescale of an electrical measurement.

Three measurements established the model. Schwank et al. annealed an irradiated oxide at +10 V and 100 °C for about a week, until its midgap shift showed no trapped positive charge at all, then reversed the bias to −10 V at the same temperature: within about a day nearly half of the "annealed" charge had returned. Lelis et al. irradiated hardened oxides with a short LINAC pulse and annealed them with the field alternating between +1 and −1 MV/cm; about 0.5 V of shift went back and forth with each reversal, repeatably over several cycles, on top of a permanent annealing that the reversals did not undo. And Conley et al. followed the E′ electron-spin signal through alternating-bias anneals and found it rising under negative bias and falling under positive bias, in step with the electrical charge — which the dipole configuration explains, because the unpaired spin and the positive charge sit on different silicon atoms of the same defect. Oldham's review describes this last result as settling the question in favour of the dipole model.

Deck 06 pp. 32–33 add the microscopic detail. Page 32 lists four charge-exchange processes involving the E′ centre and its precursor: an electron from the silicon neutralizing a charged E′ centre; an electron emitted from a neutral oxygen vacancy into the silicon, which leaves a positively charged defect without any radiation; and electron exchange between the silicon and either a charged E′ centre or a neutral precursor, each of which produces low-frequency noise. Page 33 names three structural variants — the dimer E′δ, the four-fold puckered E′γ4, which forms a dipole, and the five-fold puckered E′γ5, which does not — and explains why vacancies near the interface are the ones that exchange charge. The electron level of an unstrained vacancy in the bulk oxide lies well above the silicon Fermi level, where it can neither give nor take electrons. Stretching the Si–Si bond lowers the level, and in the strained transition region near the interface the levels of stretched vacancies fall to the silicon Fermi level, where electrons pass back and forth. First-principles calculations cited by Oldham give the same division: a dimer anneals permanently when an electron arrives, while the puckered configuration forms the metastable dipole.

Switching is a property of the process. Oldham records that radiation-hardened oxides from several manufacturers show a significant fraction of switching oxide traps, while soft commercial oxides show almost none — the same division that thermally stimulated current finds between QTSC > QCV and QTSC = QCV (§4); §10 traces the difference to a single anneal step. The consequences reach beyond the radiation response. An oxide whose shift has annealed away under positive bias still contains compensated defects, so its threshold depends on the bias it is held at afterwards, and a part annealed at one polarity can shift again when held at the other. The same near-interface defects produce the 1/f noise that M08 §4 attributes to oxide-trap and border-trap charge. M10 §6 places border traps among the oxide, interface and border traps of a processed gate stack.

7 · Thin oxides

M07 §3 showed that the oxide-trap shift scales as tox² — one power from the volume that generates pairs, one from the capacitance that converts charge to voltage — so a thinner gate oxide is less susceptible to total dose. Deck 04 p. 22 adds that below 5 nm there is almost no net hole trapping at all. This section follows the thickness dependence down through the range where the square law fails, identifies the mechanism that makes it fail, and gives the corresponding law for interface traps. The data are on Deck 06 pp. 27 and 46 (Deck 05 pp. 43 and 62).

The cleanest test of the square law is made at 80 K. At that temperature, and at the moderate fields of the measurements below, hole transport is effectively frozen on the timescale of a measurement — §2 found 85% of the initial shift still present after 103 s at 124 K, and colder is slower still — and the thermal-emission front of (9.3) advances by only about 15 mV per decade. Cold transport is strongly field-activated rather than stopped outright: the etched-back series of Deck 06 p. 23 was measured at 79 K and 5 MV/cm, and its holes crossed oxides of about 40 to 96 nm in about 0.015 to 0.5 s. The holes therefore stay where they were generated, spread uniformly through the oxide, and by (6.2) of M06 a uniform charge has its centroid at half the thickness. The shift is then the purely geometric one: pairs generated in proportion to tox, converted to voltage in proportion to tox, and weighted by one half. The dashed line on Deck 06 p. 27, after Boesch and McGarrity, is that tox² dependence.

Saks, Ancona and Modolo measured the flat-band shift of capacitors at 80 K and +2 MV/cm over a range of thicknesses, and their data follow the square law only down to about 20–25 nm. Below that the shift falls away steeply: at 12 nm it is about half a volt per Mrad(Si), against about 2.5 V on the square-law line, and near 6 nm it is about 10⁻³ V per Mrad(Si), several hundred times below the line. Transistor threshold shifts measured at +1 MV/cm fall in the same way. The cause is electron tunnelling from both electrodes. The tunnelling fronts of §5 advance into the oxide from the silicon and from the gate, and every hole within their reach is neutralized; the tunnelling distance in SiO₂ is about 3 nm, so once the oxide is only a few times thicker than that a large fraction of the holes lie within reach of one interface or the other, and at about 6 nm or less the positive charge is readily neutralized from both sides. Tunnelling needs no thermal activation, so it continues at 80 K while, at these fields, transport and emission are frozen. That is what makes the 80 K measurement decisive: it holds every thermally activated process still and leaves tunnelling as the only thing that can depart from the square law, and the departure is large.

The thickness numbers that appear across the course describe different stages of the same process and do not conflict. Departure from the square law begins near 20–25 nm in the 80 K data. It becomes large below about 10 nm, which is where Oldham's review places it and where Deck 06 p. 46 draws its thin-oxide boundary for interface traps, at 12 nm. And below about 5 nm, as Deck 04 p. 22 states, there is almost no net hole trapping, because tunnelling reaches essentially every trap — with the qualification on the same slide that net positive oxide-trap charge can still arise from hydrogen ions. Gate oxides reached about 6 nm around the 0.25 µm technology generation, as Cester and Paccagnella note in the course textbook, which is why the gate-oxide shift ceased to limit total-dose hardness and the failure moved to the thick isolation oxides of M08 §8.

Interface traps follow a thickness law of their own. Deck 06 p. 46 reports Saks' measurements on as-grown oxides irradiated at room temperature: in the thick-oxide regime above 12 nm the interface-trap density per unit dose follows a power of thickness,

Alternative dielectrics do not simply inherit the benefit: Deck 04 p. 25 shows high-permittivity films trapping more charge than SiO₂ of the same equivalent thickness, and M10 §4 takes up their radiation response.

8 · Interface-trap buildup kinetics

M07 §8 set out the two-stage hydrogen model of interface-trap formation — holes transport through the oxide, hydrogen ions are released near an interface, and the ions drift to the Si/SiO₂ interface and depassivate Si–H bonds — and listed four observations: buildup is slow, depends on field, depends on temperature, and can resume long afterwards. This section puts numbers on each observation, adds the atomistic picture of the reaction, and shows that the late stage of the process changes as parts age.

Time

Deck 06 p. 35 (Deck 05 p. 51), after Shaneyfelt et al., follows the interface-trap density of transistors irradiated with a 10 MeV electron LINAC, at four pulses per second to about 75 krad, under a constant +1 MV/cm, and plots it against time counted from the first pulse. Three pulse trains are plotted — 5 pulses at 1.4×10⁹ rad/s, 70 at 1.1×10⁸ rad/s and 572 at 1.3×10⁷ rad/s — which at four pulses per second last about 1, 17 and 143 s, and each data set begins where its train ends. When the 5-pulse train ends, about a second after the first pulse, the density has already risen to about 5×10¹⁰ cm⁻²eV⁻¹. It reaches half of its final value, the time marked τ1/2 on the slide, after some tens of seconds; about 80% by 10³ s; about 90% by 10⁴ s; and it saturates near 2×10¹¹ cm⁻²eV⁻¹ by about 10⁵ s. Once each exposure is over the three sets lie on one curve, a first sign of the dose-rate independence §9 establishes.

Normal interface-trap buildup therefore begins within a second, as protons released near the interface complete the short drift they need; it grows roughly linearly in log time from 1 to 10³ s, by which time it is about four-fifths complete, and it saturates near 10⁵ s. In oxides that show latent buildup a second rise follows near 10⁶ s. The switched-bias curves of Deck 02 p. 9 in §3 run on the same timescale, still rising at several thousand seconds.

The course's 10³–10⁶ s is the time buildup takes to finish. The course characterizes interface-trap buildup as a process of 10³–10⁶ s. Deck 06 p. 35 describes the same data as taking thousands of seconds to build up until saturation, and Deck 06 p. 39 adds the latent rise near 10⁶ s, so the range spans the approach to saturation and the latent stage. It does not locate most of the growth: on the measured curve, normal buildup is under way within 1 s, half complete in tens of seconds and about 80% complete by 10³ s. Use 10³–10⁶ s for how long buildup takes to finish, latent stage included, and the measured curve for how much has formed at a given time.

Temperature

Deck 06 p. 37 (Deck 05 p. 53), after Saks et al., measures what happens when the thermally activated steps are frozen and then released. A 26 nm oxide receives 0.74 Mrad under +5.2 V, and the measurement begins at about 80 K; the device is then warmed in steps toward room temperature, with the interface-trap density measured after each step and the gate held at +5.2, +10.4, 0 or −5.2 V during the warming. At about 80 K the density is only about 10⁸ cm⁻²eV⁻¹. Under positive bias it ends near 2×10¹¹ cm⁻²eV⁻¹ at room temperature, so buildup at 80 K is about three decades smaller than at room temperature. Two components are visible. A small one, about 3×10⁹ cm⁻²eV⁻¹, appears by about 120 K and is the same for every bias. The main one appears only above about 200 K, rises steeply under positive bias and more slowly at zero bias, and is absent under −5.2 V, which holds the density near 4×10⁹ cm⁻²eV⁻¹ all the way to room temperature. The main component is the thermally activated, bias-gated drift of hydrogen ions to the interface — the same agent the switched-bias experiments of §3 isolate — and below about 200 K it does not move.

Field

M07 §4 showed that the yield-adjusted interface-trap buildup falls with field as about Eox−0.6 (Deck 06 p. 38, Deck 05 p. 54), the same dependence within uncertainty as the capture of holes near the interface — the evidence that interface traps are made by the holes that arrive there. To that magnitude dependence §3 adds the dependence on direction: positive gate bias drives the hydrogen to the silicon and negative bias holds it off, during the exposure and after it.

The reaction at the interface

Deck 06 p. 44 (Deck 05 p. 60) writes the reaction that forms the trap, and M07 §8 explains its notation: H⁺ + H–Si≡Si₃ → H₂ + (+)Si≡Si₃. Deck 06 p. 45 (Deck 05 p. 61) shows it as density-functional calculations by Rashkeev et al. and Shen et al. resolve it, and writes it compactly as SiH + H⁺ → H₂ + D⁺. The upper panels show the atomic structure and the lower panels the calculated electron density: a proton arrives at the hydrogen of a passivated Si–H bond at the interface, the two hydrogens pair into an H₂ molecule, the molecule leaves, and the silicon is left with a dangling bond carrying positive charge — the defect D⁺, which is the charged Pb centre. The calculations find a similar reaction when the Si–H at or near the interface is replaced by an O–H. The Si–H bonds that the proton attacks are the ones the final hydrogen anneal of wafer processing deliberately forms to passivate the interface, as EEE6338 M05b §4.1 describes: the passivation that makes a new interface clean also supplies the bonds from which radiation later makes traps.

Latent buildup and aging

Deck 06 p. 39 (Deck 05 p. 55), after Schwank et al., shows the late stage on p-channel transistors, with the interface-trap shift normalized to its maximum. Normal buildup brings it to about 0.27 within a few hundred seconds; it then holds flat across what the slide calls the interface-trap window, from about 10³ to 10⁶ s; and after about 2×10⁶ s it rises again, reaching its maximum by about 2×10⁷ s. The latent stage thus nearly quadruples the interface-trap density — the slide's "as much as four times" — and it arrives around 10⁶ s, about eleven days after the exposure. The slide records that it is typically seen only in oxides with high vacancy densities, is initiated by the transport of molecular hydrogen and its reactions in or near the gate oxide, is strongly thermally activated with an activation energy of about 0.41 eV, and is a possible concern for space systems. With that activation energy a rate proportional to exp(−EA/(kT)) is about 25 times faster at 100 °C than at 25 °C, which is why heating brings latent buildup forward in time.

Deck 06 p. 40 (Deck 05 p. 56) reproduces measurements from a study by Ding et al. titled "Aging effects and latent interface-trap buildup in MOS transistors", made with 10 keV X-rays and plotted as interface-trap density, net oxide-trap charge and threshold shift against time for four data sets labelled 1989, 1997, 2008 and 2021, annealed at 25, 50 or 100 °C. The 1989 set held at 25 °C stays near 0.5×10¹¹ cm⁻² from the end of irradiation to beyond 10⁶ s and then rises to about 2×10¹¹ cm⁻² near 10⁷ s — the latent buildup of p. 39 — while at 100 °C the same set rises about an order of magnitude earlier in time. At the end of the same exposure the 2021 set carries about 2.6×10¹¹ cm⁻², roughly four times the 1989 set. The comparison is the point of the study: the radiation response of a MOS technology is not fixed when it is fabricated but changes with the history of the part, which is the subject of M15 §10.

9 · Dose rate, temperature and the anneal

Two measurements by Fleetwood and co-workers, on Deck 06 pp. 41 and 42 (Deck 05 pp. 57 and 58), turn the kinetics of §§5 and 8 into the physical basis of total-dose testing for MOS parts. The first shows that interface-trap buildup depends on the time since the dose was delivered and not on how fast it was delivered. The second shows that heating anneals trapped holes from about 75 °C upward but anneals interface traps only above about 125 °C — and grows them below that. Taken with the behaviour of the same processes at cryogenic temperature, they show what temperature does to each of the two charges.

One response curve

Deck 06 p. 41 plots the interface-trap shift ΔVit against time for transistors irradiated with five sources whose dose rates span more than eleven decades: two pulses from a LINAC at 6×10⁹ rad(SiO₂)/s, X-rays at 5550 and 52 rad(SiO₂)/s, and Cs-137 gamma rays at 0.165 and 0.05 rad/s. Every source falls on the same curve, which rises from 0.28 V at about 0.4 s to about 0.5 V at 10² s and about 0.7 V at a few thousand seconds, and levels off near 0.75–0.8 V from 10⁵ to 10⁶ s. Each source enters the plot where its own exposure ends — within a second for the LINAC, about 20 s for the faster X-ray source, about half an hour for the slower, a week or more for the two Cs-137 sources — and the fast exposures continue as room-temperature anneals. At equal elapsed time the shift is the same whatever the dose rate.

The result follows from the kinetics. Each increment of dose releases its own holes and its own hydrogen, and the sequence each increment starts — transport, release, drift, reaction — runs at rates that do not depend on how many other increments are being delivered at the same time. The response to a long, slow exposure is then the sum of the responses to its increments, each displaced in time, and it depends on the dose rate only through the time the exposure takes. In the terms of M08 §9, MOS interface-trap buildup is a time-dependent effect, not a true dose-rate effect, and the oxide-trap shift behaves the same way (M15 §4). The practical consequence is large: a laboratory source thousands of times faster than the space environment, followed by a room-temperature anneal lasting as long as the slow exposure, reproduces the slow exposure's response. An orbital exposure lasts years, so the MOS test procedure of M15 §5 does not attempt that reproduction. It uses the same time dependence to bound each failure mechanism instead: a measurement promptly after the fast irradiation, before trapped holes have had time to anneal, bounds the trapped-hole damage, and a further irradiation followed by a 100 °C anneal bounds the interface-trap damage, for reasons the end of this section gives. Neither step reproduces the orbital response. The time-dependence argument fails for linear bipolar parts, whose low-field isolation oxides show a true dose-rate effect: Deck 02 p. 15 shows pnp transistors irradiated at 300 rad/s and then annealed at 100 °C never reaching the degradation produced at 1.1 rad/s, the demonstration that hardness-assurance methods effective for MOS do not work for linear bipolar devices.

The isochronal anneal

An isochronal anneal holds a device for an equal time at each of a rising series of temperatures and measures it after each step; the temperature at which a quantity changes identifies the process that changes it. Deck 06 p. 42 anneals an irradiated transistor for 30 minutes at each of nine temperatures from 25 to 300 °C, at an oxide field of 2.5 MV/cm, and separates the threshold shift into its two components after every step by the midgap method of M07 §9. The two components respond in opposite ways.

The oxide-trap shift is unchanged by the 50 °C step, begins to anneal at 75 °C and keeps annealing from then on: from about −1.5 V it falls to about −1.3 V after the 100 °C step, about −0.56 V after 200 °C and about −0.35 V after 300 °C, so about three-quarters has gone by the end. That is the thermal-emission front of (9.3) reaching deeper traps with each rise in temperature. The interface-trap shift does the opposite at first. It grows, from 1.3 V to about 2.1 V after the 100 °C step and to a peak of about 2.2 V at 125 °C, holds near that value at 150 °C, and only then falls — to about 1.5 V after 200 °C and 0.4 V after 300 °C. The slide's conclusion is explicit, and Deck 06 p. 48 repeats it among the deck's take-aways: temperatures above about 125 °C are normally required for significant interface-trap annealing. Below that temperature, heating accelerates the buildup that is still under way faster than it removes finished traps. The threshold shift, the sum of the two, goes from −0.2 V through zero between 50 and 75 °C to +1.1 V at 150 °C before falling back toward zero: the rebound of M08 §3, driven by temperature instead of time.

Why the rebound anneal is at 100 °C

The accelerated anneal that the MOS test method applies after irradiation when rebound has to be bounded — 168 h at 100 °C, as in M08 §3 and M15 §5 — is chosen from these two results. At 100 °C interface traps are below their anneal range, and their buildup is accelerated: Deck 11 p. 10, after the same Fleetwood study, shows a 100 °C anneal of 32 nm n-channel transistors irradiated to 300 krad(SiO₂) taking the interface-trap shift to its plateau, about 0.65 V, by the first measurement near 4×10³ s and holding it there for a week, where a room-temperature anneal is still short of the plateau after 10⁷ s. Trapped holes, meanwhile, keep annealing. On Deck 06 p. 42 only about a tenth of them has gone by the end of the 100 °C step, cumulatively over the 30-minute steps at 50, 75 and 100 °C, but the emission front of (9.3) advances with time as well as temperature: at 100 °C it moves about 74 mV per decade, and the bake lasts two and a half decades longer than the step, so the front reaches about 0.19 eV deeper. Deck 11 p. 11 shows the leakage produced by trapped holes falling by several orders of magnitude during a 100 °C anneal. The bake therefore leaves the part with the most interface-trap charge and the least oxide-trap charge it can reach, which for an n-channel transistor is the most positive threshold — the worst case for the drive and timing failures of M08 §4. An anneal above 125 °C would remove the very interface traps the test exists to bound.

10 · From kinetics to hardening

Every dependence measured in this module is also a lever, because each rate depends on something a process engineer or a circuit designer controls: the thickness of the oxide, the hydrogen available to it, the density and position of its traps, the dielectric it is made of, and the bias it sits under. Deck 06 p. 48 names four routes to radiation hardening — hydrogen control, optimized oxidation, alternative dielectrics and process modifications — and Deck 02 p. 8 draws the same conclusion from the earliest hardening work: oxide thickness plays a key role, and the fabrication process is very important. Oldham's review gives the clearest single demonstration that one process step moves the kinetics. A manufacturer's split lot produced hardened and unhardened oxides that differed only in the post-oxidation anneal, the hardened one receiving it at a lower temperature. Measured after a short pulse, the hardened oxide showed about an order of magnitude less shift at 10² and 10³ s, yet extrapolated back to the end of the pulse the two may have trapped the same charge: the hardened oxide's traps sit closer to the interface, where the tunnelling front of (9.2) reaches them sooner. The same change made the hardened oxide switch (§6) where the unhardened one did not. The high-temperature inert anneal that commercial processes adopted to remove an instability associated with excess silicon near the interface also appears to drive oxygen vacancies deeper into the oxide, beyond the front's early reach, where they remain stable hole traps. Hardening by process is thus a trade among trap density, trap position and bias stability, and the choices are not independent.

Deck 06 p. 48 includes alternative dielectrics among the hardening routes, while Deck 04 p. 25 shows high-permittivity films trapping more charge than thermal SiO₂ of the same equivalent thickness; M10 §4 and §9 set out the evidence on both sides. Bias is not a process lever at all, but §3 shows it is as strong as any of them, which is why worst-case bias is specified in every test and why circuit designers can use it.

Where this leaves us

The irradiated oxide is a system still evolving when the exposure ends. Its electrons have left within picoseconds, its free holes within milliseconds at room temperature, and what remains is two populations moving in opposite directions: trapped holes, at their maximum at the end of the exposure and annealing as the logarithm of time by tunnelling and thermal emission, and interface traps, at their minimum then and building as hydrogen ions reach the silicon — under way within a second, largely formed by 10³ s, saturated near 10⁵ s, and in some oxides rising again near 10⁶ s. Temperature, field, bias and thickness each move these clocks differently. Cold, at low and moderate field, stops all but tunnelling on the timescale of a measurement; heat below 125 °C anneals trapped holes and completes interface traps, and only above 125 °C anneals the traps themselves; positive gate bias sends holes and hydrogen to the silicon, where both do the most harm; thin oxides lose their trapped holes to tunnelling from both electrodes.

Two results turn this into a test method. Interface-trap buildup follows one curve across eleven decades of dose rate, so a fast irradiation followed by a room-temperature anneal of the same duration reproduces a slow one, and a fast irradiation measured promptly bounds the damage from trapped holes; and a bake at 100 °C drives a part to its worst-case positive threshold without annealing the traps that produce it. M10 takes the process side: how the oxide is grown and annealed, where its traps come from, and what a process engineer can change to put fewer of them, or put them where the tunnelling front can reach them. M15 takes the test side, building the standard procedure from the two results above.

Quick reference

  • One time axis (pulse response): electrons out in ps; holes transit µs–ms in a room-temperature gate oxide; trapped holes anneal ∝ ln t to 10⁸ s and beyond; interface traps under way within 1 s, half formed in tens of seconds, ~80% by 10³ s, saturated ≈10⁵ s; the course's 10³–10⁶ s is the time to completion, latent stage included; latent buildup ≈10⁶ s (≈11 days), up to ×4, EA ≈ 0.41 eV (≈25× faster at 100 °C than 25 °C). Oxide-trap charge is maximum immediately after irradiation; interface-trap charge is minimum then.
  • Hole transport: transit time ∝ tox⁴ (slope 4.0 as-grown at 220 K, 1 MV/cm; 4.2 etched-back at 79 K, 5 MV/cm), against tox¹ for drift. 4 → 6 MV/cm shortens half-recovery by ~4 decades. Each cooling step of the 293–124 K series costs 1–2 decades; at 124 K, 85% of the shift remains after 10³ s.
  • Three field dependences: yield ↑ with E (M07 §2); transport faster with E; capture ↓ as E−1/2 (M07 §4). Net trapped charge peaks near 1–2 MV/cm.
  • Bias: positive gate = holes and H⁺ to the Si, x̄ ≈ tox, full shift, interface traps form; negative = holes at the gate, small shift, few interface traps; zero = built-in field only, ~0.2 V, as much as negative bias; minimum ~0.05 V near −1 V, where the applied bias cancels the built-in field. 70 nm, 10⁶ rad: ~1.2 V at +5 to +10 V against 0.15–0.2 V from −10 to −5 V. Irradiate at the worst-case operating bias (VDD on the gate); grounded pins under-test. Substrate doping plays no part in generating, transporting or trapping the charge.
  • Switched bias: interface-trap buildup waits for positive gate bias; a later switch gives less (42 nm: 2.65 → 2.3, 1.3, 0.95, 0.65 ×10¹⁰ cm⁻²eV⁻¹ per 50 krad for tsw = 0.1, 25, 100, 300 s) — the evidence for H⁺ drift.
  • TSC: biased ramp ~0.1 °C/s to ~350 °C; ~40–350 °C ↔ ~1.2–2.2 eV; peaks ~1.7–1.9 eV. Current integral = released charge × fraction of tox crossed. Q(+) = ∫ΔITSCdt = all trapped holes after positive-bias irradiation with the gate negative during the ramp (positive ramp bias gives very little current); Q(net) = −CoxΔVmg; Q(−) = Q(+) − Q(net); fe/fh = Q(−)/Q(+). Soft 350 nm at 20 krad out-releases hard oxides at Mrad.
  • (9.1) ΔNot(t) = ΔNot,0e−t/τ, t and τ in seconds, τ at one temperature: 37% at 1τ, 10% at 2.3τ, 1% at 4.6τ. One trap level only; real oxides anneal ∝ ln t.
  • Annealing is by tunnelling and thermal emission. (9.2) xm = (1/2β)ln(αt): ~0.2 nm per decade (Oldham), or the course's rule 2.5 nm at ~1 s and 0.25 nm per decade (Deck 07 p. 19, M10 (10.6)); from both electrodes, temperature-independent; a 5 nm oxide is within reach throughout by ~1 s. (9.3) φm = (kT/q)ln(AT²t): 59.5 mV per decade at 300 K, 74 at 100 °C, 15 at 77 K; T in K. Room-temperature annealing is tunnelling-dominated; every oxide anneals thermally by 200 °C.
  • Electron traps: fe/fh ≈ 0.1–0.25 wet, ≈ 0.3–0.65 dry (every dry above every wet); net shift = Nh(1 − fe/fh). Switching oxide traps (E′ dipoles, border traps): negative bias restored ~half of the "annealed" charge in a day at 100 °C; ±1 MV/cm cycling moves ~0.5 V reversibly. Hardened oxides switch; soft ones do not.
  • Thin oxides: 80 K data leave tox² below ~20–25 nm (at 80 K and +1 to +2 MV/cm tunnelling works; transport and emission do not); large departure below ~10–12 nm; <5 nm almost no net hole trapping (H⁺ excepted); tunnelling distance ~3 nm, ≤6 nm readily neutralized. (9.4) ΔDit ∝ toxn, n = 1.65 at +2 MV/cm, 1.01 at −2 MV/cm, above 12 nm. Thinner oxide = less susceptible.
  • Interface traps and temperature: ~10³ less at 80 K than at room temperature; main component forms above ~200 K and only without negative bias. Reaction SiH + H⁺ → H₂ + D⁺ (DFT).
  • Dose rate: one ΔVit(t) curve from 6×10⁹ to 0.05 rad/s — a time-dependent effect, not a true dose-rate effect, so MOS parts can be tested fast and annealed. Linear bipolar parts cannot (ELDRS).
  • Isochronal anneal (30 min per step, 2.5 MV/cm): ΔVot unchanged at 50 °C, then −1.5 → −0.35 V by 300 °C (~10% gone by 100 °C); ΔVit grows 1.3 → 2.2 V at 125 °C, then falls; ΔVth −0.2 → +1.1 V at 150 °C. Interface-trap annealing needs >125 °C.
  • 100 °C bake: completes interface-trap buildup (plateau by ~4×10³ s, held for a week) while trapped holes anneal → worst-case positive n-channel threshold. Above 125 °C the test would anneal what it must bound.
  • 77 K: H⁺ drift, thermal emission and interface-trap annealing frozen; hole transport frozen at low and moderate field but field-driven at high field (79 K, 5 MV/cm: 40–96 nm in ~0.015–0.5 s); tunnelling continues. Interface traps barely form and do not recover; frozen processes resume on warming.
  • Levers (Deck 06 p. 48): hydrogen control, optimized oxidation, alternative dielectrics, process modifications; plus thickness and bias. A lower-temperature post-oxidation anneal put traps nearer the interface (faster tunnel annealing, ~10× less shift at 10²–10³ s) but made them switch.
