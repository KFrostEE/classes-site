Module 14

Rad-Hard Circuit Design: Single-Event Effects

How a designer working in a commercial process stops a single ion from becoming an error: by removing its charge before the next stage responds, by filtering out a pulse that is shorter than a real signal, by storing every bit so that one struck node cannot change it, by coding stored data so that flipped bits are corrected, and by giving the system a way back from states no circuit technique prevents. Each technique has its price in power, speed or area, and the module sets the price beside the protection.

Prerequisites M11 (M16 for latchup) Read time ~70 min Sources Deck 10 and its speaker notes; Deck 13; Buchner and Baze, IEEE NSREC 2001 Short Course; lecture on Deck 10; guest lectures on advanced CMOS, sequential circuits and space systems

M11 followed one ion from the charge it deposits to the bit it flips. It ended on two facts that this module turns into design rules. The critical charge belongs to the circuit, not to the ion: it is the node capacitance times the voltage excursion that flips the state, and the designer chooses both. And the charge an ion delivers does not shrink when the transistors do, so every scaled generation that lowers the node capacitance and the supply voltage lowers the critical charge with them. Radiation hardening by design (RHBD) is the practice of building radiation-tolerant circuits in a commercial process, where the designer cannot change the doping, the oxides or the substrate and must work with the circuit topology, the transistor sizes and the layout instead (Deck 10 p. 8). The total-dose counterpart, which deals with trapped charge and leakage, is M13; this module deals with single events only.

§1 states what a strike must achieve to become an error and sorts the techniques by which of those conditions each one breaks. §2 treats charge dissipation, the removal of charge before it reaches the next stage, which the course returned to in three successive lectures. §3 treats temporal filtering in logic and in analog circuits, §4 spatial redundancy from triple modular redundancy to the dual-interlocked storage cell, and §5 the error-correcting codes, interleaving and scrubbing that protect memories. §6 asks where a sequential circuit's errors actually come from, latches or the logic that feeds them, and shows that the answer changes with clock frequency. §7 covers the system level, §8 the new problems of FinFET and nanosheet technologies, and §9 sets the costs side by side.

1 · What a strike must do to become an error

Hardening techniques are easiest to understand by what each one prevents. A strike becomes an error only if a chain of conditions holds, and a technique works by breaking one link. This section states the chain, the relation between charge and node voltage that underlies every link, and the three families of circuit technique that the rest of the module develops.

The four conditions

A single-event transient (SET), the voltage pulse at a struck node of combinational logic (M11 §5), becomes a stored error only if four conditions hold together. Buchner and Baze list them, after Diehl-Nagle, Vinson and Petersen (1984):

  1. the strike produces a voltage transient large enough to propagate into the circuitry around it;
  2. an open logic path, a "critical pipe", connects the struck node to a latch, so that the struck node controls the logic state along the path;
  3. the pulse that arrives at the latch has the amplitude and duration to write it; and
  4. the pulse arrives while the latch is accepting data.

A strike on a storage node itself needs only the first and third conditions in a modified form: the collected charge must hold the node past its switching point until the cell's own feedback has latched the new state, before the restoring transistor pulls the node back (M11 §4). Each family of techniques in this module attacks one or more of these conditions, and Figure 1 sets out which.

Charge, capacitance and voltage

Deck 10 p. 11 puts the whole design problem into one relation. A strike adds charge to or removes charge from a node, and the node's voltage moves by that charge divided by the node's total capacitance: the gate capacitance of the transistors it drives, its own junction capacitance and the capacitance of its wiring. The lecture stressed that most of a logic node's capacitance is gate capacitance.

Δq = C · ΔV
(14.1)
Δq charge added to or removed from the node by the strike (C; fC to pC) · C total node capacitance (F; fF) · ΔV resulting voltage excursion (V). With ΔV set to the excursion that flips the state, Δq is the critical charge, (11.3) of M11. Assumes the charge arrives faster than the node's restoring current can remove it; §2 relaxes that.

The three quantities scale differently, and that is why each generation is more sensitive. The charge an ion generates per micrometre is fixed by its LET and the material; Deck 10 p. 11 writes that charged particles do not scale with technology. The node capacitance falls with feature size, so the same charge produces a larger excursion. And the supply voltage falls too, so the excursion needed to cause a soft error is smaller. Both effects lower the critical charge. The lecture on Deck 10 concluded that without hardening by design, a smaller node is a more sensitive one. The designer controls C, the excursion the circuit tolerates and, through the transistor drive, how long the excursion lasts; the incoming charge is not under the designer's control.

The incoming charge does follow from the ion's LET. Buchner and Baze give a working form in the units that circuit designers use:

Q = 1.03×10⁻² · LET · D
(14.2)
Q charge generated along the collection length (pC) · LET linear energy transfer in silicon (MeV·cm²/mg) · D length of the ion path over which the charge is collected (µm). Equivalent to the rule of M11 that an LET of 97 MeV·cm²/mg generates 1 pC/µm in silicon, since 1/97 = 1.03×10⁻². Generated charge, not collected charge: D stands in for the collection depth of M11 §3.

Three families, and what each costs

Deck 10 p. 10 asks the designer to treat single events as random transient pulses, as if the circuit had to work in a noisy environment, and names three families of technique: charge dissipation, temporal filtering and spatial redundancy. It adds that trade-offs in cost, area, power and speed cannot be avoided. Buchner and Baze attach a characteristic penalty to each family:

  • Charge dissipation removes or absorbs the collected charge so that the node never moves far enough or long enough. It consumes power, through larger currents or larger capacitances to switch. It breaks condition 1 or 3.
  • Temporal filtering makes the circuit ignore pulses shorter than a chosen time. It slows the circuit. On this page's reading it breaks conditions 3 and 4; Buchner and Baze assign it no condition.
  • Spatial redundancy stores or computes each value in more than one place, so that one struck element cannot decide the result. It increases area. It breaks condition 2 unless two elements fail together.

The first two families raise the circuit's threshold LET: a larger strike is needed to cause an error. Spatial redundancy reduces the effective sensitive cross-section instead (Buchner and Baze): the charge needed to upset any one copy is unchanged, but an error now needs a coincidence. Above the circuit sit the architecture and the system: Deck 10 p. 32 lists triple modular redundancy, lock-stepped processors and pipeline protection at the architecture level; error-correcting codes, scrubbing, watchdog resets and latchup protection at the system level; and redundancy, graceful degradation and autonomy at the mission level. Its stated goal is resilience, continued function under radiation-induced faults, rather than hardness alone. Below the circuit sits the process, which Buchner and Baze describe as working on every element at once by reducing the sensitive volume for charge collection: an epitaxial layer over a heavily doped, strongly recombining substrate shortens the collection depth, and silicon-on-insulator (SOI) shortens it in every direction.

A designer can (a) double the capacitance of a sensitive node, (b) triplicate a block and vote its outputs, or (c) insert a delay before a latch. For each, say which of the four conditions it breaks, whether it raises the threshold LET or lowers the cross-section, and what it costs.

(a) Doubling C halves the excursion a given charge produces (14.1), so a strike needs twice the charge to move the node through the same voltage: it breaks condition 1 (and 3 for the pulse that does propagate), raises the threshold LET, and costs speed and power, because every legitimate transition must now charge twice the capacitance. (b) Triplication with voting breaks condition 2: one struck copy no longer controls the output, because the voter follows the two unstruck copies. It does not change the charge needed to upset any one copy, so the threshold LET is unchanged and the cross-section falls instead, since an error now requires two copies to fail together. It costs area, roughly three times plus the voter. (c) A delay chosen longer than the transient breaks conditions 3 and 4 in the form of §3: the latch accepts a value only if it persists, so a short pulse cannot write it. It raises the threshold LET, because only a larger strike makes a long enough pulse, and it costs speed.

2 · Charge dissipation

Three lectures in a row came back to one idea: if the deposited charge is removed before it reaches the node that matters, or before the next stage responds, there is no event. Charge dissipation is the family of techniques that acts on this idea. It works in two ways. More capacitance at the node makes the same charge move the node less. More current from the restoring transistor makes the node recover faster. A third group of techniques acts before the charge reaches the node at all, by giving it another place to go. Deck 10's speaker notes call charge dissipation the first line of defense, because it acts at the device level before circuit or system redundancy is needed.

Node capacitance

Adding capacitance to a sensitive node reduces the excursion of (14.1) for a fixed collected charge, so a pulse that would have crossed the next gate's switching threshold no longer does (Deck 10 p. 13). The required capacitance is the charge divided by the excursion the circuit can tolerate. The lecture noted two properties that make a capacitor suit this job. A capacitor passes a fast transient to ground while leaving the steady logic level untouched. And unlike a clamping diode, it works for either polarity of collected charge, which depends on the state the circuit holds. The added capacitance must sit on the node it protects, since (14.1) involves that node's own capacitance.

An area of this size at every gate is impractical, and Deck 10 p. 13 draws that conclusion for modern designs. Buchner and Baze write that capacitance hardening carries perhaps the greatest relative performance penalties of the charge-dissipation techniques, because every legitimate transition must also charge the added capacitance, but they also rank it the easiest to add to an existing design. They note two places where it pays: analog circuits, where the speed penalty matters less, and clock trees and global control lines, whose capacitance is already large. One capacitor can also filter transients from the logic upstream of it, so it is placed at selected nodes rather than at every gate. Memory designers use the same idea inside the cell: Dodd and Massengill describe capacitors added to the feedback path of SOI and deep-submicron static memories, which slow the cell's response to a strike as resistors do, with less dependence on temperature.

Drive current and transistor upsizing

A struck node does not stay disturbed: the transistor that holds it, the "restoring" transistor, keeps driving it back toward its correct value. In an inverter whose input is low, the p-channel transistor is on and holds the output high; a strike on the drain of the off n-channel transistor collects electrons there and pulls the output low, and the on p-channel transistor recharges the node once the collection is over (Deck 13 pp. 10–12). Deck 10 p. 12 states the time this takes:

Δt = ΔqID
(14.3)
Δt time to remove the collected charge and restore the node (s) · Δq collected charge (C) · ID drain current of the restoring transistor (A). Treats the restoring current as constant and the collection as fast compared with Δt. Deck 10 p. 12 draws the case of a strike that charges the node and an n-channel transistor that sinks it; the relation is the same for either polarity.

The lever is the drain current. In saturation the drain current of a MOSFET is proportional to its width-to-length ratio W/L (M06), so tripling the width at fixed length triples the current and divides the recovery time by three. If the node recovers before the next gate switches, the transient never propagates: Deck 10 p. 12 states that if the transistor can sink the collected charge in time to suppress the transition of the next gate, the SET has no effect. The cost the slide gives is about three times the area. Figure 2 computes the node voltage for a small node struck by a fixed charge, at two drive strengths.

Quiescent current in analog circuits

The same relation holds in analog circuits, where the restoring current is the bias current. Deck 10 p. 14 draws a common-source amplifier whose load is a current sink. A strike that deposits charge on the output node is removed by that sink, so the value of the load current ILOAD sets how quickly the output returns to its operating point. Raising the quiescent current, the steady current that flows when no signal is present, therefore shortens and shrinks analog transients. The price is static power, drawn whether or not a strike ever occurs.

Deck 10 p. 22 turns this into a rule for finding the nodes to protect. Low-gain, low-impedance, high-current nodes resist transients better than high-gain, high-impedance, low-current nodes, because a low impedance means a large current is available to remove the charge and a low gain means a small disturbance is not amplified. The slide recommends single-event transient simulations and measurements to find the most sensitive nodes, and notes that hardening one or two critical nodes may harden the whole circuit. It also warns that the effect is nonlinear: a slight increase in quiescent current or capacitance can raise the transient hardness abruptly, by an order of magnitude or more. The lecture added the practical consequence: finding those nodes takes simulation, a test chip, measurement and redesign, several times over, which is one reason rad-hard parts trail commercial ones by generations.

Giving the charge somewhere else to go

A strike's charge spreads by drift and diffusion before a node collects it (M11 §§2–3). Contacts placed near the sensitive junctions collect part of it first. Deck 10's speaker notes for p. 12 list four such measures:

  • Well and substrate ties hold the wells and substrate at fixed potentials and shorten the path by which carriers leave, so less charge reaches the sensitive junctions.
  • Guard rings, rings of heavily doped diffusion around a transistor tied to a fixed bias, collect excess carriers before they diffuse into the active device regions. M11 §9 gives the simulated size of the effect in a 130 nm process (3-D device simulation, LET 40 MeV·cm²/mg): a contacted guard ring around the struck transistor cut the charge collected by an adjacent p-channel drain in the same n-well by 97 %, and by an adjacent n-channel drain by 35 %, for about 30 % more area.
  • Capacitance balancing, the added node capacitance treated above.
  • Body contacts, which give a low-impedance path for excess charge in the body of the transistor; Dodd and Massengill call careful body-tie design crucial to the upset performance of SOI.

Well engineering is the process-level form of the same idea. Dodd and Massengill call the reduction of charge collection at sensitive nodes the most fundamental method of SEU hardening. They describe extra doping layers that limit collection from the substrate in memories; triple-well and even quadruple-well structures proposed for advanced static memories, in which every strike is in effect an "inside-the-well" strike; and retrograde wells and buried layers whose built-in field opposes the collection of charge deposited in the substrate. A triple well is a p-well enclosed by a deep n-well, so that the n-channel transistors sit in a well isolated from the substrate rather than in the substrate itself; the first lecture of the course named the triple well, the double well and the floating island among the options that design offers. Buchner and Baze add two layout rules: keep every junction, the drains above all, at its design-rule minimum area, and build large transistors as short interdigitated segments rather than one long stripe, which can raise the sensitivity to low-LET particles.

Collection-limiting structures change where the charge goes, not how much is deposited, and a structure that isolates a well also confines what is collected in it. Dodd and Massengill note that a strike in the body of an SOI transistor can trigger a parasitic bipolar mechanism that limits the hardness of SOI circuits, and M11 §9 shows how charge left in a shared well collapses its potential and turns on the neighbouring transistors' parasitic bipolar transistors. Each structure is judged by measurement in its own process, not assumed to help.

With 0.103 pC on an unhardened node, whose capacitance is far below 0.26 pF, the node is still driven to the opposite rail, but tripling ID divides the time it spends there by three. Upsizing protects only if that shorter pulse is then too short for the next stage or the latch to respond, condition 3 of §1, so the answer depends on the minimum pulse width the downstream circuit can respond to.

3 · Temporal filtering

A single-event transient is short: §2 computed 1.65 ns for the design charge on a 0.4 µm logic gate, and Deck 10 puts transients in 7 nm and smaller technologies at tens of picoseconds. A legitimate signal, by contrast, holds its value for at least a clock cycle. Temporal filtering exploits that difference. Deck 10 p. 15 calls it essentially low-pass filtering of SET pulses: the circuit is made to respond only to inputs that persist longer than the transients it must reject. In logic this is done with delay elements, slower storage cells, or several samples taken at different times; in analog circuits it is done by limiting the amplifier's bandwidth.

Filtering in logic

The simplest filter is a resistor between the output of one gate and the input of the next, with the next gate's input capacitance completing a low-pass network (Deck 10 p. 15). The resistor delays the transient so that the first gate's output transistors can sink the collected charge before the second gate's input crosses its switching level. Buchner and Baze size it by equating two times. The first gate needs (14.3) to remove the charge; the input of the second gate, charged through the resistor, takes −ln(½)·RC to reach half the supply. Setting them equal gives the resistance:

QIout1 = 0.693 R Cin2  ⟹  R ≈ 1.44 QCin2 Iout1

Buchner and Baze conclude that this filter would consume substantial area at every gate, and that it belongs at the end of a logic string or in combination with added capacitance or drive current. They also exclude diffused and pinched resistors, because the resistor must not itself collect charge. Deck 10 p. 16 states the same economy for every temporal technique: not every gate needs slowing. Filtering is applied to the "critical pipe", the paths along which a transient is likely to reach a storage element, and identifying them is not easy.

A storage cell can be made into its own filter by lengthening its write time, the time the input must be held for the cell to change state. Deck 10 p. 15 gives the condition: added resistance or capacitance makes twrite ≫ tSET, so a transient ends before it can write the cell. The price is the whole circuit's speed. In Buchner and Baze's 0.4 µm example a 0.25 mA gate output struck with 0.41 pC produces a transient of about 0.41 pC ÷ 0.25 mA = 1.6 ns, against a normal register write time of about 260 ps, so the register's write time must grow about sixfold. In static memories the same technique takes the form of resistors in the cell's feedback loop. Dodd and Massengill point out its cost there: an upset looks to the cell exactly like a write, so the same RC delay lengthens the write pulse, and the polysilicon resistors used are hard to control and make both the write speed and the upset response depend on temperature.

Delay and vote, sample and vote

Voting turns the time difference into a decision. In delay-and-vote hardening (Deck 10 p. 17) the output of a logic block is split three ways: one copy goes directly to a voter, one through a delay, and one through two delays. The voter outputs the value held by at least two of its three inputs. A transient of width tSET on the block's output reaches the three voter inputs at three different times; if each delay exceeds tSET, the copies of the pulse never overlap, at most one voter input is wrong at any instant, and the voter rejects it. For the 1.6 ns transient of the example, each delay must exceed 1.6 ns, 3.2 ns in total on the longest branch.

Sample-and-vote hardening (Deck 10 p. 18) moves the delays to the clock. Three latches load the same logic output on three clocks, the second delayed by 1.6 ns and the third by 3.2 ns, and a voter combines the three stored values. Buchner and Baze point out an advantage: the clock delays can be set by software, so in an environment without single events the three clocks can run together at full speed. Both forms carry the same two requirements. Each delay must exceed the longest transient to be rejected, which makes every number here specific to a technology: the lecture stressed that 1.6 ns and 3.2 ns belong to the 0.4 µm example of 2001, and that transients in current technologies are far shorter. And the voter must itself be hardened (Deck 10 p. 17), since a strike on the voter's own output passes straight through.

Triple sampling quantified

Deck 10 p. 39 counts temporal triple-sampling flip-flops among the latch designs that reduce upset cross-sections significantly, and a probability argument shows by how much. Take one sampling instant and a rectangular transient of width W whose arrival time is equally likely anywhere in an interval T over which the sampling pattern repeats. The sample captures the transient if the instant falls inside the pulse. A pulse no wider than T does so with probability W/T; a pulse wider than T always covers an instant, so in general the probability is min(W, T)/T. When W varies from strike to strike, the probability is its average:

p = ⟨min(W, T)⟩T,    Pvote = 3p²(1 − p) + p³ = 3p² − 2p³
(14.5)
p probability that one sampling instant captures the transient · W transient width (s) · T interval over which the arrival time is uniformly distributed (s): the clock period when one sample per clock is considered; for three samples spaced Δ with no clock period given, the course takes T = Δ, the arrival time uniform over one inter-sample interval · ⟨ ⟩ average over the distribution of W · Pvote probability that a two-of-three voter outputs the wrong value, which needs at least two of the three samples to be wrong. The binomial form assumes the three samples err independently, each with probability p. The same expression gives the failure probability of any two-of-three majority vote over independent inputs, including triple modular redundancy (§4).

The independence assumption is a simplification, and Figure 3 shows what it leaves out. The three instants sample the same pulse, so whether the second sample is wrong depends on whether the first was. When the spacing Δ exceeds the widest transient, no pulse can cover two instants and the true voter failure probability is zero, which is exactly the design rule of delay-and-vote and sample-and-vote. When Δ is shorter than some transients, a pulse of width W between Δ and 2Δ covers two adjacent instants if it starts in a window of length W − Δ before one of the two pairs.

The binomial form of (14.5) is the course's model of a sampled voter. Its assumption is weakest where T = Δ: the samples of one pulse are then strongly correlated, and the answer depends on the interval chosen for the arrival time. The model can err in either direction. When every transient is narrower than Δ it predicts voter errors that the timing does not allow, as the next example shows; when the spacing shrinks toward zero the three samples coincide, the true probability tends to p, and p exceeds 3p² − 2p³ for p below one half. Use the model for the probability it defines, and the timing argument to choose the spacing.

Buchner and Baze describe a "temporal latch" (after Mavis and Eaton, 2000) that combines the two ideas: sampling at three master–slave latches filters transients from the logic, and triple modular redundancy in the data latches that follow protects against upsets of the stored bits themselves.

Filtering in analog circuits

In an analog circuit operating in its linear region, the transient response is fixed by the frequency response, so temporal filtering is the same thing as limiting bandwidth (Deck 10 p. 19). The slide states the consequence: as the gain–bandwidth product falls and the phase margin rises, an amplifier's transient response is damped until a single-event transient has no effect at its output. For the two-stage Miller-compensated operational amplifier of Deck 10 p. 20, whose input stage is a bipolar differential pair, the gain–bandwidth product is set by two quantities:

GBW = gmCC
(14.6)
GBW gain–bandwidth product, the unity-gain angular frequency (rad/s; divide by 2π for Hz) · gm transconductance of the input differential pair (A/V) · CC Miller compensation capacitor (F). For a bipolar pair with tail current Itail, each transistor carries Itail/2 and gm = Itail/(2VT), with VT = kT/q = 25.85 mV at 300 K. Deck 10 p. 20 writes the ratio without the 2π; it is the angular frequency.

The bandwidth can therefore be reduced in two ways: by reducing gm or by increasing CC (Deck 10 p. 20). They are not equivalent, and Deck 10 p. 21 says which to choose. Reducing gm means reducing the tail current of the differential pair, which also reduces the current available to remove a strike's charge, and so degrades the pair's resistance to transients. Increasing CC filters equally well and leaves the current alone; from the viewpoint of both charge dissipation and temporal filtering, the slide concludes, the larger capacitor is the better choice. A larger CC moves the amplifier's dominant pole to a lower frequency, which is what lowers the cutoff; Deck 10's speaker notes for p. 20 describe the same change as "increasing the dominant pole", which reads the wrong way. The slide's final line is a reminder that either choice degrades the amplifier in order to harden it.

4 · Spatial redundancy

Spatial redundancy stores or computes each value in more than one place, arranged so that a single wrong element cannot change the result. It does not make any one element harder to upset; it makes a single upset harmless, so an error requires two elements to fail together. Buchner and Baze put the reduction in cross-section at factors of 10 to 100, depending on how far apart the redundant elements are, for area penalties of two to three times. The family runs from triplicating whole blocks to building redundancy into a single gate or a single storage cell.

Triple modular redundancy

Triple modular redundancy (TMR) implements the same circuit three times and passes the three outputs to a voter that outputs the value at least two of them agree on (Deck 10 p. 23). If one copy is struck and produces a wrong value, the other two outvote it. With each copy wrong independently with probability q, the voted output is wrong with probability 3q² − 2q³, the majority expression of (14.5): a copy error of 10⁻⁶ becomes a voted error of 3×10⁻¹². That quadratic gain is the attraction. Deck 10 p. 23 names the two drawbacks that limit it:

  • An upset at the input is not corrected. A wrong value that enters before the triplication point reaches all three copies, which then agree on it, and the voter passes it as valid.
  • The voter can itself be upset. It is a single point through which every result passes. A voter error of 10⁻⁸ would dominate the 3×10⁻¹² of the copies, so the voter must be hardened by the other techniques of this module.

The quadratic gain also assumes that the copies fail independently, and that depends on layout. Deck 10 p. 24 states that critical nodes and circuits must be physically separated so that the same ion cannot upset two adjacent copies: the design is meant to require two simultaneous upsets. A single track, or the charge sharing of M11 §9, can supply that coincidence if two copies sit close together, and the voter then follows the two wrong copies. Deck 10's speaker notes add guard rings, deep n-wells and isolation trenches as layout aids that confine collection. Buchner and Baze give TMR the greatest penalties of the spatial techniques: about three times the area and power, plus the voter's area and its delay (Table III; Deck 10 notes).

TMR applies at every level from the gate to the system, and the 9 Nov guest lecture showed how its granularity matters in field-programmable gate arrays (FPGAs), where the user implements it on a commercial device. Taking the three names in their usual sense, in FPGAs whose configuration is held in static memory, distributed TMR, which triplicates the logic and votes at intermediate points, is the preferred compromise between efficiency, complexity and area; local TMR, which triplicates only the storage elements, can have an error cross-section similar to or even worse than the unmitigated circuit there and should be avoided; and global TMR, which triplicates everything including the clocks, is too complex because of clock skew. In flash-based FPGAs, whose configuration memory is non-volatile and largely immune to single events, local TMR suffices at low LET. For software executed repeatedly on one processor core, the same lecture stated the rule that also governs copies in hardware: with two executions an error can only be detected; with more than two it can be detected and corrected.

Dual-stream logic

Dual-stream redundancy builds the redundancy into each gate (Wiseman, 1994; Deck 10 pp. 26–27). Each logic function is duplicated in a p-channel and an n-channel version with two outputs, POUT and NOUT. In the two-input NAND of Figure 6, POUT is driven by the two parallel p-channel pull-ups and NOUT by the two series n-channel pull-downs. Two cross-coupled, weakly driving transistors complete each output for the opposite level: a p-channel transistor, gated by NOUT, pulls POUT down to a weak "0", and an n-channel transistor, gated by POUT, pulls NOUT up to a weak "1". Deck 10 p. 26 labels POUT strong "1", weak "0", and NOUT strong "0", weak "1". The outputs feed the next stage separately: POUT drives only p-channel gates and NOUT only n-channel gates, which Buchner and Baze describe as connecting p-channel outputs only to p-channel inputs and n-channel outputs only to n-channel inputs.

The protection follows from which diffusions each node touches. Every transistor terminal on POUT is a p⁺ diffusion: the drains of the pull-ups and the source of the weak p-channel pull-down; the n-channel transistor it connects to sees it only at its gate. A strike on a p⁺ diffusion collects holes, which raise the node voltage. POUT can therefore only be pulled up, and NOUT, which touches only n⁺ diffusions, can only be pulled down. Deck 10 p. 27 and Buchner and Baze state the result: POUT can only upset "high", NOUT can only upset "low". Now follow a strike into the next stage. If the output is "0", POUT and NOUT are both low, the next p-channel transistor is on and the next n-channel transistor is off. A strike can only push POUT high, which turns the next p-channel transistor off as well: the next stage's output is then driven by neither network and holds its previous value on its capacitance, instead of switching to the wrong one. The mirror case holds for a "1" output and a strike on NOUT. One strike can make a following stage float briefly; it cannot make it switch. A wrong logic value needs both streams to carry the wrong level at once (Deck 10 notes: both outputs must fail simultaneously), and a strike on either output node alone cannot produce that, because each node can be moved in one direction only.

Deck 10's speaker notes for p. 27 describe POUT's upset as a pull from "1" toward "0", the opposite of the slide's own label. The slide, Buchner and Baze's figure of the same gate, and the diffusion argument above agree that POUT can only be pulled up and NOUT only down; the slide is the version used here. The technique has costs that the slide calls significant in speed and area, and it requires careful transistor sizing so that the weak cross-coupled transistors restore the outputs quickly enough without fighting the strong ones. Buchner and Baze estimate about twice the area, power and delay of standard logic, and judge it the least costly spatial technique, but one that requires a new logic-cell library and architecture.

The dual-interlocked storage cell (DICE)

The DICE cell extends the dual-stream idea to storage (Calin, Nicolaidis and Velazco, 1996; Deck 10 p. 28). An ordinary static memory cell holds a bit on two nodes in a single feedback loop, and a strike that flips one node flips the bit (M11 §4). The DICE cell holds the bit on four nodes, arranged as two latches whose outputs drive each other's inputs: latch 1's POUT and NOUT become latch 2's PIN and NIN, and the reverse. Deck 10 p. 28 states the consequence: a transient in either latch is damped out by the other, and both latches must be upset at the same time to lose the data. A strike that disturbs one node drives the nodes it controls only partway, because each of those nodes is also held by an undisturbed node of the other latch; when the collection ends, the undisturbed nodes restore the struck one. Dodd and Massengill note that hardened memory cells of this kind use 12 to 16 transistors against the six of a standard cell, and Deck 10's speaker notes for p. 29 put a DICE flip-flop at about two to three times the area of a standard D flip-flop.

The DICE cell's weakness is the same coincidence that limits TMR. One track that delivers charge to sensitive nodes of both latches upsets them together, and charge sharing supplies exactly that in a dense layout: M11 §9 describes the upsets of a 130 nm DICE latch at low LET traced to charge sharing between its node pairs. The response is to separate the sensitive node pairs in layout, at a further cost in area, and Deck 10 p. 35 records that the classical DICE latch, still the baseline hardened latch, faces scaling limitations below 16 nm.

Isolation offers spatial redundancy at the device level. Deck 10 p. 30 notes that SOI gives every transistor its own isolated body, which reduces the charge-collection volume and permits gate designs in which a transient propagates only if two separate p-channel or n-channel transistors are struck at once; the slide adds the condition that leakage near the buried oxide be controlled to keep the total-dose hardness (M08 §7), and the lecture added that the buried oxide's isolation also impedes heat removal. Buchner and Baze's biased-well inverter applies the same principle at the gate level: series isolation transistors in separately biased wells pass the drive signal but block an upset in the drive transistors, so at least two transistors must be upset. It costs speed, through the load of the added series transistors, and about two to three times the area, and it needs a process that can form separately isolated p- and n-wells, for which their example is SOI.

In a dual-stream inverter chain, the gate's output is "1". A heavy ion strikes the drain of the n-channel pull-down transistor on NOUT. What happens to NOUT, to the next stage's two transistors, and to the next stage's output? Why would the same strike on POUT do nothing?

With the output at "1", the p-channel pull-ups drive POUT high and the weak n-channel transistor holds NOUT at a weak "1". The struck n⁺ drain collects electrons, which pull NOUT down. In the next stage, NOUT drives only the n-channel gate: that transistor turns off. POUT is still high, so the next stage's p-channel transistor is also off. Neither network drives the next stage's output, which floats and holds its correct value on its capacitance until NOUT recovers; it does not switch. A strike on POUT in the same state could only push it higher (holes collected on p⁺ diffusions raise the voltage), and POUT is already at the supply, so nothing changes. An error needs both streams to carry the wrong level at once; a strike on either output node alone cannot do that, because each node can be moved in one direction only.

5 · Error-correcting codes, interleaving and scrubbing

Memories are the largest and most numerous storage elements on a chip, and making every cell a hardened cell costs too much area. Codes protect them instead: extra bits stored with each word let the circuit detect and correct a flipped bit when the word is read. Dodd and Massengill describe error detection and correction (EDAC) as a system-level approach whose overhead can be nonnegligible, but which is sometimes the only method available when susceptible parts must be used, and the IEEE NSREC 2025 Short Course notes that most modern complex devices include it. A code is limited by how many bits it can correct in one word, so its partners are interleaving, which keeps one strike from flipping several bits of one word, and scrubbing, which keeps errors from accumulating.

How a code corrects a bit

A parity bit makes the number of 1s in a stored word even (or odd); a single flipped bit changes that count and is detected, but not located. Hamming codes (Hamming, 1950) add r check bits, each the parity of a different subset of the data bits, chosen so that the pattern of failed checks, the syndrome, names the flipped bit. For a single-error-correcting (SEC) code, the syndrome must distinguish every bit position plus the case of no error:

2r ≥ k + r + 1
(14.7)
r number of check bits for single-error correction · k number of data bits per word. A single-error-correcting, double-error-detecting (SEC-DED) code adds one more, overall parity bit: r + 1 check bits in all. Smallest r + 1 for SEC-DED: 6 bits for 16 data bits, 7 for 32, 8 for 64, giving the (72,64) SEC-DED codes used in many memories. Hsiao (1970) gave a construction of such codes, with odd-weight columns in the parity-check matrix, that reaches the same distance with the same number of check bits and no separate overall parity bit.

The code's power is set by its minimum distance, the smallest number of bits in which two valid codewords differ. A Hamming SEC code has distance 3: a single flip leaves the word closer to the original codeword than to any other, so it is corrected; two flips can leave it closer to a wrong codeword, so it is miscorrected. The extra parity bit of SEC-DED raises the distance to 4, so a double flip is recognized as uncorrectable instead of being miscorrected. The guest lecture's overview of codes describes the Hamming (7,4) code, four data bits and three check bits, as enabling SEC-DED; (7,4) satisfies (14.7) with equality and is SEC only, and the SEC-DED version is the extended (8,4) code with one more parity bit. The storage overhead of SEC-DED falls with word length: 8 check bits are 12.5 % of a 64-bit word, against 200 % for storing the word three times.

Other codes serve other error patterns (9 Nov guest lecture): cyclic redundancy checks detect burst errors without correcting them; Reed–Solomon codes work on multi-bit symbols of b bits, so a burst of up to b + 1 consecutive bit errors corrupts at most two symbols, which suits the burst errors that single-event functional interrupts cause in dynamic memories; Bose–Chaudhuri–Hocquenghem (BCH) codes are prized for compact, power-efficient syndrome decoders; and low-density parity-check codes give high correction capability with simpler decoding than Reed–Solomon or BCH, which suits high-speed memories. All of them cost more check bits and decoder logic than a SEC-DED Hamming code.

A code corrects random, independent bit flips that are few per word. That is the signature of single-event upsets, and it is why the course stressed that error correction protects against single-event upset but not against total ionizing dose. The reason, on this page's reading, is that total dose acts gradually on every transistor at once, through threshold shifts and leakage (M08): it degrades the cells, the peripheral circuits and the code circuitry together instead of flipping a few random bits. A code also cannot clear an interrupted state: a latchup needs a power cycle and a persistent functional interrupt a reset or power cycle, although a symbol code such as Reed–Solomon can correct the burst of data errors that a functional interrupt leaves in a dynamic memory.

Multiple-cell upsets and interleaving

SEC-DED corrects one flipped bit per word. A multiple-cell upset (MCU), one strike flipping several physically adjacent cells (M11 §§1, 9), defeats it if the adjacent cells belong to the same word. Deck 10 p. 37 notes that MCUs grow with scaling and with three-dimensional integration, because of smaller cell spacing and stronger coupling, and lists the mitigation: error correction with interleaving, which disperses the spatial correlation, layout separation, and hardened cell design. Bit interleaving assigns physically adjacent cells to different logical words. With an interleaving factor of four, as in Figure 8, the bits of word A sit in every fourth column, so a strike that flips up to four adjacent cells puts at most one wrong bit in each of four words, and SEC-DED corrects all four. The interleaving factor must be at least the span of the largest MCU that is to be corrected.

Choosing an interleaving factor

Given A memory protected by SEC-DED records 3.0 upset events per day in its environment. Of these events, 75 % flip one cell, 18 % two adjacent cells of a row, 5 % three and 2 % four (an assumed distribution). Find the number of events per year that leave a word with more than one wrong bit, for interleaving factors of 1, 2 and 4, and the smallest factor that removes them.

Plan. With interleaving factor I, consecutive cells of a row belong to I different words in turn, so an event spanning s cells puts ⌈s/I⌉ wrong bits into at least one word. SEC-DED corrects one bit per word, so a word with two or more is uncorrectable. Events that straddle the end of a word's cells are ignored.

Events per year. 3.0 × 365.25 = 1096.

I = 1. Every multi-cell event puts all its bits in one word: 18 + 5 + 2 = 25 % of events, 274 per year.

I = 2. A two-cell event gives one bit to each of two words, both corrected. A three-cell event gives two bits to one word, and a four-cell event two bits to each of two words: 5 + 2 = 7 %, 77 per year.

I = 4. No event spans more than four cells, so no word receives more than one bit: none.

274, 77 and 0 events per year; the smallest factor is 4, the span of the largest MCU, as the rule above states. Check: what remains at I = 4 is second order, two independent events landing in the same word before it is read and corrected, which is what scrubbing addresses below.

The same counting applies to a single read. Suppose that on one access a word holds a single-bit upset with a small probability ps and a two-cell MCU with a small probability pm. Without interleaving the word is uncorrectable with probability ≈ pm, to first order: the MCU term sets the result. With interleaving at least as wide as the MCU, the MCU contributes no uncorrectable word at first order, and what remains is the coincidence of two independent events in one word. For a per-word event probability r ≪ 1 that coincidence has probability about r²/2 (the Poisson term used for scrubbing below), with r ≈ ps + 2pm, since each interleaved two-cell MCU now delivers one bit to each of two words; MCUs wider than the interleaving factor add to it. Interleaving turns a first-order failure into a second-order one, the same change that TMR makes for logic.

The guest lecture on space systems showed how much of a modern memory's event rate MCUs carry. In measured data on a 5 nm static memory, about 83 % of events at an LET of 2.19 MeV·cm²/mg flipped one bit, and about 8 % at 85.76 MeV·cm²/mg, where the plotted events reach 12 bits (the chart's legend provides for up to 16). The same lecture recommended interleaving of the cells within memory blocks against multiple-bit upsets in dynamic memories, and Reed–Solomon coding against the bursts of errors that functional interrupts cause there.

Scrubbing

A code corrects an error only when the word is read, and a word that is rarely read can collect a second upset before the first is removed. Scrubbing prevents this: the memory is periodically read, corrected and written back, so that any correctable single-bit error is repaired before a second one joins it (IEEE NSREC 2025 Short Course, Part IV-A). Deck 10 p. 33 lists memory scrubbing with error correction among the system-level strategies, for memories and for the configuration memory of FPGAs. The scrub rate sets the window in which two upsets must coincide, and a short derivation gives the mean time to failure.

Let a memory hold N words of M stored bits, each bit upset at a rate λ, and scrub every word at a rate ν, so that each word is cleaned every T = 1/ν. In one interval a word receives on average MλT upsets; when that is small, the probability of two or more is (MλT)²/2. The word therefore fails at a rate (MλT)²/(2T) = M²λ²/(2ν), and N independent words fail N times as often:

MTTFno ECC = 1λ N M,   MTTFECC ≈ 1λ(M + C)π2N,   MTTFECC+scrub ≈ 2νN M² λ²
(14.8)
MTTF mean time to the first uncorrectable error (s, or the time unit of λ) · N number of words · M bits per word · C check bits per word · λ upset rate per bit (s⁻¹) · ν scrub rate per word (s⁻¹). The first two forms are as tabulated in the IEEE NSREC 2025 Short Course (Part IV-A, after its ref. [20]); the second is the mean time until some word, never scrubbed, receives its second upset. The third is the derivation above, with M taken as all stored bits including the check bits. Valid when MλT ≪ 1.

The short course prints the third entry as 2ν/(Mλ²N²) while defining N as the number of words and M as the bits per word, so with its own definitions the powers are interchanged relative to the derivation. The likely cause is notation: the result goes back to Saleh, Serrano and Patel (1990), and later reviews tabulate it with M counting words and N bits per word, in which notation the printed form is the derived one. The derivation's dependence is the physically required one: words fail independently, so doubling the number of words halves the time to the first failure, while doubling the word length doubles both the chance of a first upset and the chance of a second in the same word. The short course's stated conclusion holds for either form: the MTTF falls with the square of the upset rate and rises linearly with the scrub rate, so a radiation test at high flux must raise the scrub rate faster than the flux to keep the memory consistent.

An orbit change raises a memory's per-bit upset rate tenfold. By what factor must the scrub rate change to keep the same mean time to failure with SEC-DED, and why does interleaving not remove the need for scrubbing?

With scrubbing, MTTF ∝ ν/λ² (14.8). A tenfold rise in λ divides the MTTF by 100, so the scrub rate must rise a hundredfold to restore it. Interleaving and scrubbing remove different second errors. Interleaving keeps the bits of one strike out of a single word, so an MCU no wider than the interleaving factor is never a double error. Scrubbing keeps two separate strikes, at different times, from accumulating in one word. A memory with interleaving but no scrubbing still fails when a rarely read word collects two independent upsets, at the rate of the second form of (14.8).

6 · Where a sequential circuit's errors come from

Hardening costs area, power or speed, so it should go where the errors originate. A sequential circuit, logic feeding flip-flops on every clock, has two sources: a strike on a flip-flop's own storage nodes, and a transient in the logic that the flip-flop captures. The guest lecture on Deck 13 showed that their relative size changes with clock frequency, so the right place to harden depends on how fast the circuit runs. This section builds the cross-section of each source and the frequency at which they are equal.

Latch upsets and logic upsets

A D flip-flop is two latches in series, a primary (master) stage and a secondary (slave) stage, each holding its value in a loop of two inverters while its input is closed off by the clock (Deck 13 pp. 13–19). Its next state after the rising clock edge equals the D input just before the edge. A strike on a holding latch produces a transient on one inverter output; if the transient has enough amplitude and width, the loop feeds it back and stores it, and the wrong value persists until the next write (Deck 13 p. 20). The guest lectures gave the timing form of the condition: a transient becomes a persistent upset when it is wider than the time the feedback loop takes to complete. Transient widths are conventionally measured at half the supply voltage.

A strike in the logic upstream instead produces a transient that travels to the flip-flop's input, including the transparent stage of the flip-flop itself, which behaves as part of the logic. Whether it is stored depends on its width and on when it arrives relative to the setup and hold window, the interval around the clock edge in which the input must be steady to be written correctly (Deck 13 pp. 23–28). A higher clock frequency opens that window more often, so logic upsets become more frequent as the clock speeds up, while a latch upset, which needs no clock edge, does not.

Cross-sections and the three maskings

Deck 13 defines the single-event cross-section of a latch the way M11 §6 does, normalized per flip-flop (p. 21) or per test block (p. 48) because tests use many copies for statistics:

σlatch = number of errorsΦ · NFF,    σlogic = ∑nodes ∑tSET A · E · L · T

Each masking factor is the probability that one of the four conditions of §1 holds. Deck 13 pp. 29–34 defines them:

  • Logical masking occurs when no logically sensitized path connects the struck gate to an output or flip-flop. A NAND gate whose other input is "0" outputs "1" whatever its struck input does, so a transient on that input stops there. It depends on the input pattern and is estimated by fault simulation.
  • Electrical masking occurs when the transient is attenuated as it propagates along a sensitized path, because each gate it passes reshapes it according to its drive strength. It is estimated by circuit simulation over a range of deposited charge, and matters only where a sensitized path exists.
  • Temporal masking occurs when the transient does not overlap the latching window. It depends on the clock frequency and is the same for all gates of a logic block for transients of the same width.

The guest added that the first two tend to go together: a gate deep in the logic, far from any flip-flop, is both more likely to be logically masked and more likely to be electrically attenuated, while a gate near a flip-flop input is exposed to both. The threshold frequency

The circuit's total cross-section is the sum: a constant latch term plus a logic term that rises with frequency (Deck 13 p. 37). The frequency at which the two are equal is the threshold frequency. Below it latch errors dominate and hardening the flip-flops pays; above it logic errors dominate, and hardening only the flip-flops may fail to meet the specification (Deck 13 pp. 38, 45). Changing the latch design moves the flat line up or down; changing the logic, through logical masking, sizing or drive current, moves the slope; either moves the threshold (pp. 39–44).

Is hardening the flip-flops enough?

Given A pipeline stage has a latch cross-section of 3×10⁻⁹ cm² per flip-flop, independent of frequency, and a logic cross-section of 4×10⁻¹⁰ cm² per flip-flop at 200 MHz, proportional to frequency. The measured threshold frequencies have fallen with scaling. In 28 nm planar CMOS the lines extrapolated from data up to 500 MHz cross at about 1.6 GHz for an inverter chain and below 1 GHz for a comparator (Deck 13 p. 36). Test chips of D flip-flop shift registers with seven minimum-size sensitive nodes in the logic, at nominal supply and high LET, gave about 550, 335 and 230 MHz for 16, 7 and 5 nm bulk FinFETs (Deck 13 p. 51; values read from the plot). The threshold also depends on the particle: in the 5 nm flip-flop it is about 230 MHz for a heavy ion of 35 MeV·cm²/mg but about 18 MHz for alpha particles (p. 52). For alpha particles both terms fall, the logic term by about two orders of magnitude, since lower LET gives shorter transients, and the latch term by about three, so the crossing moves down (values read from the plot); and it depends on the supply voltage in a way the deck calls a complex function of many parameters that must be established empirically (pp. 53–55). Since low-LET particles are far more numerous in most environments, the guest concluded that logic must be hardened as well as flip-flops in these technologies. The 9 Nov guest lecture, showing the same 16, 7 and 5 nm data, drew a different conclusion: logic errors have not been as dominant as expected, and with processor clock frequencies stagnating they are unlikely to dominate a chip's error rate, though a higher operating frequency would raise their contribution.

Deck 13 p. 35, for a 40 nm technology, states that the flip-flop cross-section is 2 to 8 times the logic cross-section at 1 GHz. The points plotted on the same slide give a flip-flop chain at about 1.9×10⁻⁹ cm² per bit and logic circuits between about 0.45×10⁻⁹ and 1.25×10⁻⁹ at 1 GHz, ratios of about 1.5 to 4, with wide error bars. The slide's text is the course's statement; the plotted data support a narrower range. Either way the flip-flops still dominated at 1 GHz in 40 nm, which is the contrast with the sub-gigahertz thresholds of the FinFET nodes.

Measuring the logic term requires circuits that run at gigahertz rates under the beam. Deck 13 p. 47 describes identical logic-and-flip-flop stages chained as a shift register with on-chip clock generation, error detection and error counting, because external pads cannot carry such clocks. Low frequency yields the latch cross-section; high frequency yields latch plus logic.

A designer measures a flip-flop design at 5 MHz and at 1 GHz and finds nearly the same cross-section. A second design, with half the latch cross-section, shows a cross-section at 1 GHz three times its 5 MHz value. Which design has the lower threshold frequency, and what does the second result say about where its errors come from at 1 GHz?

For the first design the 1 GHz value is nearly the 5 MHz value, so the logic term is small even at 1 GHz: its threshold frequency lies well above 1 GHz. For the second, the total at 1 GHz is three times the latch term, so the logic term is twice the latch term there; since the logic term is proportional to frequency, it equals the latch term at about 500 MHz, which is its threshold frequency. The second design has the lower threshold, partly because its latch was hardened. At 1 GHz two-thirds of its errors come from the logic, and further latch hardening would at best cut its total by a third.

7 · The system level

No combination of device and circuit techniques prevents every error, and some errors leave a system in a state from which it does not recover on its own. Deck 10 pp. 31–34 closes its treatment of single events at the system level, and the 9 Nov guest lecture organized its whole treatment of commercial parts in space around the levels at which mitigation is applied. The aim at this level shifts from preventing upsets to continuing to operate despite them.

The hard reset and the watchdog

Deck 10 p. 31 asks whether an upset or a latchup can push a digital state machine, such as a microprocessor, into an unrecoverable error state, and whether a transient or latchup can push an analog circuit into an unrecoverable but nondestructive operating state. Both are possible, and standard digital test vectors rarely exercise such states, because they were never designed in. The slide's answer is a failsafe ability to power the whole mixed-signal system down and up, a hard reset, which clears every stored state at once. The event that typically requires it is the single-event functional interrupt (SEFI), defined in the JESD57 test standard as a nondestructive interruption, caused by a single ion strike, that makes a component reset, hang, or enter a different operating condition or test mode (9 Nov guest lecture). In dynamic memories and flash, the same lecture distinguished SEFIs that clear on the next access or by software housekeeping from persistent ones that only a reset or a power cycle removes.

A watchdog timer makes the reset automatic (9 Nov guest lecture). It counts down from a preset value, and the software resets it periodically as a health check. If an upset disrupts execution or freezes the processor, the software stops refreshing the timer, the timer expires, and its expiry triggers a reset, or signals an external supervisor that resets or power-cycles the processor. The watchdog detects a loss of normal execution: a hang, a stuck state, or control-flow corruption such as an unexpected program-counter value. Silent data corruption, a wrong result produced by a processor that keeps running normally, needs the redundancy of the next paragraphs (IEEE NSREC 2025 Short Course, Part IV-A).

Deck 10 p. 32 states that device-level hardening alone is not enough, because it reduces sensitivity without preventing all events, and assigns techniques to levels. The 9 Nov guest lecture gives the corresponding view from the user of a commercial part:

Several of these are the circuit techniques of §§3–5 applied to larger blocks. Lock-step operation duplicates each instruction on more than one processor core and compares or votes the results (9 Nov guest lecture). In fine-grain lock-step the cores are fully synchronized and compared every cycle; in coarse-grain lock-step they run independently and are compared periodically; on a disagreement, execution returns to a checkpoint. A voter in hardware can itself be hardened, while a voter in software is exposed to the same events as the code it checks. Temporal redundancy in software, running a task several times on one core and comparing, costs little hardware but much execution time. The rule of §4 applies: two executions detect, three or more correct.

Latchup belongs here because the response to it is a system response (M16 §3): the current is limited and the power is cycled before the struck structure is damaged. Deck 10 p. 33 adds subsystem isolation, which limits a latchup's effect by separating power domains and signal lines. The 9 Nov guest lecture described the latching current limiter, which protects a power bus by cutting it on overcurrent, and two of its limitations: it can miss micro-latchups, because an FPGA's consumption is dynamic and their current step is hard to tell from it, and it can trip falsely on the normal current steps of a component, such as an FPGA rewriting its configuration memory. Deck 10 p. 34 lists current limiting with automatic recovery for spacecraft power and mixed-signal circuits, and among its flight examples a quad-core processor that uses lock-stepped cores and on-chip fault detection.

The system view also explains why system error rates rise even when the rate per circuit stays roughly constant across technology nodes. The 9 Nov guest lecture quoted an estimate (after Bhuva, 2015): each system built from thousands of integrated circuits, each circuit unhardened at 10 000 FIT; taking the thousands as 1000 circuits gives 10⁷ FIT, one failure per 10⁹ h ÷ 10⁷ = 100 hours, a soft error about every four days (FIT as defined in M11 §8). More circuits per system multiply the rate even at a fixed rate per circuit, which is why system-level detection and recovery are not optional.

A spacecraft computer occasionally stops responding after a heavy-ion event and recovers only when power is cycled from the ground, hours later. Its memory already has SEC-DED and scrubbing. Name the event, explain why the existing protection cannot help, and propose the two-part system change that restores autonomous recovery.

A hang that needs a reset or power cycle is a single-event functional interrupt (or, if the current also rose, a latchup). The code and scrubbing correct flipped bits in stored words; they do nothing for a control register or state machine driven into a state it cannot leave, which is not a memory error. The change is a watchdog timer refreshed by the software as a health check, whose expiry triggers a reset, combined with an external supervisor, hardened or radiation-tolerant, that can power-cycle the computer when a reset is not enough; if latchup is possible, a current limiter that removes power on overcurrent. Recovery then takes the watchdog period instead of hours.

8 · Hardening at FinFET and nanosheet nodes

The techniques of §§2–5 were developed in planar technologies of micrometre to hundred-nanometre dimensions. Deck 10 pp. 35–39 and the guest lecture on advanced CMOS describe what changes in FinFET and gate-all-around technologies: the critical charge keeps falling, the charge collected by a strike no longer falls with it, single strikes reach more nodes, and new failure modes appear. Some of the classical techniques lose effectiveness as a result.

Cross-sections no longer fall with scaling. The move from planar transistors to FinFETs cut the per-bit cross-section considerably at LET below about 10 MeV·cm²/mg, because a fin presents a much smaller collection volume, but only weakly at high LET (M11 §9). Below 7 nm the trend reversed. Deck 10 p. 36 states that 5 nm upset cross-sections can be larger than 7 nm ones at the same LET, and that in 7 nm bulk FinFETs the cross-section depends strongly on the supply voltage and the threshold-voltage option; M11 §9 records where that paper's data qualify the slide. The guest lecture gave two equivalent explanations: the critical charge fell while the collected charge stayed about the same, or, seen from the circuit, transients widened faster than the feedback loops sped up. A threshold-voltage option acts on both sides of that race: it sets the restoring transistor's drive current, which sets the transient width through (14.3), and it also sets the delay of the latch's feedback loop, so the net effect depends on which changes more (M11 §9). Deck 10 p. 36 concludes that scaling does not guarantee better hardness: a smaller feature size is not a mitigation.

Multiple-cell upsets and micro-latchup. Dense FinFET memories show MCUs from charge sharing and diffusion, more than one bit flipped per strike, and the probability grows with scaling and with three-dimensional integration (Deck 10 p. 37). The same slide reports a new event in the single-event taxonomy, the micro-latchup (micro-SEL): a localized, latchup-like event triggered by parasitic paths between adjacent fins or wells. Its supply-current step is small against the large step of classical latchup (M16 §3), which is why a current limiter can miss it (§7). Deck 10 p. 37 lists three mitigations: error correction with interleaving (§5), layout separation and isolation wells, and cells with enclosed-layout transistors or guard rings that suppress the spread of charge.

Nanosheets and forksheets. Gate-all-around transistors replace the fin with stacked horizontal sheets of silicon surrounded by the gate; the forksheet places two such stacks against a dielectric wall. Deck 10 p. 38 reports a critical charge below 1 fC, new charge-collection geometries, and early data showing transients of about 10 to 50 ps, shorter than in older technologies but still long enough to be latched, with shallow-trench isolation and coupling between fins dominating the upset physics. These are the slide's own values: the paper its notes cite for them is a total-dose study of FinFET shallow-trench isolation and contains none of them, so they await a published single-event source. The deck's closing slide (p. 39) draws the design consequence: transients shorter than 50 ps still exceed latch windows, so pulse filtering, charge-steering latches and joint optimization of bias and layout are needed. The guest lecture on advanced CMOS added that gate-all-around devices are very tolerant of total dose, while their single-event response depends on the implementation and has not proved clearly better than that of FinFETs.

Latches for these nodes. Deck 10 p. 35 keeps the DICE latch as the baseline hardened latch but notes its scaling limits below 16 nm, and states that charge-steering latches in FinFET technologies offer comparable or better upset tolerance for 30 to 50 % less area and power overhead than conventional DICE; the paper the slide cites for this could not be located, so the figure is the slide's. It adds that temporal filtering and redundancy, including sampling-based voting, remain effective at the cost of design complexity and latency, and that layout techniques combined with suppression of charge sharing strengthen any hardened latch. The guest lecture on advanced CMOS added the economic limit: separating nodes costs area that dense designs will not pay, so spatial separation becomes less effective with each node, and interleaving and the pulse quenching of M11 §9 take over part of its role. The same lecture summarized the field: there is no single technique that suffices, and every one has a cost.

A team moving a hardened design from a 7 nm to a 5 nm bulk FinFET process plans to keep the same DICE flip-flops, the same layout spacing in units of the cell pitch, and the same low-threshold-voltage option, and expects the smaller transistors to improve hardness. Give three reasons from this module to expect the opposite.

First, the measured trend has reversed below 7 nm: the critical charge falls while the collected charge does not, so the 5 nm cross-section at a given LET can exceed the 7 nm one (Deck 10 p. 36). Second, keeping the spacing in units of pitch means a smaller absolute distance between the DICE cell's sensitive node pairs, so one track's charge reaches both latches more easily and defeats the cell (§4); Deck 10 p. 35 puts DICE's scaling limit below 16 nm. Third, the logic matters more: the 5 nm threshold frequency is lower (about 230 MHz at high LET, tens of megahertz for alpha particles), so at any gigahertz clock most errors come from the logic, which flip-flop hardening does not touch (§6). In addition, the threshold-voltage option affects the cross-section strongly at 5 nm, so the choice must be re-evaluated rather than carried over.

Two rankings follow. By area, the table's multipliers make triple modular redundancy the most expensive general technique, at three times the circuit plus the voter; Buchner and Baze call it, among the spatial techniques, the one with the greatest penalties and also the easiest to implement, and dual-stream logic the one with the least penalties but the largest change to the design flow. By effectiveness per unit cost, the answer depends on where the errors come from (§6) and on how many nodes are critical (§2): a few critical nodes hardened by capacitance or current, a code over the memories, and a watchdog over the system often achieve what triplicating everything would, at a fraction of the area. The 2001 short course concluded that spatial redundancy is preferred when large improvements are required, its two- to threefold area offset by error rates ten to a hundred times lower; the guest lectures of this course added that no single technique suffices and that each has its cost, so at the newest nodes the techniques are combined at every level.

A radiation-tolerant controller for a low-Earth-orbit instrument, built in a 5 nm FinFET process, runs at 50 MHz, has 8 Mbit of data memory, and has a power budget that cannot accept a threefold increase. Which techniques would you combine, and which would you rule out, and why?

For high-LET ions the 5 nm threshold frequency of §6 is about 230 MHz, so at 50 MHz the logic term is 50/230 ≈ 22 % of the latch term and the flip-flops carry most of those errors: they are the first place to harden, with DICE or another hardened latch laid out with its node pairs separated, at roughly two to three times the flip-flop area but not the whole design. The 8 Mbit memory gets SEC-DED with interleaving at least as wide as the expected multiple-cell upsets, and scrubbing at a rate set from (14.8) and the orbit's upset rate. Full TMR is ruled out by the power budget, since it triples power; it can be kept for a few control registers whose corruption would be catastrophic, with a hardened voter. A watchdog and an external supervisor with current limiting handle hangs, functional interrupts and latchup, which no circuit technique prevents. Temporal filtering of selected critical paths is affordable at 50 MHz, where a nanosecond of added delay is a small fraction of the 20 ns cycle, and it is needed: for low-LET particles, which §6 notes are the more numerous, the measured 5 nm threshold is about 18 MHz, below the 50 MHz clock, so for that population the logic errors exceed the latch errors (50/18 ≈ 2.8 times) and must be evaluated rather than assumed small.

Where this leaves us

Hardening by design starts from (14.1). The charge an ion delivers is fixed by the ion, so the designer works on the capacitance that absorbs it, the current that removes it, the time a circuit takes to respond, and the number of places a value is held. Charge dissipation and temporal filtering raise the LET needed to cause an error at a cost in power and speed; spatial redundancy, from TMR to dual-stream logic and the DICE cell, makes one strike harmless at a cost in area, and depends on layout to keep two elements from failing together. Memories are protected by codes, interleaving and scrubbing, and the system by watchdogs, resets and current limiting for the events nothing else prevents. At nanosheet nodes the critical charge is put below a femtocoulomb, logic transients dominate above a threshold frequency that has fallen to hundreds of megahertz, and single strikes reach several nodes, so the techniques must be combined at every level. M15 turns to how a part's total-dose hardness is assured by test.

Summary

  • Four conditions (Buchner and Baze, after Diehl-Nagle): a propagating transient, an open path, enough amplitude and width to write, arrival in the latching window. Each technique breaks one.
  • (14.1) Δq = CΔV: charge does not scale; C and the tolerable ΔV fall with each node. (14.2) Q[pC] = 1.03×10⁻² LET·D[µm]; LET 40 over 1 µm → 0.41 pC.
  • Charge dissipation: capacitance (0.16 pF, ~80 µm² per node at 2.5 V); drive current Δt = Δq/ID (14.3), ID ∝ W/L, ~3× area; quiescent current in analog (static power); low-impedance, high-current nodes resist; ties, guard rings, triple wells, epi and SOI collect charge before the node.
  • Temporal filtering: RC filter R ≈ 1.44Q/(CinIout) (14.4); twrite ≫ tSET; delay or sample and vote with each delay > the widest transient; critical paths only. Analog: GBW = gm/CC (14.6); raise CC, keep the tail current.
  • Voting (14.5): p = ⟨min(W, T)⟩/T (course: T = Δ), 3p² − 2p³ for independent errors; for sampling, spacing beyond the widest transient makes the vote immune.
  • TMR: triplicate and vote; input errors and the voter are not covered; separate the copies; 3× area and power, the costliest spatial technique. Dual stream: POUT on p⁺ only upsets high, NOUT on n⁺ only upsets low; next stage floats, does not switch; ~2×. DICE: four nodes, two interlocked latches; both must be upset; charge sharing defeats it.
  • Codes: 2r ≥ k + r + 1 (14.7); SEC-DED (72,64); protects against SEU, not TID. Interleave ≥ MCU span (uncorrectable ≈ MCU probability without, second order with); scrub so that MTTF ≈ 2ν/(NM²λ²) (14.8).
  • Sequential circuits: σlogic = ∑∑ AELT (14.9), temporal masking (14.10) ∝ frequency; threshold frequency where logic = latch; FinFET 16/7/5 nm ≈ 550/335/230 MHz.
  • System: hard reset, watchdog, lock-step, current limiting, subsystem isolation; two executions detect, three correct.
  • Advanced nodes: 5 nm can exceed 7 nm; micro-SEL; nanosheets Qcrit < 1 fC, 10–50 ps transients still latchable (slide values); charge-steering latches 30–50 % below DICE overhead (slide value).
