Module 11

Single-Event Effects

What one particle does to a circuit: how its track becomes a current pulse within picoseconds, why a reverse-biased junction collects that pulse, when it flips a memory cell or corrupts a logic signal, how a part's susceptibility is measured as a cross-section and converted into an orbital error rate, why scaling moves the per-bit and per-chip rates in opposite directions, and how the same charge can instead trigger latchup, gate rupture or burnout.

Prerequisites M04, M05, M06 Read time ~55 min Sources Deck 02 pp. 16–22, 27–28; Deck 03 pp. 24–33; Dodd & Massengill (2003); IEEE NSREC Short Courses 2001 and 2024; Lectures 3 and 4

M04 ended with a conversion factor: in silicon, a particle liberates 10.4 fC of charge per micrometre of track for every unit of linear energy transfer. M05 then separated single-event effects from total dose and displacement damage by one property — a single particle causes them, and nothing accumulates. This module follows that single particle through a device on its own timescale, from the femtoseconds in which the charge is created to the nanoseconds in which the last of it is collected, and then outward: into a memory cell, a logic path, a beam test, an orbit and a technology roadmap.

Sections 1 to 3 are the device physics: what the track contains, how it evolves in time and why a reverse-biased junction is the structure that collects it. Sections 4 and 5 are the circuit response — the upset of a storage cell and the transient in combinational logic. Sections 6 to 8 are measurement and prediction: the cross-section, the role of the angle of incidence, and the integral that combines a cross-section with an environment into an error rate. Section 9 applies all of it to scaling, section 10 turns to the events that destroy the device rather than its data, and section 11 to the cases where a single value of LET stops describing the particle at all.

One comparison organises the whole module. On one side sits the collected charge, set by the particle and by the geometry of the struck device; on the other sits the critical charge, set by the circuit. Every section either changes one side of that comparison, measures it, or examines the conditions under which the comparison itself stops being an adequate description.

1 · One track, one event

Every single-event effect runs through the same three stages: charge generation along the particle's track, collection of part of that charge at a circuit node, and the response of the circuit to the charge it did not expect. Deck 03 p. 25 compresses the chain into one line — a single energetic particle, charge deposition, circuit upset — and the rest of this module is an expansion of each link. The stages differ in what controls them. Generation is set by the particle and the material, collection by the device structure and its bias, and the response by the circuit design.

Generation is the physics of M04 §§2–4. A heavy ion slows by Coulomb interaction with the electrons of the lattice, and the energy it loses per unit path length — its linear energy transfer, LET — is converted into electron–hole pairs at one pair per 3.6 eV in silicon. Dividing the energy deposited per micrometre by the pair energy and multiplying by the elementary charge gives the charge per micrometre of track.

With the units the slide itself specifies, the expression evaluates to a charge per micrometre of track; it becomes a charge only when multiplied by a length. The quantity the circuit compares with the critical charge is the collected charge, the part of the deposit that reaches the struck node within the collection depth of §§2–3, and it is smaller than the charge deposited along the whole track. This page keeps the slide's symbol Qcol for the charge per length (pC/µm) and writes Qcoll for the collected charge (C) of (11.4); the two symbols differ by one letter and by their units.

The numbers fix the scale of the problem. An ion of LET 10 MeV·cm²/mg liberates about 100 fC in each micrometre of silicon it crosses. Deck 03 p. 26 notes that the critical charge of modern unhardened circuits can be below 50 fC, equivalent to 3×10⁵ electrons; against such a circuit, half a micrometre of collected track from such an ion is already enough. Heavy ions upset circuits by this direct ionization, and the first single-event upsets reported from space were of this kind: Binder, Smith and Holman (1975) attributed four upsets in seventeen years of operation of bipolar J–K flip-flops on a communications satellite to galactic cosmic rays. A proton's LET is two to four orders of magnitude lower, so protons act mainly through nuclear reactions whose recoils ionize far more densely, although below roughly the 65 nm node direct proton ionization also suffices (M01 §7).

Collection is the step that decides how much of the deposited charge the circuit sees. Only carriers that reach the electric field of a junction, or diffuse to it before recombining, contribute; the rest recombine where they were generated. The collected charge, Qcoll, is therefore smaller than the deposited charge, and it depends on the position and direction of the track relative to the junction as much as on the LET. Sections 2 and 3 are about this step. The circuit response is the third link: the collected charge moves the voltage of the node it arrives at, and whether that disturbance becomes an error is decided by comparing Qcoll with the critical charge Qcrit — a property of the technology and the circuit, not of the ion (M05 §4). Section 4 develops that comparison.

Single-event effects are named by what the circuit does with the charge, and Deck 03 pp. 24–25 sorts them into soft and hard errors on the criterion of M05 §5: a soft error is removed by rewriting the correct state, a hard error is not. A single-event transient (SET) is a voltage or current pulse on a node — in combinational logic it is harmless unless a storage element captures it. A single-event upset (SEU) is a change of stored state in a memory cell, latch or register; it is a single-bit upset (SBU) when one cell flips and a multiple-cell upset (MCU) when one particle flips several. Dodd and Massengill call the second case a multiple-bit upset (MBU); the JEDEC usage reserves MBU for the subset in which the flipped bits belong to the same logical word, which is the case that defeats a code correcting one bit per word. A single-event functional interrupt (SEFI) is a strike that throws a complex part into a test, reset or idle mode, so that it stops functioning until a reset or power cycle restores it, although no stored data bit need have changed. Deck 03 p. 25 counts it among the soft errors: the recovery returns the circuit to a correct state and nothing is damaged.

The hard errors are four. Single-event latchup (SEL) switches on the parasitic four-layer thyristor inherent in bulk CMOS into a self-sustaining low-impedance path between the supply rails. Snapback is a comparable high-current state sustained by a parasitic bipolar transistor together with avalanche multiplication. Single-event gate rupture (SEGR) is the destructive breakdown of a gate dielectric along the track, and single-event burnout (SEB) the thermal destruction of a power device by a regenerative current. Section 10 develops all four.

2 · A strike in time

The three stages of §1 are not simultaneous. Generation is over in about a picosecond, drift collection runs for a few hundred picoseconds, and diffusion collection continues for nanoseconds. The order matters because the circuit responds while the charge is still arriving, and because the first event — the creation of a column of carriers far denser than the doping of the silicon — changes the electrostatics of the junction that then collects them. The IEEE NSREC 2024 Short Course gives the three timescales: generation from a few femtoseconds to a few picoseconds, drift collection including the funnel from a few picoseconds to a few hundred, diffusion for a few nanoseconds or longer. What follows is the sequence for the structure Deck 02 p. 16 draws, an n⁺ region held at a positive voltage above a p-type substrate at ground — the drain of an n-channel transistor in the off state.

Femtoseconds to a picosecond: the column forms

A heavy ion crosses the device almost instantaneously on circuit timescales: at 10 MeV per nucleon it traverses 10 µm in about 0.2 ps (M04 §7). Along its path it transfers energy to lattice electrons, and the more energetic of those — the delta rays — carry part of the energy sideways before they thermalize, so the ionization forms a column with a dense core and a radial extent that for energetic ions reaches some hundreds of nanometres (Musseau and Ferlet-Cavrois, IEEE NSREC 2001 Short Course). Within femtoseconds to picoseconds the excited carriers relax to the band edges and the column is simply a population of electrons and holes in equal numbers. Its density dwarfs the doping: an LET of 10 MeV·cm²/mg liberates about 6.5×10⁵ pairs per micrometre, and spread over a core of order 0.1 µm radius that is of order 10¹⁹ pairs/cm³, against substrate dopings of 10¹⁵–10¹⁷ cm⁻³.

Picoseconds: the plasma wire

A region in which electrons and holes are both present at densities far above the doping is a quasi-neutral plasma, and it conducts. Deck 02 p. 16 labels the column a plasma wire and marks the consequence: "+V appears here". Before the strike the junction potential was dropped across a thin depletion layer beneath the n⁺ region. The column now connects the n⁺ region to the substrate below that layer through a conductor, so the depletion layer is short-circuited along the track and the node's potential is carried down the column into the substrate. The equipotentials that sat parallel to the junction are pulled down along the track — Dodd and Massengill's Fig. 2, which Deck 02 p. 17 reproduces, shows the 3.0 V and 2.0 V contours distorted into the substrate — and the junction field now extends along the column to a depth several times the original depletion width. This transient extension of the field is the field funnel, first identified in numerical simulations of alpha-particle strikes at IBM (Hsieh, Murley and O'Brien, 1981).

The funnel exists only while the column's carrier density exceeds the doping; the column is able to screen the junction field only while it holds more free charge than the dopant ions around it (Buchner and Baze, IEEE NSREC 2001 Short Course). Everything that lowers the carrier density in the column — collection, spreading, recombination — therefore also shortens the funnel.

Tens to hundreds of picoseconds: drift collection

Inside the funnel the field drives the carriers apart. Electrons drift up the column to the positively biased n⁺ node, holes drift down and away toward the grounded substrate contact, and the node sees an electron current. Because drift in a field of order 10⁴–10⁵ V/cm moves carriers micrometres in tens of picoseconds, this current rises within tens of picoseconds. As the column is drained its density falls toward the doping, the field is no longer screened, the depletion layer re-forms at its original width and the funnel collapses. The charge drifted out of the depletion layer itself is called prompt charge; the funnel adds to it by collecting, also by drift, from a depth the undisturbed junction never reaches (§3).

Nanoseconds: diffusion and recombination

Carriers generated below the funnel, or beside it, feel no field. They spread by diffusion, and those that reach the edge of the re-formed depletion layer before recombining are swept across it and collected. Diffusion is slow and has no preferred direction, so this component is a small current lasting nanoseconds or longer, and it may be collected at more than one junction — the mechanism by which one particle upsets neighbouring cells (§9). Whatever is neither collected nor diffused away recombines, and at that point the event is over for the device, though not necessarily for the circuit.

The current pulse

Observed at the node, the sequence is a current pulse with a fast leading edge set by drift, a slower trailing edge set by the draining of the funnel, and a low diffusion tail. Circuit simulators represent the drift-dominated part by a double exponential, the standard model of Messenger (1982) that Dodd and Massengill describe with a rise of tens of picoseconds and a fall of 200 to 300 ps.

What the struck node does

The node is not a fixed-voltage contact. It has a capacitance — the junction itself, the gates it drives, its wiring — and a finite conductance to the supply through whatever transistor is holding it. The collected electron current discharges that capacitance, so a node held at VDD falls while the pulse lasts, and the transistor holding it supplies a restoring current that recharges it once the pulse subsides. Dodd and Massengill describe the node as a low-pass filter with a time constant set by the total node capacitance and the equivalent resistance seen by the current. When that time constant is long compared with the pulse, the node integrates the pulse and responds only to its total charge, and the single number Qcoll of §1 describes the strike completely. When the node is faster than the pulse, it follows the current as it arrives and the pulse shape matters as well as its area.

The node's response also feeds back on the collection. As the struck node's voltage falls, the reverse bias across the junction falls with it, and so do the depletion field and the funnel that depended on it. In a static circuit the struck junction can pass from reverse bias to zero bias during the transient, which suppresses further drift collection. Dodd and Massengill draw the consequence for memory cells: drift, including the funnel, flips the node early, while the slower diffusion component decides whether it stays flipped.

The "plasma wire" is a short circuit across the depletion layer that lasts exactly as long as the track holds more free carriers than the doping around it. For that interval the junction's potential extends down the track, and charge from micrometres below the junction is collected by drift in tens of picoseconds rather than by diffusion over nanoseconds. The rise of the current pulse is the funnel forming; its fall is the funnel draining.

3 · Funnelling and the reverse-biased junction

Everything in §2 required an electric field ready to separate the carriers before they recombine. In a finished device the strong fields sit in reverse-biased junctions, and that is why those junctions are where single events happen. Dodd and Massengill state it as the starting point of charge collection — the most sensitive regions are usually reverse-biased p–n junctions — and Buchner and Baze put it more strongly: significant charge collection occurs only in reverse-biased junctions, which in CMOS means the drain–well and drain–substrate junctions and in bipolar circuits the emitter–base, base–collector and collector–substrate junctions whenever they are reverse-biased. This section explains why, what the funnel adds to the collection, and how a device structure can limit the volume from which charge reaches the junction.

Why reverse bias, and why a high-impedance node

A reverse-biased junction holds a depletion layer that is nearly free of mobile carriers and carries a large field, so any pair generated in or near it is separated within picoseconds and delivered to the contacts as a current that nothing in the circuit requested. The node on the other side of that junction is usually a high-impedance one: the transistor whose drain it is, being off, presents only its junction, and the voltage of the node is whatever charge sits on its capacitance. A current delivered to such a node moves its voltage in proportion to the node's impedance, which is what makes the charge visible to the circuit. This is also why the off state is the vulnerable one: an off transistor's drain is the reverse-biased, weakly held junction, while an on transistor's drain is tied to a rail through a conducting channel.

A forward-biased junction is the opposite case. Its field is small, it already carries a large diffusion current, and it sits in a low-resistance path; the charge a particle adds is conducted away without building a large voltage anywhere. Lecture 3 drew the same contrast and carried it to the destructive limit: a reverse-biased junction in a high-resistance, high-voltage power device has nowhere to dissipate the energy the collected current brings, which is the setting of burnout in §10.

What the funnel adds

Without the funnel, the charge collected promptly is the charge generated inside the depletion layer: the charge per micrometre of (11.1) times the depletion width W. The funnel extends drift collection along the track below W, and the extension is set by how long the track can screen the junction field, which in turn depends on how its carrier density compares with the doping. McLean and Oldham's analytical model of 1982 was an important early quantitative treatment of this length (Dodd and Massengill). The dependences follow from the mechanism. Lighter doping widens the depletion layer, lets the track outnumber the dopants to a greater depth and lengthens the diffusion length, and all three increase the collected charge; heavier doping reverses all three. Buchner and Baze report microbeam measurements on diodes in which raising the doping of the lightly doped side from 2×10¹⁵ to 7×10¹⁵ cm⁻³ nearly halved the width of the current transient, and in which the collected charge rose with applied voltage as the depletion layer widened (after Nashiyama et al., 1993).

Two further mechanisms enlarge the collected charge beyond what one junction receives. When an n-channel transistor sits in a p-well, as in twin- and triple-well processes, the strike's electrons are collected at the drain–well and well–substrate junctions, while its holes remain in the well, raise the well's potential and lower the barrier between the well and the transistor's source; the source then injects electrons that the drain collects in addition to the track charge (Dodd and Massengill). The source, the channel and the drain act as the emitter, base and collector of a parasitic bipolar transistor, and the effect strengthens as the channel, which is its base width, is shortened. In a p-channel transistor in an n-well the roles of electrons and holes are exchanged: the electrons left in the well lower its potential, and the source injects holes. And a track at near-grazing incidence that passes through both source and drain perturbs the channel potential enough to conduct a short source–drain current, the source–drain penetration effect, whose injected charge Dodd and Massengill report rising rapidly below about 0.5 µm effective gate length.

Limiting the collection volume

Every structural route to single-event hardness at the device level works by shrinking the volume from which charge can reach the sensitive junction. A lightly doped epitaxial layer on a heavily doped substrate terminates the funnel at the layer boundary, and carriers generated in the heavily doped substrate recombine quickly; the same heavily doped substrate suppresses latchup (EEE6338 M02 §7). A well isolates: the well–substrate junction is a potential barrier that stops charge deposited deep in the substrate from diffusing back to a drain inside the well, which is why a drain outside the well is the more sensitive location. Triple and quadruple wells make every strike an inside-the-well strike, and retrograde wells and buried layers build an internal field that opposes collection from the substrate (Dodd and Massengill).

Silicon on insulator goes furthest. The transistor is built in a thin silicon film on a buried oxide, the source and drain reach down to that oxide, and the reverse-biased drain junction shrinks to the depletion region between drain and body; charge deposited in the substrate beneath the buried oxide cannot reach the drain (M08 §7). The deposited charge is then fixed by the film thickness tSi, as (11.1) × tSi. Cristoloveanu and Ferlet-Cavrois, in the course textbook, give the compiled result: for the technology generation of about 2000, commercial SOI memories showed a saturated cross-section of about 1 µm² per bit, an order of magnitude below bulk memories of the same generation, with a similar threshold LET. The advantage is not unconditional. Holes left in a floating body raise its potential and turn on the lateral parasitic bipolar transistor, amplifying the collected charge to (1 + β) times the deposited charge, where β is the parasitic gain; body ties that drain the holes suppress most of it, fully depleted films are less sensitive than partially depleted ones because they are thinner and have lower bipolar gain, and capacitive coupling across the buried oxide can still deliver some substrate charge (Dodd and Massengill; Cristoloveanu and Ferlet-Cavrois).

Raising the substrate doping under a junction by a factor of four halves its depletion width. Why does the collected charge from a normally incident ion fall by more than a factor of two?

Because the depletion width is only one of three collection depths, and doping shortens all three. The prompt charge scales with W, which in a one-sided junction goes as the inverse square root of the doping. The funnel lasts only while the track's carrier density exceeds the doping, so a more heavily doped substrate is screened over a shorter length of track. And the diffusion length falls with doping, so less of the charge generated below the funnel reaches the junction before it recombines. The measured transients narrow for the same three reasons.

4 · Critical charge and the memory-cell upset

Section 2 ended at a node whose voltage moves when a strike delivers charge to it. Whether that movement becomes a stored error depends on what is holding the node. A static memory cell holds its state by active feedback and must be driven into the opposite state to fail; a dynamic memory cell holds a packet of charge with no feedback at all and fails as soon as that packet is degraded beyond what its sense amplifier can read. This section develops the critical charge that decides the first case, the race that sets it, the second case, and the probabilistic form the comparison takes when the collected charge is not a single number.

Critical charge

The critical charge is the minimum charge that, delivered to a sensitive node, upsets the circuit (Dodd and Massengill). A node must be moved through a voltage excursion Vcrit before the circuit responds to it, and the charge that moves a capacitance Cn through that excursion is their product. Capacitance is the node's charge store: a larger capacitance must be filled or emptied by more collected charge before the voltage reaches the point where the circuit switches.

They keep the critical charge as a figure of merit for comparing circuit designs and technologies, since it describes vulnerability without reference to ion species, energy or LET — and that is the sense in which it belongs to the circuit.

The static-memory upset: a race between feedback and recovery

A six-transistor static memory cell is two inverters, each driving the other's input, with two access transistors that connect the cell to the bit lines for reading and writing. One internal node is high and the other low. The inverter whose output is high has its p-channel transistor on, tying the node to VDD, and its n-channel transistor off, with the drain of that off transistor reverse-biased at VDD against the grounded substrate. That drain is the struck location Deck 02 p. 17 marks.

When a particle strikes it, the collected electron current flows through the off transistor and pulls the high node down. The on p-channel transistor sources current to replace it, but its drive current and its channel conductance are finite, so the current through it drops a voltage across it and the node falls. The falling node is the input of the opposite inverter. If it falls below that inverter's switching point for long enough, the opposite inverter switches, its output rises, and that output turns the struck inverter's p-channel transistor off and its n-channel transistor on — the cell has latched the wrong state. Dodd and Massengill describe the transient as essentially a write pulse. The outcome is a race between two times: the feedback time, which the disturbed voltage needs to propagate through the opposite inverter and back, and which in its simplest form is the RC delay of the inverter pair; and the recovery time, in which the restoring transistor pulls the node back as the single-event current dies away, set by its drive strength and by the minority-carrier lifetime that governs the diffusion tail.

The struck node's voltage shows the race directly. Dodd and Massengill's simulations of a static memory cell show that even a particle well below the upset threshold drives the struck node through the switching point for a moment; the cell recovers because the restoring transistor wins before the opposite inverter has responded. Just below threshold the node stays low longer and recovers late. Just above, the feedback completes first and the node never returns. Drift, including the funnel, produces the rapid initial fall; diffusion collection prolongs it, and so decides which way the race ends.

Which of the four drains in the cell is sensitive depends on the stored state, and not equally. In a technology with n-wells in a p-type substrate, the off n-channel drain sits in the substrate and collects charge diffusing back from deep in it, which makes that strike location the most sensitive in most technologies; the off p-channel drain sits inside the n-well, whose junction with the substrate blocks that diffusion. Dodd and Massengill's simulated maps of a 256K cell show the sensitive area growing around the reverse-biased n-channel drain as the LET rises, with the reverse-biased p-channel drain becoming susceptible at 33 MeV·cm²/mg. That growth of sensitive area with LET is the shape of the cross-section curve of §6.

The race also shows how to harden the cell: slow the feedback or speed the recovery. The classical method places resistors in the cross-coupling paths, Rfb in Figure 7, which lengthens the RC delay of the loop; Dodd and Massengill cite a microprocessor whose predicted geosynchronous upset rate fell from about once a day to about once a century with resistive hardening. The cost follows from the mechanism — an upset is a write, so the write time lengthens with it — and the polysilicon resistors are hard to control and change with temperature. Adding capacitance to the node raises Qcrit through (11.3) and slows the node at once, with less temperature dependence. Design-hardened cells of 12 to 16 transistors against the standard six, triple modular redundancy and error correction are circuit and system methods for the design modules still to be built (M13 and M14 on the course roadmap).

Dynamic memory: no feedback, no flip needed

A dynamic memory cell stores a packet of charge on a capacitor behind one access transistor. It has no regenerative path and no load that replenishes the charge, so any disturbance a strike causes persists until external circuitry rewrites the cell. A bit flip in the static-memory sense is not required: degrading the stored signal until it falls outside the noise margin of the sense circuitry is enough (Dodd and Massengill). Two parameters describe the vulnerability — the noise margin, which plays the part of a critical charge, and a critical time window, because the circuit's susceptibility changes through its operating cycle. Strikes at or near the storage capacitor or the access transistor's source collect charge from the cell directly; this was the mechanism May and Woods identified at Intel in 1979 in 16K and 64K memories, where alpha particles from radioactive contaminants in the packaging caused the errors. Strikes to the bit lines while they float during precharge and sensing unbalance the read, and because those stages occupy a larger fraction of a faster cycle, the bit-line error rate rises as the cycle time falls. Dynamic-memory makers kept the cell capacitance nearly constant through successive generations, which matters for scaling in §9.

When the collected charge is a distribution

At a given LET, the collected charge is not a single value. Strikes land at different distances from the junction, at different angles and with different track structures, so the charge delivered to the node is distributed around a mean. If that distribution is approximately Gaussian, the probability that a strike at that LET causes an upset is the fraction lying above the critical charge.

5 · Single-event transients

A strike on a node of combinational logic produces the same current pulse and the same voltage excursion as a strike on a memory node, but nothing holds the disturbed state: the node recovers when the pulse ends. The transient does harm only by travelling. It propagates through the gates downstream of the struck node until it reaches a storage element, where it may be captured as a wrong value, or an output, or until it dies away. Single-event transients are therefore a problem of propagation and timing, and they grow in importance as clocks become faster and gates smaller.

Propagation and capture

Dodd and Massengill set out three conditions that a transient must meet to become an error. There must be an active logic path from the struck node to a latch at that instant, which depends on the state of the other inputs of every gate on the way. The pulse must survive the gates it passes, each of which reshapes and delays it. And it must arrive at the latch inside the window around the clock edge — the setup-and-hold time — in which the latch samples its input. When all three hold, the latch stores a wrong value, a soft fault; it becomes an observable error only if that value later reaches the circuit's outputs rather than being overwritten or ignored.

The timing condition gives transients a dependence that memory upsets lack. Baze and Buchner's simulations and laser experiments, as Dodd and Massengill summarize them, found the error rate of latch circuits independent of clock frequency when the setup-and-hold time is much less than the clock period, and the error rate of combinational circuits rising linearly with clock frequency: every clock edge opens a capture window, and a faster clock opens more of them per unit time. Dodd and Massengill also cite a prediction that propagated transients would become a primary single-event failure mode in technologies below 0.35 µm, and add the limiting case: a latch read ten picoseconds after a strike passes the corrupted value on even if the latch itself later recovers.

The capture condition has a simple quantitative form for a transient that decays exponentially from its peak. The pulse remains above the latch's input threshold for a time set by its amplitude, its decay constant and the threshold; a sampling instant that falls inside that interval captures it.

Pulse width, technology and scaling

How long a transient lasts depends on how much charge the struck node collects and how fast the restoring transistor can remove it, so it rises with LET and depends strongly on the collection volume of §3. Deck 02 p. 19 shows the three-dimensional mixed-level simulations of Dodd, Shaneyfelt, Felix and Schwank (2004) for inverters in four generations from 0.25 to 0.1 µm. In bulk CMOS the transient width grows nearly linearly with LET and reaches roughly 700 to 950 ps at 70 MeV·cm²/mg, with no consistent ordering by generation, while the peak current falls with scaling, from about 13 mA at 0.25 µm to about 6.5 mA at 0.1 µm at that LET. In silicon on insulator the widths stay below about 250 ps and fall steadily with scaling, to about 120 ps at 0.1 µm. The buried oxide removes the substrate beneath the transistor, and with it the slow diffusion component that stretches the bulk transient. A transient hundreds of picoseconds long outlasts the switching delay of a gate in these technologies by a wide margin, so the gates it crosses pass it rather than filter it.

Transients are measured, and their sensitive nodes located, with focused beams. An ion microbeam places single ions at chosen positions, and a focused picosecond laser pulse generates carriers at a chosen place and time; both are read out with high-speed sampling oscilloscopes (Dodd and Massengill). A laser pulse of a few picoseconds is longer than an ion's charge generation but shorter than the response of most circuits, which is what lets it stand in for an ion (Buchner and Baze). Its advantages are spatial and temporal selectivity — mapping which nodes produce transients and how they propagate — and its limits are optical access, which metallization and packaging block, a restricted range of angles, and a charge distribution different enough from an ion track that a cross-section is hard to derive from it (IEEE NSREC 2024 Short Course, §4.2). In silicon, light below the bandgap is absorbed only by two-photon absorption where the focused intensity is highest, which confines the generated charge to the focal volume and allows it to be placed at depth. Deck 02 p. 19 shows pulsed-laser transients in germanium-channel p-channel FinFETs (Rony et al., 2021): a 1260 nm laser pulse on the drain produces a drain transient of about 0.18 mA lasting well under a nanosecond, with smaller transients at source, substrate and gate. A 1260 nm photon carries 0.98 eV, below the 1.12 eV bandgap of silicon but above the 0.66 eV bandgap of germanium, so the silicon absorbs that pulse only by two-photon absorption while the germanium channel absorbs it by ordinary single-photon absorption.

Imagers show single events in the image itself. A proton or ion crossing a charge-coupled device leaves charge in the pixels along its track that is collected exactly like signal: single-pixel transients or short streaks, and blooming along the column when the deposit exceeds a pixel's full-well capacity, all gone at the next readout (M08 §9; M12 §6 separates these transients from the permanent hot pixels of displacement damage).

6 · The cross-section

A beam test measures neither the collected charge nor the critical charge. It exposes the part to a known number of particles per unit area and counts what happens. The quantity it returns, the cross-section, is the bridge between the node-level comparison of §4 and the error rate of §8: it summarizes, in one area for each LET, how much of the part responds to that particle. This section defines it, separates it from the other quantities in this subject that carry square centimetres, and shows how its dependence on LET is read and fitted.

Definition and units

The part is exposed to a monoenergetic ion beam of known LET at normal incidence until a fluence Φ — particles per square centimetre of beam — has crossed it, and N events are counted. The cross-section is their ratio. Deck 03 p. 29 writes the denominator as the total number of incident ions; it has to be read as ions per unit area, the fluence, for the ratio to be an area.

σ = NΦ   (cm²/device),     σbit = NΦ · Nbits   (cm²/bit)
(11.7)
σ single-event cross-section of the part, cm² per device · N number of events counted (dimensionless) · Φ fluence, particles per cm² of beam (cm⁻²) · σbit the same normalized to one storage element, cm² per bit · Nbits number of bits exposed. An effective target area: the area that, struck by every particle, would produce the observed count. It is not an LET (MeV·cm²/mg), not a current density (A/cm²) and not a dose (rad). Valid for one ion, one energy, one angle and one bias condition; each point of a cross-section curve is a separate measurement. On the formula sheet as (11.7).

Per device and per bit. The cross-section of a part is an area per device, cm²/device — the unit M05 §4 introduced and the one a part-level measurement reports. Deck 02 p. 20 and memory calculations quote it per bit, cm²/bit, because a memory's rate scales with its size and the per-bit value is what transfers between parts of different capacity. The two differ by the bit count and by nothing else, so every cross-section must say which it is. The bit count itself needs a convention: a "1 Mbit" memory holds 2²⁰ = 1,048,576 bits, and a calculation that takes it as 10⁶ differs by 4.9%.

The cross-section against LET

Measured at several LETs, the cross-section traces a characteristic curve. Below some LET no events occur at all, because no strike deposits the critical charge within the collection depth. Above it the cross-section rises, first steeply and then more slowly, toward a saturated value. Shenai, Galloway and Schrimpf, in the course textbook, identify the two parameters that matter: the threshold LET, which measures the critical charge needed to trigger the event, and the saturated cross-section, which is related to the total area of the sensitive regions. From (11.1) and (11.4), a node of critical charge Qcrit collecting over a depth d has a threshold near Lth ≈ Qcrit/(10.4 fC/µm × d), with Qcrit in fC and d in µm; 10 fC collected over 1 µm, for example, corresponds to about 1 MeV·cm²/mg.

An ideal part — identical nodes, a single collection depth, every strike collected fully — would show a step: zero below threshold, the full sensitive area above it. Measured curves are gradual for three reasons. Nodes differ in critical charge and in collection depth across a die. The sensitive area around each junction grows with LET, since a more densely ionizing track delivers the critical charge from farther away, the growth Dodd and Massengill's cell maps show in §4. And strikes at a fixed LET deliver a distribution of charge, as in (11.5). Deck 03 p. 29 shows curves for 4 Mbit static memories measured with gold, iron and carbon beams from the front and the back. The cross-section rises from an upper limit of about 5×10⁻⁶ cm²/device at 0.5 MeV·cm²/mg, through 10⁻⁴ cm²/device near 0.8, to 4–5×10⁻³ cm²/device over 20–50 MeV·cm²/mg and about 8×10⁻³ cm²/device near 90 MeV·cm²/mg. Divided among the 4 Mbit, 4.19×10⁶ bits, the upper part of the curve is of order 10⁻⁹ cm² — 0.1 to 0.2 µm² — per bit. Those measurements used the variable-depth Bragg-peak method of M04 §3, because charge collection is greatest where the Bragg peak coincides with the sensitive volume. The LET on the abscissa of a cross-section curve is conventionally the surface LET, which assumes the LET is constant through the device; for low-energy accelerator ions, whose LET changes rapidly with depth, the assumption fails, and the collected charge averaged over the collection depth gives a better effective value (IEEE NSREC 2024 Short Course).

The Weibull fit

The measured points are fitted with a cumulative Weibull function, the form CREME96 takes as input for rate calculations (Tylka et al., 1997) and the one the IEEE NSREC 2024 Short Course gives. Its four parameters are the saturated cross-section, an onset LET below which the fit is zero, a width, and a dimensionless shape exponent.

σ(L) = σsat {1 − exp[−(L − L0W)s]}  for L > L0;    σ = 0  for L ≤ L0
(11.8)
σ(L) cross-section at LET L, in the unit of σsat (cm²/device or cm²/bit) · σsat saturated cross-section · L LET (MeV·cm²/mg) · L0 onset or threshold LET (MeV·cm²/mg), written LETth in the 2024 Short Course · W width parameter (MeV·cm²/mg) · s shape exponent (dimensionless). The parameters have direct readings. L0 is the LET at which the first nodes respond; W is the span of LET over which the curve climbs to 63% of saturation; s sets how sharply it leaves the onset, since just above L0 the cross-section grows as the s-th power of (L − L0). Musseau and Ferlet-Cavrois read the Weibull as the failure distribution of a population of nominally identical elements: the cross-section at L is the fraction of the sensitive area whose effective threshold lies below L. On that reading, simpler technologies, whose elements differ little, give large exponents and near-step curves, while a sensitivity set mostly by a single hardening parameter gives a gradual rise; resistively and capacitively hardened memories show exactly that slow climb.

Counting matters at high LET. The cross-section counts events, and an event cross-section cannot exceed the area of the sensitive regions. When a single strike flips several bits — through diffusion to neighbouring junctions, or a grazing track across several cells (§7, §9) — a count of flipped bits divided by fluence is larger than the event cross-section, and at high LET it can exceed any physical area on the part. Multiple-bit events must therefore be identified and counted once each, which requires knowing which logical addresses are physical neighbours.

7 · Angle of incidence and effective LET

Particles in space arrive from every direction: the galactic cosmic-ray flux is nearly isotropic (M02 §2), so every chord through a sensitive volume occurs, from the shortest at normal incidence to the longest at grazing incidence. A beam test sends particles from one direction. The link between the two is the path length the particle travels inside the sensitive volume, and for the simplest geometry that path length turns an angle into an equivalent LET. This section derives that equivalence and sets out the conditions under which it fails.

Consider a sensitive volume that is a thin layer — laterally large, of depth d, with every charge generated inside it collected and none outside. A particle at normal incidence crosses it along a chord d and deposits (11.1) × d. Tilted by an angle θ from the normal, the same particle crosses along a chord d/cos θ and deposits 1/cos θ times as much. To the node, that particle is indistinguishable from a particle of LET/cos θ at normal incidence.

LETeff = LETcos θ
(11.9)
LETeff effective LET (MeV·cm²/mg) · LET the particle's LET at the sensitive depth (MeV·cm²/mg) · θ angle between the track and the normal to the die surface. At 45° the factor is 1.41 and at 60° it is 2. Assumes a sensitive volume that is thin compared with its lateral extent, collection only within that volume, and an LET constant along the tilted path. It applies to direct ionization only. On the formula sheet as (11.9).

Test practice uses the relation in both directions. Tilting a part lets a single ion species reach a higher effective LET, so fewer beam changes are needed to trace a cross-section curve, and a threshold measured at an angle is quoted as an effective LET. Tilting also changes the fluence the die receives: the beam's fluence is counted per unit area normal to the beam, and a die tilted by θ presents a projected area smaller by cos θ, so the particles per unit die area are Φ cos θ. Cross-sections from tilted exposures are referred to that effective fluence.

Where the cosine law fails

Every assumption behind (11.9) fails somewhere, and each failure has a physical cause.

Thin films. In silicon on insulator the buried oxide ends collection at the bottom of the film, so at normal incidence the collection length is set by the film thickness; toward grazing incidence the track runs along the film, and the collection length is limited instead by the lateral isolation trenches. Musseau and Ferlet-Cavrois summarize a thick-SOI part as behaving like thin SOI at normal incidence and like bulk at grazing angles, and they report a very strong angular dependence in SOI, with heavy ions and with protons. In Figure 14 the thick-SOI register's cross-section exceeds 1/cos θ by a factor of two to three between 45° and 75°, while the bulk memory rises more slowly than 1/cos θ. They add a second effect of thinness: the ion track has a radial extent of up to hundreds of nanometres, set by the range of its delta rays, so in a film thinner than that some of the energy the ion loses escapes the volume, and the product of LET and chord — which is the effective-LET concept — overestimates the energy deposited.

In Musseau and Ferlet-Cavrois's Fig. 77 the reference curve labelled 1/cos(angle) is displaced by one 10° step: its points, 1.06 at 30°, 1.31 at 50°, 2.0 at 70° and 2.9 at 80°, are the values of 1/cos(θ − 10°), and the bulk data lie on that displaced curve. The accompanying text, which says the 1/cos law fits the bulk data and that SOI differs drastically from it, rests on the displaced curve. Against 1/cos θ itself, drawn in Figure 14, the bulk memory lies below the law from 40° on — 1.6 against 2.0 at 60°, 2.8 against 5.8 at 80° — and the thick-SOI register lies above it by a factor of two to three up to 75°. Both data sets are 300 MeV proton results. Protons upset through reaction products, for which an effective LET is not defined (below), so neither part is expected to follow 1/cos θ.

Collection outside a fixed layer. In bulk devices the funnel and diffusion collect charge from outside any fixed layer, from a depth that itself depends on the track direction, so the chord through a slab is an approximation to the collection geometry. Dodd and Massengill note that angled strikes need full three-dimensional device simulation.

Nuclear secondaries. Protons and neutrons cause upsets through reaction products, not through their own LET, so an effective LET is not defined for them; the IEEE NSREC 2024 Short Course states that the relevant metric for those particles is the particle energy. The reaction products are forward-scattered along the incident direction (Dodd and Massengill), so the angular dependence of proton upsets follows the reaction kinematics.

Overlayers and range. A tilted ion crosses 1/cos θ times more metallization and passivation before it reaches the silicon, so a low-energy accelerator ion arrives at the sensitive depth with a different energy, and a different LET, from the one the surface value assumes (Musseau and Ferlet-Cavrois note the added shielding thickness at grazing incidence). Several cells. Finally, a grazing track can cross several sensitive regions, turning one event into a multiple-cell upset.

Radiation in orbit therefore arrives from all directions, and the direction matters. A thin sensitive volume in an isotropic environment is struck most often along short chords, but the rare long chords carry the largest deposits and set the rate for a part whose threshold is high. Rate codes account for this by integrating over the distribution of chord lengths in the sensitive volume, which is the subject of §8.

An error rate needs two curves: how many particles of each LET arrive, which the environment models of M02 §11 supply, and how large a target the part presents at each LET, which §6 measured. As a teaching form it makes the leverage of the threshold explicit: the integral LET spectrum rises steeply toward low LET, so a small fall in threshold admits many more particles, while the saturated cross-section enters only linearly.

The environment's spectrum has one feature that shapes every rate. Galactic cosmic-ray abundances fall steeply above iron, Z = 26 (M02 §2), and by Dodd and Massengill's rule of thumb an ion's maximum LET in MeV·cm²/mg is roughly its atomic number; the LET spectrum therefore drops sharply above the region iron can reach, near 30 MeV·cm²/mg. A part with its threshold above that region sees only the rare heavier nuclei and has a far lower rate than one whose threshold sits below it.

CREME96

The standard tool for evaluating (11.10) in orbit is CREME96, the revision of the Cosmic Ray Effects on Micro-Electronics code documented by Tylka et al. (1997), a NASA and Naval Research Laboratory tool (Deck 02 p. 20) now hosted at Vanderbilt (M02 §11). Deck 02 p. 20 lists its inputs — the orbit, the shielding thickness and the mission duration — and its outputs: the flux against LET behind the chosen shielding, the predicted upset rate for a memory, logic or custom device, and worst-case environments. For heavy ions it computes the rate by the integral rectangular parallelepiped method: the sensitive volume is represented as a rectangular box whose depth the user supplies, the cross-section as Weibull parameters, and the calculation integrates over the distribution of chord lengths an isotropic flux produces in that box. It also computes proton-induced rates from a cross-section against proton energy. Tylka lists the transport code's limitations at release: no neutron production in the shielding, no target fragments, aluminium as the only shield and silicon as the only target.

The worst cases are models of one real event. The solar-minimum galactic cosmic-ray environment, when that flux is highest (M02 §4), is the design benchmark for the steady background. For solar particles CREME96 offers three models built from the October 1989 event, which Tylka describes as a genuine worst case at about the 99% confidence level: a worst-week model averaged over 180 hours of the 19–27 October disturbance, a worst-day model averaged over 18 hours from 13:00 UT on 20 October, when an interplanetary shock arrived, and a peak model from the highest five-minute fluxes on that day, which exceed the worst day by roughly a factor of five.

Deck 02 pp. 20–21 list the worst-week and worst-day models with the label "(solar maximum/minimum)". In CREME96 they are solar-particle-event models, both built from the October 1989 event (Tylka et al.). Solar minimum and solar maximum are conditions of the separate galactic cosmic-ray model, which follows the solar cycle and is highest at solar minimum.

Deck 02 p. 22 shows why the revision mattered. For a set of representative devices in geosynchronous orbit behind 100 mils of aluminium, the old CREME solar-particle models, labelled M = 9 and M = 10 and meant to represent the peak of the August 1972 event, predicted rates at least an order of magnitude above even the CREME96 peak model. The old models were unconstrained by heavy-ion measurements at high energy — all of the August 1972 heavy-ion data above about 40 MeV per nucleon came from a single four-minute sounding-rocket flight — while the solar-minimum cosmic-ray rates of the two codes agree closely. The choice of environment model moves a predicted rate by more than the uncertainty in most device parameters.

9 · Scaling

Each technology generation shrinks the transistor, lowers the supply voltage and packs more bits onto a die, and each of those changes acts on a different term of the preceding sections. Whether single-event errors become more or less frequent therefore depends on which rate is meant — per bit or per chip — and on which of the opposing changes wins. This section assembles the answer from the pieces already in place: the collected charge of §3, the critical charge of §4 and the rate integral of §8.

Four things shrink or grow at once

Scaling moves both sides of the comparison of §1 and both factors of the rate. The sensitive area per bit shrinks with the cell, so the saturated per-bit cross-section falls. Junctions become shallower and collection volumes smaller, most of all in silicon on insulator and in fins, so each strike delivers less charge. The node capacitance and the supply voltage fall together, so the critical charge Qcrit = CnVcrit falls, and with it the threshold LET. And the number of bits per chip rises. The IEEE NSREC 2024 Short Course summarizes the result for memories: the per-bit upset rate is expected to fall as the cell shrinks, the reduction in per-bit cross-section is offset by the lower voltage and critical charge, which make the cell susceptible to the far more abundant particles of low LET, and the growing density raises the rate per chip. Cristoloveanu and Ferlet-Cavrois's compilation shows the first two trends directly — both the saturated cross-section and the threshold LET of bulk and SOI memories fell with each generation.

Which effect wins per bit follows from §8. With the step approximation the rate is σsatΦ(>Lth), and the figure of merit makes the same point more sharply: the rate goes as σsat/L0.25². The shrinking cross-section enters linearly; the falling threshold enters through a spectrum that rises steeply toward low LET, or through the inverse square. A generation that shrinks the sensitive area faster than it lowers the threshold sees its per-bit rate fall; one that lowers the threshold faster sees it rise. Dodd and Massengill, writing in 2003, describe the overall trend of their time as a general increase in susceptibility with each generation — Sandia static memories without feedback resistors lost threshold LET generation by generation — and add that the reduction in total node capacitance matters at least as much as any change in the physics, because it shortens the RC feedback time that sets the race of §4. They also cite simple scaling rules predicting an increase in soft-error susceptibility of about 40% per technology generation (Ronen et al., 2001). The observation that per-bit error rates decline with gate length and the prediction that susceptibility rises are therefore statements about different quantities: the second concerns the threshold, the first weights that threshold by a sensitive area that can shrink faster.

Capacitance: memories that held it and memories that did not

The two memory families took different paths because of (11.3). Dynamic-memory manufacturers deliberately held the storage-cell capacitance nearly constant across generations through modified cell structures, and their single-event performance stayed roughly constant as a result; the nodal capacitance of static memories, which store the bit on transistor gates and drains, fell steadily (Dodd and Massengill). Dodd and Massengill note that manufacturers may have to add capacitance to dense static memories explicitly, the same remedy §4 derived for hardening. The per-device reliability requirement then closes the loop: a terrestrial specification of, typically, under 1000 FIT per device has to be met while the number of bits per device rises, so the per-bit rate must keep falling just to stand still.

The chip-level history of one product shows how many terms move at once. Dodd and Massengill trace the alpha-particle soft-error rate of the DEC Alpha microprocessor from a 0.5 µm, 3.3 V generation to a 0.18 µm, 1.6 V one. The rate rose with the first shrinks, as larger caches, smaller features and lower voltage predict, but less than simple scaling expected because a lid coating reduced the alpha flux from the package; error correction then cut it sharply although scaling continued; the next shrink raised it again, moderated by the lower charge-collection efficiency of the 0.25 µm technology; and the last rose steeply when flip-chip packaging placed lead-bearing solder bumps, an alpha source, directly on the die. (Deck 02 p. 16's label "flip-chip" beside SEU denotes a bit flip; the packaging technology is the one meant here.)

New mechanisms at small dimensions

Lower critical charge brings particles that were once harmless into play. Below roughly the 65 nm node, direct ionization by low-energy protons can upset a cell without a nuclear reaction (M01 §7), and the low-LET end of every spectrum, which an older threshold ignored, starts to contribute. Multi-gate FinFETs have altered the trend: the 2024 Short Course notes that they made it possible to limit the reduction in critical charge, while their small footprint raises the density of transistors on a chip.

Geometry adds the second change. Deck 02 p. 28 lists ion tracks larger than device sizes and single events that affect multiple cells among the new challenges, and shows a proton-induced nuclear reaction whose products — an alpha particle, a gamma ray, a proton and an oxygen recoil — deposit charge in several cells at once: about 40 fC in each cell along the oxygen recoil's track, and about 30 fC in the cell at the reaction site and in one the alpha crosses. Once the cell pitch approaches the radial extent of the track and the reach of diffusion, one particle deposits charge in several cells: multiple-cell upsets grow as a fraction of all events, grazing tracks cross whole rows, and a code that corrects one bit per word fails unless physically adjacent bits are assigned to different words (Dodd and Massengill). Transients follow the same logic: in silicon on insulator their widths shrink with scaling (§5), but the faster clocks that come with scaling open more capture windows per second.

A memory moves to a new generation in which the per-bit saturated cross-section halves and every characteristic LET of its Weibull curve halves too. By the figure of merit of §8, what happens to its per-bit upset rate in the same orbit?

It doubles. The figure of merit is σsat/L0.25², and halving every characteristic LET halves L0.25. The numerator falls by 2 and the denominator by 4, so the ratio rises by 2. The smaller target is outweighed by the larger number of particles able to upset it, because the threshold enters squared. Only if the cross-section fell by more than a factor of four would the per-bit rate fall.

10 · Destructive single events

The same collected charge that flips a bit can, in the right structure and at the right voltage, switch on a current path that sustains itself or destroy the structure it crosses. M05 §5 classified these hard errors by the fact that rewriting does not recover them. This section gives their mechanisms, and with them the answer to what separates a destructive event from an upset: a structure that contains a regenerative path or a dielectric under high field, a voltage across it large enough to feed the event, and an LET large enough to trigger it. The threat rises with both the LET and the applied voltage, which is why Deck 03 p. 31 calls hard errors mostly a concern for high-power devices.

Single-event latchup

Bulk CMOS contains a four-layer structure it was never designed to have. The p⁺ source of a p-channel transistor, the n-well it sits in, the p-type substrate and the n⁺ source of a neighbouring n-channel transistor form a p–n–p–n sequence between the supply and ground. It acts as two parasitic bipolar transistors: a vertical pnp, whose emitter is the p⁺ source, base the n-well and collector the substrate, and a lateral npn, whose emitter is the n⁺ source, base the substrate and collector the n-well. Each collector feeds the other's base, and the well and substrate resistances convert any current flowing in them into the voltage that forward-biases an emitter–base junction. A particle strike that injects enough current into the well or substrate turns one transistor on; its collector current turns the other on; and once the loop sustains itself the structure latches into a low-impedance path from the supply to ground (IEEE NSREC 2024 Short Course). The loop sustains itself when the product of the two transistors' current gains exceeds unity, the standard condition for a thyristor.

The latched state persists until power is removed. Switching off the supply collapses it and the part returns to service, which is why spacecraft carry latchup-detection circuits that cycle power on an over-current; left powered, the current rises until the structure is thermally destroyed (M05 §5). Even a latchup that is cleared can leave latent damage if the current is not limited and cut quickly enough: it can melt metal lines and leave voids that shorten the part's life (IEEE NSREC 2024 Short Course). The first report of single-event latchup was in 1979 (Kolasinski et al., via Dodd and Massengill), and neutron-induced latchup has since been observed in dense static memories at ground level. Everything that lowers the loop gain or the parasitic resistances hardens against it: an epitaxial layer on a heavily doped substrate (EEE6338 M02 §7), guard rings and channel stops (M08 §6), and, completely, silicon on insulator, whose dielectric isolation removes the four-layer structure. There is no established total-fluence requirement for a latchup test; as a possible guide, a part is treated as latchup-immune if it does not latch after a fluence of 10⁷ ions/cm² (Buchner and Baze, IEEE NSREC 2001 Short Course).

Snapback

Snapback needs only one parasitic transistor. In an n-channel transistor the source, body and drain form a lateral npn; a strike leaves holes in the body that forward-bias the body–source junction and turn it on, and at a high drain voltage impact ionization in the drain junction generates further holes that keep the body forward-biased, so the transistor cannot return to its off state. The result is a sustained high-current condition similar to latchup (Dodd and Massengill; Cristoloveanu and Ferlet-Cavrois). It is the concern in silicon on insulator, where the floating body makes the bipolar action easy and latchup itself is absent, and in junction-isolated circuits generally (IEEE NSREC 2024 Short Course).

Single-event gate rupture

Gate rupture is dielectric breakdown along an ion track. In a vertical power MOSFET held off with a large drain voltage, a heavy ion that crosses the gate–drain overlap region, the neck, generates charge in the silicon that accumulates at the silicon–oxide interface beneath the gate and raises the field across the gate oxide; the charge the ion generates in the oxide itself briefly lowers the oxide's breakdown field. The oxide breaks down locally, current flows from gate to drain, and thermal runaway melts a permanent short (Shenai, Galloway and Schrimpf). The off state is the vulnerable one, and the gate insulator and the epitaxial layer beneath it are the sensitive region (IEEE NSREC 2024 Short Course). Because the part does not survive the event, gate-rupture data are presented as boundaries — the combinations of gate and drain voltage at which rupture occurs for ions of given LET — and a rupture cross-section is one event divided by the fluence that produced it.

Thin gate oxides rupture by the same physics at lower voltage, and Deck 03 p. 30 shows that the damage accumulates before it becomes catastrophic (Sexton et al., 1998). A 7 nm silicon-dioxide capacitor of 0.0025 cm² was exposed to successive fluences of 10⁶ ions/cm² of 360 MeV gold — about 2,500 ions through the capacitor per step — with the gate bias raised in steps from 3.0 to 3.7 V. The leakage current grew after each exposure, which is precursor damage from ions that did not rupture the oxide, until the oxide broke down at 3.7 V. The critical breakdown voltage falls with LET and with oxide thickness: across the slide's data it runs from about 15 V to about 9 V for 18 nm oxides between LETs of 27 and 80 MeV·cm²/mg, and from about 6 V to about 4 V for 7 nm oxides. Precursor damage connects rupture to the high-field defect generation of M10 §7, and to M05 §6's point that an oxide weakened by accumulated damage ruptures under a strike an undamaged one survives. Lauenstein et al. (2011) express the power-device risk against the environment: Deck 03 p. 31 overlays a part's rupture results on the geosynchronous heavy-ion flux as a function of LET and atomic number, with regions marked safe, unknown threat and known threat, and states the conclusion that the threat of gate rupture increases with LET and with applied gate voltage.

Single-event burnout

A vertical double-diffused power MOSFET (Deck 03 p. 31) carries current vertically: from the n⁺ source through a channel in the p body, down through a lightly doped n⁻ epitaxial drift region that holds the blocking voltage, to the n⁺ substrate and the drain contact. Its source, body and drift region form a parasitic npn transistor, normally held off because the source metallization shorts the body to the source. Burnout begins when a heavy ion crosses a device that is off and blocking a high drain voltage, along a path through the source, body and drift region. Its current flows laterally through the body beneath the source, and the voltage it develops across the body resistance forward-biases the source–body junction, turning the parasitic transistor on. Electrons injected from the source cross the body into the high-field drift region, where impact ionization multiplies them; the avalanche-generated holes flow back into the body, add to the lateral current and raise the base–emitter voltage further. The feedback is regenerative: the device passes into second breakdown, loses the ability to block, and the current rises until thermal failure shorts source and drain (Shenai, Galloway and Schrimpf; IEEE NSREC 2024 Short Course). Shenai, Galloway and Schrimpf note that burnout of p-channel power MOSFETs had not been reported, and attribute its rarity to the lower impact-ionization rate of holes, which makes the avalanche current smaller.

The failure voltage falls with LET. Liu et al. (2011), on Deck 03 p. 32, simulated the quasi-stationary avalanche characteristic of production and burnout-sensitive samples of one power MOSFET, which shows the snapback of the parasitic transistor, and measured the burnout failure voltage of the sensitive samples against surface LET with several ion species. The failure voltage fell from about 200 V at an LET of 5 to about 75 V near 90 MeV·cm²/mg, fitted by V = 399.13·LET−0.386 with R² = 0.856. Burnout also occurs without any parasitic gain. In Schottky power diodes, avalanche multiplication in the high-field region multiplies the ion's charge until the current heats the lattice enough to sustain itself, and silicon carbide devices, whose peak fields are much higher than silicon's, need not have a higher burnout threshold than silicon diodes of the same rating (IEEE NSREC 2024 Short Course). Deck 03 p. 24 shows the physical result in a silicon carbide Schottky diode and a gallium oxide Schottky diode.

Wide-bandgap power devices

Deck 03 p. 33 reports the voltage thresholds of high-voltage power MOSFETs and diodes for two heavy-ion effects (Sengupta et al., 2024). Single-event leakage current (SELC) is a permanent increase in leakage left by strikes that do not destroy the device; single-event burnout is destruction. The burnout threshold voltage falls steeply as the LET rises to about 10 MeV·cm²/mg and then saturates, at about 800 V for the 3300 V parts, roughly 20% of their avalanche breakdown voltage, and about 500 V for the 1200 V parts, roughly 30% of theirs; the saturated burnout thresholds of the two ratings therefore differ by about 300 V. The leakage threshold also falls steeply up to an LET of about 10 but then continues to fall more slowly: read from the slide, from about 620 V to about 400 V between LETs of 10 and 65 for the 3300 V parts, and from about 280 V to about 150 V for the 1200 V diodes, the two ratings again offset by about 300 V. Voltage rating, epitaxial thickness and doping all move the thresholds, which is why the slide ends with a call for structural and process optimization. The saturating burnout threshold has a direct consequence for derating: within the tested range, ions above an LET of about 10 do not find a lower burnout voltage, so the burnout-safe voltage of a given part is a fixed value, a rating-dependent fraction of its avalanche breakdown. Leakage degradation has no such floor in the tested range and continues to worsen as the LET rises.

Deck 03 p. 33 is titled "Single-Event Effects in Discrete Silicon Power Devices", but both of its references, Sengupta et al. at NSREC 2023 and in IEEE Transactions on Nuclear Science (2024), concern high-voltage silicon carbide devices, and the plotted MOSFETs and diodes are silicon carbide parts. The saturation of the burnout threshold at 20–30% of avalanche breakdown and the 300 V offsets are silicon carbide results. The burnout mechanism and the LET and voltage dependences of the preceding paragraphs apply to both materials.

An upset changes a stored value; a destructive event changes the current path. Three conditions separate them. The struck structure must offer a path that can sustain or amplify current — a parasitic thyristor, a parasitic bipolar transistor with avalanche multiplication, or a thin dielectric under high field. The voltage across it must be large enough to drive that path and supply the energy that damages it, which is why the threat rises with the applied gate or drain voltage. And the ion's LET must deliver enough charge to trigger it, which is why the threat rises with LET. Power devices combine all three, which is why hard errors concentrate there.

11 · Beyond a single LET

Every section so far has described a particle by one number, its LET, and a device by one sensitive volume with a critical charge. Both descriptions are approximations, and Deck 02 pp. 27–28 show where they break down: nuclear reactions of the incident ion itself, charge collection volumes that are not boxes, tracks wider than the devices they cross, and material layers above the silicon that change what arrives. The replacement for a single LET is a calculation that follows individual particle histories through the actual device.

Nuclear reactions of heavy ions

Protons and neutrons act through nuclear reactions (M01 §7), and so, occasionally, do heavy ions. Deck 02 p. 27 shows a simulated event from Weller et al. (2010): an iron ion at 2 GeV per nucleon undergoes a nuclear reaction in silicon next to a tungsten layer, and the reaction sends delta rays, protons, neutrons and heavier fragments outward in a fan. Recoiling target fragments are slow and carry several units of charge, so some of them ionize far more densely than the fast primary. The IEEE NSREC 2024 Short Course states the consequence: for high-energy heavy ions whose LET is below a part's threshold, events are caused not by direct ionization but by secondary particles from nuclear interactions, which have higher LETs than the incident ion. A cross-section curve measured with such ions therefore shows events below the direct-ionization threshold that no Weibull in LET can represent. Metal layers above the device, which Deck 02 p. 28 lists among the new challenges, add target nuclei for these reactions, heavy ones in the case of tungsten and the other high-Z metals whose dose enhancement M04 §5 treats.

Collection volumes, track structure and straggling

The rectangular sensitive volume of §§7–8 is itself an idealization. Deck 02 p. 28 shows a simulated charge-collection volume that follows the potential distribution of a device and its neighbours rather than any box, and an ion track larger than the devices around it. Musseau and Ferlet-Cavrois set out the consequence for small volumes: once the body of a transistor is smaller than the range of the energetic electrons the ion releases, the energy the ion loses and the energy deposited in the volume are no longer the same, both because delta rays carry energy out and because energy loss is statistical, with the LET giving only its average. The LET-times-chord estimate of §7 then overestimates the deposit, and a distribution of deposits replaces a single value.

Monte Carlo transport

The replacement is to follow the particles themselves. A Monte Carlo transport calculation samples individual particle histories — the primary, its delta rays, any nuclear reaction and each fragment — through a three-dimensional model of the device including its metal and dielectric overlayers, and records the energy deposited in each of several sensitive volumes event by event (M02 §11). Geant4 is the general toolkit, whose main advantage is exactly this detailed particle transport, and MRED, developed at Vanderbilt on Geant4, specializes it for microelectronic geometries (Deck 02 pp. 26–27). Counting the events whose deposited or collected charge exceeds the critical charge, across the environment's species, energies and directions, gives a rate without reducing either the particle to an LET or the device to one volume, and it treats angle, overlayers, nuclear secondaries and multiple-cell events in the same calculation.

The cost is computation. Dodd and Massengill describe the device-simulation hierarchy that such calculations feed — drift–diffusion, hydrodynamic and Monte Carlo carrier transport, and mixed-level simulation in which the struck device is solved in three dimensions while the rest of the circuit is a compact model — and give a measure of the expense: one directly simulated cross-section curve for a static-memory cell took nearly 7,000 three-dimensional strike simulations and about three months on thirty nodes of a parallel computer. Deck 02 p. 30 frames the goal as an automated chain from environment through transport, device, circuit and system to a predicted error rate, with experiments feeding each link. LET remains the variable in which tests are run, environments are tabulated and CREME96 computes; Monte Carlo transport is where it is replaced, for sensitive volumes comparable with the track, for devices beneath high-Z layers, and for the multiple-cell events that scaling makes common.

Where this leaves us

A single particle has now been followed from its track to an error rate. Its charge appears in a column far denser than the doping, which for a few picoseconds conducts the junction potential down the track and collects charge by drift from well below the depletion layer; the rest arrives by diffusion over nanoseconds. Only reverse-biased junctions on weakly held nodes collect it efficiently, which is why the off transistor's drain is the sensitive spot and why epitaxial layers, wells and silicon on insulator harden by shrinking the collection volume. A static memory cell upsets when the collected charge outruns its critical charge, CnVcrit, in a race between feedback and recovery; a dynamic cell needs only to lose its signal margin; a logic transient needs a path, a width and a latch window.

A beam test compresses all of that into a cross-section against LET, an area per device or per bit whose onset measures the critical charge and whose saturation measures the sensitive area; a Weibull function fits it; tilting the part converts an angle into an effective LET for direct ionization of a laterally wide layer that collects only within itself, and the conversion fails for silicon-on-insulator films, for funnel and diffusion collection in bulk, and for protons and neutrons. Integrated against the differential LET spectrum, the cross-section becomes an orbital rate, dominated by LETs between the onset and the iron edge and sensitive by an order of magnitude to the environment model. Scaling shrinks the target and the threshold together, so the per-bit rate can fall while the chip rate rises and multiple-cell events multiply. The same charge, in a structure with a regenerative path or a thin dielectric under high field and at a high enough voltage, latches, ruptures or burns out the part instead.

M12 turns to the third mechanism of M05, the displacement of lattice atoms, whose damage is cumulative like total dose but whose physics is nuclear collision rather than ionization. The circuit and system hardening techniques that act on the single-event mechanisms of this module — dual-interlocked cells, redundancy, error correction and interleaving — belong to the design modules on the course roadmap.
