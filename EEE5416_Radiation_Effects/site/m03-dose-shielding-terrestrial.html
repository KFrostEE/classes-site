Module 03

Dose, Shielding and Terrestrial Environments

Absorbed dose and the units it is quoted in, how orbit fixes the dose a spacecraft accumulates, why adding shielding mass stops helping, and what happens to the same particles when they enter an atmosphere instead of a spacecraft wall.

Prerequisites M01, M02 Read time ~40 min Sources Deck 01 pp. 24–29, 31, 36, 42–55; Deck 03 pp. 16, 23; Lectures 1–2

M02 ended with a flux: so many particles of each species, at each energy, per square centimetre per second, at a given orbit. A flux is not yet a requirement. Nothing can be procured, tested or qualified against it, because it says nothing about how much energy the parts actually absorb. This module supplies the quantity that closes that gap — absorbed dose — and then uses it for the two engineering questions that follow immediately from it: how much dose a given orbit delivers, and how much of that dose can be removed by putting mass between the environment and the part.

Shielding works well against one of the three space populations, poorly against the second, and effectively not at all against the third, and the reason is contained entirely in the interaction physics of M01: a particle is removed by a shield only if the shield is thicker than its range, and a shield that stops a particle may create new penetrating radiation in the process. That last point governs the final part of the module. The Earth's atmosphere is a shield of 1,033 g/cm² — some 750 times the areal density of a typical spacecraft wall — and yet aircraft and ground-level electronics still suffer single-event effects, because the shield itself is the source of the particles that cause them. The atmospheric cascade, and the neutron that dominates it, close the module. The man-made environments come last, since they are the environments the course later uses to test against everything described here.

1 · Absorbed dose

Every effect in this course that accumulates — threshold voltage shift, gain degradation, dark current growth — scales with the energy the radiation leaves behind in the material, not with the number of particles that arrived. Two environments made of entirely different species produce comparable damage if they deposit comparable energy per gram. Absorbed dose is the quantity that makes that statement precise, and it is the reason a single number can specify a mission.

Absorbed dose is the mean energy imparted by ionizing radiation per unit mass of the irradiated material at the point of interest. The word imparted carries the whole definition. What is counted is not the energy the particles brought into the volume, but the energy they left there: energy entering minus energy leaving. A 1 GeV proton crossing a 1 µm silicon layer arrives with 1 GeV and departs with very nearly 1 GeV, and the dose it delivers is set by the small difference. Energy carried out of the volume by the particle itself, by knock-on electrons that escape, or by bremsstrahlung photons produced inside it and not reabsorbed, is not dose.

D = ΔEDΔm    ΔED = ΔEE − ΔEL
(3.1)
D absorbed dose (rad, or Gy) · ΔED mean energy deposited in the volume · ΔEE energy entering · ΔEL energy leaving · Δm mass of the volume (g, or kg). The volume must be small enough that the field is uniform across it and large enough that the mean is statistically meaningful. In a small device those two requirements conflict, which is why single-event analysis abandons dose entirely and works in collected charge instead (M11 §§3–4).

Two unit systems are in use and both appear in this course. The customary unit is the rad, defined as 100 erg per gram. The SI unit is the gray, defined as one joule per kilogram, and it is the larger by a factor of 100. Expressed in electron-volts — the natural unit for what follows, since the quantities dose is later divided by are pair-creation energies and displacement thresholds of a few eV to a few tens of eV — one rad is 6.24×10¹³ eV/g.

The dose units, in every form the course uses

1 rad = 100 erg/g = 0.01 J/kg = 6.24×10¹³ eV/g
1 Gy = 1 J/kg = 100 rad = 6.24×10¹⁵ eV/g

The eV/g form follows from the definitions: 0.01 J/kg divided by 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ J/eV gives 6.24×10¹⁶ eV/kg, and dividing by 1,000 g/kg gives 6.24×10¹³ eV/g. It expresses a dose as the number of ionization events that dose can support, which is how M07 converts a dose into a trapped charge density.

Total ionizing dose is that quantity accumulated over an exposure: the energy deposited per unit mass by ionizing energy loss, summed over every particle and photon arriving during a mission or a test. Each word is doing work. Dose is equation (3.1), energy imparted per unit mass. Total is the accumulation over time, which is why it is quoted for a mission rather than for an instant, and why the quantity that matters is a fluence rather than a flux. Ionizing restricts the accounting to the channel of M01 §8 that liberates charge; energy that goes to displacing atoms instead is counted separately, by the non-ionizing quantity of M04 §6. Total ionizing dose carries the units of dose and the same obligation to name a material: rad(Si), rad(SiO₂), or gray. It is the number a part is qualified against, and M07 onward traces how an accumulated dose becomes trapped charge and then a shifted device parameter.

Dose is a property of a material, not of a radiation field. The same fluence passing through silicon and through silicon dioxide deposits different energy per gram in each, because the mass stopping power of a charged particle and the photon interaction cross-sections both depend on the atomic composition and electron density of the absorber. A dose is therefore incomplete until the material is named, and the convention throughout this course is to write the material into the unit: rad(Si), rad(SiO₂). The difference between the two is modest for Co-60 gammas and large for low-energy X-rays, where the photoelectric cross-section rises steeply with atomic number. Quoting a dose against the wrong material propagates a systematic error into every number computed from it.

A dose without a material is not a specification

"100 krad" is not a requirement; "100 krad(Si)" is. A part qualified against a dose in one material and flown against a specification written in another carries a margin error of unknown sign. The same discipline applies to the shielding configuration: a total dose figure with no stated shield depth cannot be checked, because the number depends on depth as strongly as it depends on orbit.

2 · Converting a flux into a dose

Dose as defined in §1 presumes something absorbed the energy. Predicting a dose before flight requires connecting it to the environment description M02 produced. That connection runs through two quantities: the fluence, which counts the particles, and the linear energy transfer, which says how much energy each one leaves per unit path.

Fluence Φ is the time-integrated flux — the number of particles crossing unit area, in cm⁻². Flux, in cm⁻²s⁻¹, is its time derivative. The distinction matters throughout the course: cumulative effects depend on fluence, while single-event rates depend on flux, because one accumulates and the other is a rate of independent trials.

Linear energy transfer is the energy a charged particle transfers to the material per unit path length, divided by the material density so that the quantity describes the particle and its energy rather than the sample. The course quotes it in MeV·cm²/mg. LET is developed properly in M04, along with its relation to stopping power, its dependence on ion species and energy, and the Bragg peak; here it is needed only as the coefficient that turns a fluence into a dose.

D(rad) ≈ 1.60×10⁻⁵ · LET · Φ
(3.2)
D absorbed dose in the material for which the LET was quoted (rad) · LET linear energy transfer, MeV·cm²/mg · Φ fluence, cm⁻². The constant carries the whole unit conversion: MeV→J multiplies by 1.602×10⁻¹³, energy per mg→energy per kg multiplies by 10⁶, and dividing the resulting 1.602×10⁻⁷ J/kg by 0.01 converts gray to rad. Valid for a monoenergetic beam at normal incidence with LET approximately constant across the layer. A real environment is a spectrum, so the practical calculation integrates this expression over the differential fluence spectrum of every species present.

Dose rate is dose per unit time, and it is a separate specification rather than a convenience. Two parts that accumulate the same total dose at different rates do not end in the same state, for two reasons the course returns to. Trapped charge in an oxide anneals over hours to years, so a slow irradiation permits recovery that a fast one does not (M07 §7). And bipolar devices show enhanced low dose rate sensitivity, degrading more for the same total dose when it arrives slowly (M08 §9). A ground test at a convenient laboratory rate therefore does not automatically predict flight behaviour at 10⁻⁴ rad(Si)/s.

Space dose rates span roughly 10⁻⁶ to the middle 10⁻³ rad(Si)/s across the orbits of interest. Converting to the annual figures missions are specified in needs only the number of seconds in a year, 3.156×10⁷, so that band runs from about 32 rad(Si) to about 160 krad(Si) per year. Both orbit figures of §4 fall on this axis, and their positions expose the assumption behind each: a figure far outside the band differs in its shielding depth rather than in its physics.

3 · Equivalent dose, and where rem belongs

Absorbed dose measures energy per unit mass and nothing else. Biological damage depends additionally on how densely that energy is deposited along the track, because a cell that takes its energy from a single heavy ion crossing it suffers correlated damage that it repairs less successfully than the same energy delivered by many sparse electron tracks. Radiation protection therefore uses a second quantity, weighted by particle type. It enters this course because the avionics environment is quoted in it, and because the course materials state a conversion between the two that holds only in that context.

H = wR · D
(3.3)
H equivalent dose (sievert, Sv; or rem) · D absorbed dose in tissue (Gy, or rad) · wR radiation weighting factor, dimensionless. 1 Sv = 100 rem, so 10 mSv = 1 rem, exactly parallel to 1 Gy = 100 rad. wR = 1 for photons, electrons and muons; 2 for protons and charged pions; 20 for alpha particles, fission fragments and heavy ions; and for neutrons a continuous function of energy peaking near 20 around 1 MeV (ICRP Publication 103; the older ICRP 60 values, still found in pre-2007 references, give 5 for protons and a step function for neutrons). Defined for tissue only — it is not defined for silicon and never appears in a device specification.

The occupational limits that follow set the scale for the aircrew discussion in §8. Under the United States regulations the annual limit for a radiation worker is 50 mSv, and a separate limit of 5 mSv applies to the dose received by the embryo or fetus over the whole of a declared pregnancy — a gestation total rather than a second annual figure. The ICRP recommends stricter values: 20 mSv per year averaged over five years, and 1 mSv to the embryo or fetus. Aircrew accumulate a measurable fraction of the occupational figure from the atmospheric field of §7, and it is the pregnancy limit that produces roster restrictions; ordinary flight-time limitations are duty and fatigue rules rather than radiation rules.

"1 rem ≈ 1 rad" is a statement about one particular field

Deck 01 p. 43 states that to within 15–20%, 1 rem ≈ 1 rad. That is correct for the field it is discussing — the atmospheric radiation an aircrew member is exposed to, whose dose is delivered predominantly by particles with wR near 1. It is not a general equivalence between the units. In a trapped-proton environment the factor is 2, and in a heavy-ion environment it is 20. Equation (3.3) is the general statement; the 15–20% figure is a numerical coincidence of one spectrum.

4 · Dose varies with orbit

With dose defined, the populations of M02 can be ranked by what they deliver. Three orbit classes cover most missions, each dominated by a different mechanism, and the ordering by total dose does not follow the ordering by altitude.

Low Earth orbit at moderate inclination. The reference case is the orbit of the Hubble Space Telescope. The spacecraft is exposed to trapped protons, encountered almost entirely during passes through the South Atlantic Anomaly, and to trapped electrons. The geomagnetic field excludes solar protons effectively at this inclination, because the vertical cutoff rigidity near the equator is high enough to reject the solar spectrum. Total dose is moderate.

Polar and sun-synchronous low Earth orbit. The reference case is Landsat-8. Doses are more severe than in the moderate-inclination case for two reasons. The outer belt extends down to low altitude at high latitude, where its field lines dip towards the Earth, so a polar orbit passes through that low-altitude extension on every revolution and the trapped electron flux integrated over an orbit is greater. And the cutoff rigidity falls towards zero over the poles, so the protection against solar protons that the moderate-inclination orbit enjoys is here only partial.

Geostationary orbit. At 35,800 km the spacecraft sits at L = 6.6, inside the outer electron belt and outside the region where the geomagnetic field usefully attenuates solar protons. The dominant contributions are outer-belt electrons and unattenuated solar protons. Trapped protons are present but unimportant: at this distance their energies are low, and very light shielding stops them.

The course anchors this ordering with two numbers. For low Earth orbit at high inclination, 200 mils of aluminium — 5.08 mm, or 1.37 g/cm² — limits the total proton dose to less than 1 krad(Si) per year. At half of geosynchronous altitude, with light shielding, total dose can approach 1 Mrad(Si) per year. The three-order-of-magnitude gap is a statement about which population dominates rather than about altitude: half-GEO is approximately L = 3.8, inside the outer electron belt and just below the shells where its flux peaks, L = 4–4.5.

Dose is delivered episodically, not smoothly

A mission dose divided by a mission duration is a bookkeeping average, not a description of what the parts experience. Two mechanisms break the average. In low Earth orbit almost the entire trapped-proton dose arrives during the few South Atlantic Anomaly passes per day, so the instantaneous rate during a pass far exceeds the orbital mean. And a solar particle event raises proton flux and dose rate sharply and then decays over hours to days, delivering in that interval a dose comparable to months of quiet-time accumulation. Systems must survive both the peak rate and the accumulated total, which is why operational responses exist alongside design margin: real-time monitoring, safing of sensitive payloads during an event, and mission planning against the solar cycle.

5 · Shielding, and why it saturates

Shielding is the first line of defence, and in a spacecraft much of it is already present: the structure, the propellant tanks, the battery and the surrounding boxes all attenuate the environment reaching the parts inside. The design question is not whether to shield but how much attenuation additional mass buys, and the answer is that it buys a great deal at first and very little after a few millimetres. That behaviour follows from the ranges of the incident particles and from the secondary radiation the shield itself produces.

The correct variable for shield thickness is not length but areal density, the mass per unit area presented to the beam. A charged particle loses energy by interacting with the electrons of the absorber, so the energy lost is governed by the number of electrons encountered along the path, which is proportional to the product of density and thickness rather than to thickness alone. Expressing depth in g/cm² makes different materials comparable to first order, and it is the variable that every shielding curve and every range table uses.

xρ = ρ · t
(3.4)
xρ areal density, also called mass thickness (g/cm²) · ρ material density (g/cm³) · t physical thickness (cm). For aluminium ρ = 2.699 g/cm³, so 1 mm = 0.270 g/cm² and 200 mils = 5.08 mm = 1.37 g/cm². The equivalence across materials is first order only: at equal areal density a high-atomic-number absorber produces more bremsstrahlung and more nuclear secondaries, which §6 quantifies.

A dose-depth curve gives the dose behind a given depth of shielding, and decomposing it by the species responsible shows why it has the shape it does. Three contributions appear, and they behave completely differently with depth. Trapped electrons deposit heavily at shallow depth and then vanish, because an electron has a finite range and once the shield exceeds it no electrons of that energy reach the far side at all. Bremsstrahlung — the photons radiated as those electrons decelerate, introduced in M01 — is generated inside the shield rather than arriving from outside, and photons attenuate exponentially with no finite range, so this contribution falls slowly and eventually dominates. Trapped protons fall between the two: they have a finite range like electrons, but the trapped spectrum extends to hundreds of MeV, and the range of a few-hundred-MeV proton exceeds anything a spacecraft can carry.

Ranges make the argument quantitative. For electrons the standard empirical range–energy relation is due to Katz and Penfold, and it gives the depth beyond which essentially no electrons of that energy emerge.

R = 0.412 En, n = 1.265 − 0.0954 ln E  ·  R = 0.530 E − 0.106
(3.5)
R extrapolated range — the practical thickness required to stop the electrons (g/cm²; divide by ρ for a physical thickness) · E electron kinetic energy (MeV). The paper states the first form for 0.01 < E < 2.5 MeV and the second for 2.5 < E < 20 MeV. Extrapolating the first to 3 MeV gives 1.474 g/cm² against the second form's 1.484, so the branches join smoothly and a disagreement much larger than 1% near the boundary means the wrong one has been used. The constants are an empirical fit to aluminium absorbers, so carrying the result to another material at equal areal density is an approximation on top of an approximation. Katz & Penfold, Rev. Mod. Phys. 24, 28 (1952).

Galactic cosmic rays sit outside this picture altogether. Their flux peaks between 100 and 1,000 MeV per nucleon, which places them near the minimum of the stopping-power curve, where a particle loses the least energy per gram of material traversed. Each additional gram per square centimetre therefore removes only a small fraction of the energy of a particle with tens of grams per square centimetre of range still in hand. The dominant interaction at these energies is also nuclear rather than electronic: a heavy primary striking a nucleus in the shield fragments into several lighter nuclei plus neutrons and protons, all continuing forward. The shield converts one heavy ion into several lighter particles instead of removing it. For electronics inside a spacecraft, galactic cosmic rays are not shieldable at any practical mass, which is why they set the single-event requirement however the structure is arranged.

6 · Why aluminium, and where high-Z is right

Lead is the shielding material of everyday experience, and it is correct for the environment it is used in: the apron in a dental X-ray room stops photons of tens of keV, where the photoelectric cross-section rises steeply with atomic number and lead is far more effective per unit thickness than a light element. Spacecraft are built of aluminium instead. Three separate reasons converge, and only the first is obvious.

Mass. Launch cost scales with mass, and shielding competes directly with payload for the same budget. This is what makes shielding a trade rather than a solution, and it is why the meaningful comparison between materials is at equal areal density — equal areal density is what equal mass buys.

Bremsstrahlung. At equal areal density a high-atomic-number absorber is worse against an electron environment, not better. How much of an electron's energy loss goes into radiated photons rather than into ionization grows in proportion to the atomic number of the absorber.

(dE/dx)rad(dE/dx)col ≈ ZE700
(3.6)
Ratio of radiative to collisional mass stopping power for electrons, both in MeV·cm²/g · Z atomic number of the absorber · E electron kinetic energy (MeV). An order-of-magnitude relation: Attix puts 800 in the denominator where Knoll puts 700, so the absolute values carry perhaps 15%. At 1 MeV the ratio is 1.9% in aluminium (Z = 13) and 11.7% in lead (Z = 82). It is a ratio of stopping powers at one instantaneous energy, not the fraction of the electron energy ultimately radiated, which is smaller because the ratio collapses as the electron slows. Use it for the proportionality to Z, which is exact here and is the whole of the shielding argument.

Electrical conductivity. A spacecraft has no ground. Sunlit surfaces photoemit while shadowed surfaces collect ambient plasma electrons, and different materials charge to different potentials. The resulting differential potentials can reach kilovolts and discharge through the structure, and electrostatic discharge is a well-established cause of spacecraft anomalies. A continuous conductive skin bonded to a single reference bleeds the accumulated charge away and prevents the potentials from establishing. Aluminium is simultaneously the structure, the shield and that reference, so its shielding is obtained without a dedicated mass allocation at all.

Spot shielding is where high-Z material belongs

The mass argument applies to shielding the whole spacecraft. It reverses for a single small part. Placing one sensitive component behind a few grams of a dense material, or behind mass that is already present such as a battery or a propellant tank, costs a negligible fraction of the budget and buys a large local attenuation. The resulting design pattern is a light, conductive, structural shell for the vehicle plus selective local shielding for the few parts whose requirement the shell cannot meet. Graded shields — a high-atomic-number layer to stop the electrons, backed by a low-atomic-number layer to absorb the bremsstrahlung the first layer produced — apply the same reasoning within a single stack.

7 · The atmospheric cascade

The same galactic cosmic rays and solar protons that reach a spacecraft also arrive at the top of the Earth's atmosphere, and what happens next is the shielding problem of §5 taken to its limit. The atmosphere presents 1,033 g/cm² at sea level, calculated directly from surface pressure divided by gravitational acceleration. That is roughly a thousand times the areal density of a spacecraft wall, and no primary cosmic ray survives it. What reaches an aircraft or the ground is entirely secondary: particles created inside the shield by the primaries it stopped.

A primary proton of several GeV entering the upper atmosphere undergoes a nuclear interaction with a nitrogen or oxygen nucleus. The nucleus fragments, and the collision produces pions — the lightest strongly interacting particles, and the dominant product of high-energy hadronic collisions — together with neutrons, protons and nuclear fragments. Charged pions decay to muons and neutrinos; neutral pions decay to two photons, each of which initiates an electromagnetic shower of electron–positron pairs and further photons. The surviving hadrons interact again further down, so the hadronic component regenerates itself until the energy per particle falls below the threshold for further production. One primary particle becomes a cascade of many.

The cascade builds up and then attenuates, and the competition between the two produces a maximum in ionization rate part-way down. Near the top of the atmosphere there is too little material for the primaries to have interacted and the secondary population is still small; deep in the atmosphere the secondaries have themselves been absorbed. The peak between these regimes is the Pfotzer–Regener maximum, and it lies at 15–20 km altitude, corresponding to an atmospheric depth of 50–100 g/cm². The point of practical importance is that commercial aviation altitudes, at 10–13 km, sit on the rising side of that peak.

8 · Avionics, ground level, and what the neutron does there

At 33,000 to 42,000 feet — 10.1 to 12.8 km, the band commercial aircraft cruise in — the radiation field is dominated by the secondary neutron, and the upset rate there is roughly 300 times the sea-level value. That is the figure the course quotes, and it sits at the low end of a published range extending to about 500 for the >10 MeV neutron flux ratio. Muons and protons are also present, and electrons and photons carry much of the ionization, but for effects in electronics the neutron is the particle that matters, because it is both abundant and capable of depositing a large amount of energy in a very small volume.

A neutron carries no charge, so it produces no ionization along its path and cannot deposit energy the way a proton or a heavy ion does. It interacts instead through nuclear reactions with the silicon nuclei of the device, elastic and inelastic, producing recoiling nuclei and light fragments. Those products are charged and they are slow, so their linear energy transfer is high and they deposit their entire energy within a fraction of a micrometre. A single neutron that would have passed harmlessly through can therefore produce, at the point where it happens to react, a dense track of electron–hole pairs indistinguishable from that of a heavy ion. This is the indirect ionization mechanism introduced in M01.

The consequence is a sharp asymmetry between the two classes of effect in this environment. Total ionizing dose is not a concern for avionics. The accumulated dose over a working life at altitude is a small fraction of the occupational limits of §3, and far below what any part responds to. Single event effects are, and increasingly so. The mechanism has no threshold in accumulated dose: one neutron reacting at the wrong place in a memory cell flips a bit regardless of how little dose has been accumulated, so the rate depends on the flux and on the sensitivity of the technology and not on mission duration. Since that sensitivity increases as feature sizes and node capacitances shrink, the problem grows with each technology generation while the environment stays fixed.

The evidence tying in-flight upsets to atmospheric neutrons is correlational and was assembled by Taber and Normand: measured upset rates on avionics were plotted against altitude and latitude, and the resulting surface matched the independently calculated atmospheric neutron flux as a function of the same two variables. The latitude dependence has the same origin as the orbital dependence of M02. Geomagnetic cutoff rigidity falls towards the poles, so more of the primary cosmic ray spectrum enters the atmosphere at high latitude, more cascade is produced, and the neutron flux is correspondingly higher. Transpolar routes carry a several-fold higher upset rate than equatorial ones at the same altitude.

"SEE rate increases with altitude" holds below the peak

Within the atmosphere the upset rate rises steeply with altitude, and across the range of aircraft operation that statement is unambiguous. It stops being general above the Pfotzer–Regener maximum. The RaD-X balloon campaign measured energy-deposition count rates to 38 km; interpreted through the MAIRE and EXPACS transport models, those data show the neutron flux declining above about 20 km while the flux of primary cosmic ray ions continues to rise, so the dominant upset mechanism shifts from indirect ionization by neutrons to direct ionization by heavy ions. Whether the total upset rate keeps increasing then depends on the device: for a sensitive volume that is thin and planar, long chords make direct heavy-ion upset dominant and the peak is washed out, while for a compact cubic volume the neutron contribution dominates and the upset rate peaks with the ionization, at the Pfotzer–Regener altitude — in every case modelled except zero geomagnetic cutoff, where no peak appears at all. Stratospheric platforms and high-altitude vehicles fall in exactly this transition region. Hands et al., IEEE Trans. Nucl. Sci. 64, 587 (2017) — a reading assigned with this deck.

At sea level the same field persists, attenuated. The reference environment used by industry is defined in the JEDEC standard as New York City at sea level under mid-level solar activity, where the neutron flux above 10 MeV is 3.6×10⁻³ cm⁻²s⁻¹, or about 13 neutrons per cm² per hour. That is a small number, and it produces failures anyway, because modern memory arrays present an enormous number of sensitive nodes for it to act on.

This is why error detection and correction is standard in server memory, why automotive and avionics systems require it in safety-critical paths, and why ground-level soft errors became an engineering concern at all — the environment did not become harsher, the systems became larger and their nodes more sensitive.

9 · Man-made environments

The natural environments described so far are encountered. The environments in this section are produced, and they matter twice over: some systems must operate in them, and all of them serve as the ground test facilities the course later uses to qualify parts against everything above.

Accelerators. The Large Hadron Collider produces a mixed field of protons, neutrons, electrons and pions around its detectors, with future doses projected into the gigarad range — among the most severe environments electronics are required to survive anywhere. The same facilities serve as high-energy radiation test sources; ESA uses CERN for that purpose.

Reactors. Nuclear power plant instrumentation operates in a mixed gamma-and-neutron field, and accident conditions such as loss of cooling raise it substantially. Pulsed and fast burst reactors are also used deliberately as neutron and prompt-gamma test sources.

Weapon environments. A nuclear detonation produces prompt gamma rays, X-rays across a wide spectrum, neutrons, and the secondary charged particles that those generate in surrounding material. Two features distinguish it from every natural environment in this course. The dose arrives in a pulse rather than accumulating, so dose-rate effects and prompt photocurrents dominate over the slow trapping mechanisms of Part III. And the X-ray component drives dose enhancement at material interfaces, where the photoelectric cross-section's steep dependence on atomic number makes the local dose in a high-Z layer greatly exceed that in the adjacent silicon.

Cobalt-60 gammas at 1.17 and 1.33 MeV are not trapped electrons; a heavy-ion beam at a cyclotron is not a cosmic ray spectrum; a spallation source is not the atmospheric neutron spectrum. Every ground test involves an argument that the differences do not matter for the mechanism being qualified, and the failures of that argument are what hardness assurance exists to catch.

Why space parts are not commercial parts

The environments above explain a set of differences that otherwise look like conservatism. Space electronics carry a high radiation requirement against a commercial part's low one, on long design cycles rather than short ones, for low volumes rather than high, and are driven by reliability rather than by cost and performance. Replacement is effectively impossible, so a small failure risk that is acceptable on the ground is not acceptable in flight. They are packaged in hermetically sealed ceramic rather than plastic, and cooled radiatively rather than by convection, because there is no air. The static-charge environment is the differential charging problem of §6. Each of these is a consequence of the environment rather than a preference.

A part is qualified behind 200 mils of aluminium for a high-inclination LEO mission and the total dose requirement is met with margin. The mission is rescoped to a 20,000 km circular orbit. Why is doubling the shield thickness not a proportionate response?

Two separate reasons, and both follow from §5. The dose-depth curve is already past its steep region at 200 mils — 1.37 g/cm² is beyond the range of most of the trapped electron spectrum — so the second 200 mils removes far less dose than the first did. The curve has flattened onto its bremsstrahlung and high-energy proton components, neither of which has a finite range, and mass is being spent for a small fractional reduction.

The larger problem is that the environment has changed in kind, not only in magnitude. The new orbit is inside the outer electron belt, where the course quotes doses approaching 1 Mrad(Si) per year with light shielding, three orders of magnitude above the LEO figure. Closing a gap of that size with aluminium is not possible within any realistic mass budget. The available responses are selecting parts qualified to a higher dose, spot shielding the few parts that cannot be, and reconsidering the orbit — which is why the environment analysis belongs at mission definition rather than after part selection.

Where this leaves us

Absorbed dose is the mean energy deposited per unit mass, and it is a property of a stated material: 1 rad = 100 erg/g = 6.24×10¹³ eV/g, 1 Gy = 100 rad. It is obtained from an environment by integrating the linear energy transfer against the fluence spectrum, and the rate at which it accumulates is a separate specification from the total, because oxides anneal and bipolar devices degrade faster at low rates. Equivalent dose in sieverts and rem is a tissue quantity weighted by particle type, and it coincides numerically with absorbed dose only for photons and electrons.

Dose by orbit follows from which population dominates rather than from altitude. A shielded high-inclination low orbit takes under 1 krad(Si) per year; half of geosynchronous altitude, inside the outer electron belt, approaches 1 Mrad(Si) per year. Shielding removes essentially all trapped electrons within about 13 to 19 mm of aluminium, removes trapped protons only partially because their spectrum extends beyond any affordable range, and does not remove galactic cosmic rays at all — it fragments them. What remains behind a thick shield is bremsstrahlung created inside the shield and the high-energy end of the proton spectrum, and neither falls quickly with depth. That is the saturation. Aluminium is chosen over lead because mass is the binding constraint, because bremsstrahlung yield scales with atomic number, and because a conductive skin is simultaneously the answer to spacecraft charging.

The atmosphere is the same shielding problem at 1,033 g/cm², and it demonstrates the limiting case: the shield stops every primary and becomes the source. The cascade peaks at the Pfotzer–Regener maximum, 15–20 km, and at aircraft altitudes the field is neutron-dominated at roughly 300 times the sea-level flux. Total ionizing dose is not a concern there; single-event effects are, because a neutron needs no accumulated dose to flip a bit, only one nuclear reaction in the wrong place.

That completes the environment. Everything from here is the response of matter and devices to it. M04 supplies the energy-deposition physics that this module has been using on credit — stopping power, LET, range, the Bragg peak, and the conversion from deposited energy to electron–hole pairs — and M05 uses it to separate the three effects the rest of the course is organized around.

Quick reference

  • Absorbed dose is mean energy imparted per unit mass: energy entering minus energy leaving, divided by mass. 1 rad = 100 erg/g = 0.01 J/kg = 6.24×10¹³ eV/g; 1 Gy = 1 J/kg = 100 rad = 6.24×10¹⁵ eV/g. Always name the material — rad(Si), rad(SiO₂).
  • Fluence to dose: D(rad) ≈ 1.60×10⁻⁵ · LET · Φ with LET in MeV·cm²/mg and Φ in cm⁻². Both units exactly, or the constant is wrong.
  • Dose rate in space runs 10⁻⁶ to mid-10⁻³ rad(Si)/s. 1 rad/s = 3.156×10⁷ rad/yr. Dose rate is a separate requirement from total dose: oxides anneal, and bipolar devices are worse at low rate.
  • Equivalent dose H = wRD, in Sv or rem; 1 Sv = 100 rem, 10 mSv = 1 rem. wR = 1 photons and electrons, 2 protons, 20 alphas and heavy ions; neutrons follow a continuous function of energy from 2.5 to a maximum of 20.7 near 1 MeV (ICRP 103 — ICRP 60's older 5–20 step function is still widely quoted). Tissue only. Occupational limit ~50 mSv/yr, 5 mSv if pregnant.
  • Dose by orbit: LEO moderate, dominated by SAA protons and trapped electrons; polar and sun-synchronous more severe, from greater electron flux and reduced solar-proton cutoff; GEO dominated by outer-belt electrons and unattenuated solar protons. High-inclination LEO behind 200 mils Al (1.37 g/cm²): under 1 krad(Si)/yr. Half-GEO with light shielding: approaching 1 Mrad(Si)/yr.
  • Shield depth is areal density, xρ = ρt in g/cm². Aluminium: 1 mm = 0.270 g/cm².
  • Dose-depth decomposition: electrons fall steeply and stop; bremsstrahlung is made inside the shield and attenuates slowly; protons fall slowly because the spectrum reaches hundreds of MeV. The total flattens once the electrons are gone — that is why shielding saturates.
  • Ranges in aluminium: 1 MeV electron 0.41 g/cm² (1.5 mm); 3 MeV 1.48 g/cm² (5.5 mm); 7 MeV 3.60 g/cm² (13.4 mm); 10 MeV 5.19 g/cm² (19.2 mm); 100 MeV proton 9.99 g/cm² (37 mm). Electron range from Katz–Penfold, equation (3.5).
  • GCR are not shieldable. Flux peaks at 100–1000 MeV/nucleon, near minimum ionizing, and nuclear interactions in the shield fragment a heavy primary into lighter secondaries rather than stopping it.
  • Aluminium over lead: mass is the binding constraint; radiative-to-collisional stopping ratio ≈ ZE/700 makes lead six times worse for bremsstrahlung at equal areal density; and a conductive bonded skin prevents differential charging. High-Z belongs in spot and graded shields.
  • Atmosphere: 1,033 g/cm² at sea level, 197 g/cm² at 12 km, 77 g/cm² at 18 km. A primary interacts with N or O, producing pions, neutrons, protons and fragments; π± → µ±, π⁰ → γγ → electromagnetic shower. Cascade peaks at the Pfotzer–Regener maximum, 15–20 km.
  • Avionics, 33,000–42,000 ft: neutron-dominated, ~300× the sea-level flux. TID is not a concern; single-event effects are. Neutrons upset by indirect ionization — a nuclear reaction with a Si nucleus liberates a high-LET charged recoil. Polar routes are worse because cutoff rigidity falls towards the poles.
  • Ground level: JEDEC reference is New York City at sea level, 3.6×10⁻³ neutrons cm⁻²s⁻¹ above 10 MeV ≈ 13 cm⁻²h⁻¹. A 90 nm SRAM at 777 FIT/Mbit gives one error per Gbit per 1.3×10³ hours, and one per Pbit every 5 seconds. 1 FIT = 1 failure per 10⁹ device-hours.
  • Man-made environments: accelerators (LHC, gigarad range), reactors and accident conditions, and weapon environments — prompt gammas, X-rays, neutrons and secondary charged particles, delivered as a pulse. Simulators: Co-60 and fast burst reactors for total dose, flash X-ray and LINAC for prompt gamma, pulsed reactors and spallation sources for neutrons. The Van Allen belts are natural, not man-made.
