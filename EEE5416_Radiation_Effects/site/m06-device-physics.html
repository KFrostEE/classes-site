Module 06

Device Physics for Radiation Effects

The device machinery total ionizing dose acts through: how a MOS capacitor responds to gate bias, what a charge placed inside the oxide does to that response and how much it matters where the charge sits, how the same charge reaches the drain current of a transistor and the base current of a bipolar device, and how a charge-coupled device moves signal charge that defects can intercept.

Prerequisites M05 Read time ~40 min Sources Deck 03 pp. 42–44; Deck 04 pp. 5–34, 37–44, 47–48; Deck 05 pp. 4–15

M05 placed total ionizing dose as the cumulative ionizing cell of the taxonomy and stated its chain in four steps: pairs are generated in an oxide, some holes survive recombination, those holes transport to the silicon interface, and the trapped charge moves a device parameter. The first three steps are oxide physics and belong to M07. The fourth is device physics, and it cannot be made quantitative — or even stated with the right sign — without the electrostatics of the devices themselves.

This module supplies that electrostatics. It is deliberately radiation-free in its mechanisms: no process here creates charge, and the only radiation that appears is as the eventual source of a charge whose origin is deferred. What the module does establish is the machinery that converts a charge density into a measurable terminal quantity — the oxide capacitance that sets the exchange rate between charge and voltage, the position weighting that makes a charge near the silicon count fully and a charge near the gate count not at all, the occupancy rule that decides the sign of the interface contribution, and the exponential subthreshold characteristic on which the standard separation measurement depends.

Three device families follow, because the course examines all three and they fail differently. The MOS transistor responds to charge in its gate oxide through a threshold shift and to charge in its isolation oxides through leakage. The bipolar transistor responds through recombination at the surface where its emitter–base junction meets the oxide, which is a completely different mechanism reaching a completely different terminal. The charge-coupled device responds through the efficiency with which it passes a charge packet from one electrode to the next. Each is developed here in its unirradiated form, with the radiation response named and deferred.

Assumed background. This module takes as known: the band structure of a semiconductor, the meaning of the Fermi level and of doping, the existence of a depletion region at a junction, and the exponential current–voltage relation of a diode. Everything else it builds. The course itself assumes rather more — the syllabus treats this material as prerequisite from earlier device courses — so the coverage here is deliberately more complete than the lecture.

1 · The MOS capacitor and its three regimes

Every effect in Part III acts through a metal–oxide–semiconductor capacitor, either as a structure in its own right or as the gate stack of a transistor. It is three layers: a conducting gate, an insulating oxide, and a doped semiconductor substrate. The gate and the substrate are the two plates; the oxide is the dielectric. What makes it more than a capacitor is that one of its plates is a semiconductor, whose carrier concentration at the surface is itself controlled by the applied voltage.

Consider a substrate doped p-type, so that holes are the majority carrier. Applying a negative voltage to the gate attracts holes to the oxide interface. The surface carries a higher hole concentration than the bulk; this is accumulation, the semiconductor surface behaves as a conductor, and the structure is a parallel-plate capacitor whose dielectric is the oxide alone.

Applying a small positive voltage instead repels holes from the interface and leaves the immobile negative acceptor ions behind. The surface is stripped of mobile carriers to a depth W that grows with the applied voltage; this is depletion. The depleted layer is an insulator, so it adds a second capacitance in series with the oxide, and the total capacitance falls as W grows.

Raising the gate voltage further bends the bands far enough that the minority carrier — here the electron — becomes the more numerous species at the surface. This is inversion, and the surface layer is now n-type although the bulk is p-type. Once inversion is established, further increases in gate voltage are accommodated by adding electrons to that layer rather than by widening the depletion region, so the depletion width stops growing at a maximum value. The inversion layer is the conducting channel of a MOS transistor.

The gate voltage at which the semiconductor surface is neither accumulated nor depleted — where the bands are flat all the way to the interface — is the flat-band voltage, VFB. The gate voltage at which inversion is established is the threshold voltage, VT. Both are the reference points against which radiation-induced shifts are measured, and both move when charge appears inside the oxide.

2 · The capacitance–voltage curve

Sweeping the gate voltage and measuring the small-signal capacitance traces out the three regimes and is the standard characterisation of a MOS capacitor. It is also the measurement from which radiation-induced charge densities are extracted, so its shape must be understood in detail.

In accumulation the semiconductor surface is conductive and the measured capacitance is that of the oxide alone.

Cox = εoxtox
(6.1)
Cox oxide capacitance per unit area (F/cm²) · εox permittivity of the oxide (F/cm), equal to κoxε0 with κox = 3.9 for SiO₂ and ε0 = 8.854×10⁻¹⁴ F/cm, giving εox = 3.45×10⁻¹³ F/cm · tox oxide thickness (cm, not nm — this is the most common unit slip in the subject). A 5 nm oxide gives Cox = 6.9×10⁻⁷ F/cm², which is 6.9 fF per square micrometre of gate. On the formula sheet as (6.1).

In depletion the depleted silicon adds a capacitance CS = εSi/W in series with the oxide, so the total is CoxCS/(Cox + CS). Because W grows with gate voltage, the curve falls monotonically through this region.

In inversion the result depends on the measurement frequency, and the reason is the rate at which minority carriers can be supplied. At low frequency the inversion layer can be replenished from thermal generation in the bulk fast enough to follow the small-signal excitation, so the surface again behaves as a conductor and the capacitance returns to Cox. At high frequency it cannot; the inversion charge does not respond, the depletion layer at its maximum width still does, and the capacitance stays at the low value CoxCS,min/(Cox + CS,min). If the gate voltage is swept faster than minority carriers can be generated at all, no inversion layer forms, the depletion region grows past Wmax, and the capacitance falls below the high-frequency curve; this is deep depletion.

The two distortions marked in Figure 2 are the whole diagnostic content of the measurement, and Deck 04 states them in one line each: fixed charge translates the curve, and interface traps change the slope of the curve in depletion. A translation with no change of shape means charge that does not exchange carriers with the silicon and therefore contributes the same amount at every bias. A change of shape means states whose charge depends on where the Fermi level sits at the surface, so their contribution varies through the sweep and the curve is stretched along the voltage axis. M07 §9 turns this into the standard measurement that separates the two radiation-induced densities.

The frequency that separates the two branches is set by how fast the inversion layer can be replenished, and that rate is thermal generation in the depleted silicon rather than anything electrical. Generation is slow — the generation lifetime in device-grade silicon is orders of magnitude longer than the recombination lifetime — so the inversion charge follows only excitations well below the kilohertz range, and a conventional capacitance meter operating at 100 kHz or 1 MHz always reports the high-frequency branch. Obtaining the low-frequency branch therefore requires a different instrument rather than a lower dial setting: the quasi-static measurement ramps the gate voltage slowly and records the displacement current it drives, which gives the capacitance directly at an effective frequency near zero. The two branches taken together are more informative than either alone, because the interface-trap capacitance appears in the low-frequency curve and not in the high-frequency one, and the difference between them is a direct measure of the interface-trap density across the gap.

3 · Charge in the oxide, and where it sits

An oxide is never charge-free. Deck 04 names four populations, distinguished by where they sit and whether their charge state depends on bias.

  • Mobile ionic charge, QM — alkali contamination, principally sodium and potassium, which drifts through the oxide under bias at elevated temperature. Its signature is a flat-band voltage that changes with bias-temperature history, producing hysteresis in a swept measurement.
  • Fixed oxide charge, QF — positive charge sitting within a few nanometres of the silicon interface, created during oxidation, independent of bias and immobile.
  • Interface-trapped charge, Qit — states at the silicon surface whose charge state depends on the surface potential, treated in §5.
  • Oxide-trapped charge, ρox — charge captured at defect sites distributed through the oxide bulk. This is the population radiation creates, and it is the reason the position dependence below matters.

Any charge inside the oxide must be balanced by image charge on the two conductors that surround it, and the split between them is what determines its effect. Deck 04 states the division: charge in the oxide is mirrored in the gate electrode and the substrate, and the closer the charge is to the silicon, the greater its effect on the silicon. A charge sitting immediately against the gate is imaged entirely on the gate, induces nothing in the silicon, and does not shift the flat-band voltage at all. Moving it toward the silicon transfers a growing share of the image charge to the substrate, where it does act on the surface.

The flat-band condition makes that quantitative, and it is the condition the measurement of §2 reports. Flat band is by definition the gate voltage at which there is no field in the substrate, so the applied bias has been adjusted until all the image charge has returned to the gate. The field the trapped sheet drives then occupies only the oxide lying between the gate and the sheet, and the voltage dropped across that region is exactly the shift in flat-band voltage. A sheet against the gate spans no oxide and costs nothing; a sheet at the silicon interface spans the whole oxide and costs the most. Deck 04 states the limiting case in those terms: the effect is maximum when x1 = xox.

Weighting each element of charge by its distance from the gate gives the general result.

ΔVFB = −1εox ∫0tox x ρ(x) dx = −QoxCox · x̄tox
(6.2)
ΔVFB shift in flat-band voltage (V) · ρ(x) volume charge density in the oxide (C/cm³) · x distance measured from the gate electrode (cm) · tox oxide thickness (cm) · Qox = ∫ρ dx, the total areal charge density (C/cm²) · x̄ its centroid, the charge-weighted mean distance from the gate (cm). Positive Qox gives a negative shift. The full Qox/Cox is recovered only when every charge sits at the silicon interface, x̄ = tox. On the formula sheet as (6.2).

Two consequences run through the rest of Part III. First, the sign: radiation-induced oxide charge is net positive, so it always shifts the flat-band and threshold voltages negative, in an n-channel and a p-channel device alike. Second, the geometry: because trapped holes accumulate close to the silicon interface rather than uniformly, x̄ is near tox and the shift is close to its maximum possible value for the charge present. This is also why the same areal density of trapped charge is far more damaging in a thick oxide than a thin one — the charge scales with thickness while Cox falls with it, which M07 §3 makes quantitative.

4 · The MOS transistor above threshold

A MOS field-effect transistor is the capacitor of §1 with two heavily doped contacts, the source and the drain, placed at either end of the surface so that the inversion layer forms a channel between them. Gate voltage controls whether the channel exists and how much charge it holds; drain voltage draws current along it. For an n-channel device on a p-type substrate, the channel is an electron layer and the device conducts once VGS exceeds VT.

The charge per unit area in the channel is Cox(VGS − VT) at the source end, falling toward the drain as the local channel-to-gate voltage falls. Integrating along the channel gives the current.

ID = µnCox WL [ (VGS − VT)VDS − VDS22 ]
(6.3)
ID drain current (A) · µn effective channel electron mobility (cm²/V·s) · Cox from (6.1) (F/cm²) · W, L channel width and length (same units, so the ratio is dimensionless) · VGS, VDS gate–source and drain–source voltages (V) · VT threshold voltage (V). Valid in the linear region, VDS < VGS − VT, where the channel reaches from source to drain.

When VDS reaches VGS − VT the channel charge falls to zero at the drain end and the channel pinches off. Beyond that point the current stops rising with drain voltage, which is the saturation region: substituting VDS = VGS − VT into (6.3) gives ID,sat = (µnCox/2)(W/L)(VGS − VT)². In a short-channel device the saturation current rises less steeply than this square law: the short channel puts the carriers in a high enough lateral field that their drift velocity saturates, and ID,sat becomes roughly proportional to (VGS − VT) rather than to its square.

Equation (6.3) is the reason the two radiation-induced quantities of Part III reach the terminal current by different routes, and Deck 04 separates them explicitly. A change in VT translates the transfer characteristic along the gate-voltage axis: the same current is available, at a different gate voltage. A change in µn scales it: less current at every bias point, with the threshold unmoved. A third change, the stretchout produced by interface traps, distorts it. All three occur together in an irradiated device, and reading a single post-irradiation curve requires knowing which signature is which.

Because the threshold voltage is defined by a condition on surface potential rather than by anything directly measurable, extracting it from a measured curve requires a convention, and different conventions give slightly different numbers. Deck 04 gives two that the course uses. The constant-current method defines VT as the gate voltage at which the drain current reaches 100 nA × (W/L), which scales the criterion with device geometry. The transconductance-peak method defines it as the gate voltage at which gm = dID/dVGS is largest, corrected by half the drain voltage: VT = VG(at gm peak) − 0.5VDS. A shift quoted without its extraction convention cannot be compared across studies.

5 · Interface traps and their occupancy

At the boundary between crystalline silicon and amorphous silicon dioxide the lattice terminates, and not every silicon atom at the surface finds four bonding partners. Those that do not leave an unsatisfied bond — a dangling bond — whose energy level lies inside the silicon bandgap. These are interface traps. Their areal density per unit energy is Dit, in cm⁻²eV⁻¹, and the integrated areal density is Nit, in cm⁻².

An interface trap exchanges carriers with the silicon, so its charge state is not fixed: it is set by whether its level sits above or below the Fermi level at the surface, which the gate voltage controls. Deck 04 gives the two kinds and the occupancy rule.

  • Donor-like traps are neutral when occupied by an electron and positive when empty.
  • Acceptor-like traps are neutral when empty and negative when occupied.

Radiation-induced interface traps in silicon are distributed such that the states below midgap are donor-like and those above midgap are acceptor-like. The consequence is the single most useful property in the whole of Part III: at midgap the net interface-trap charge is zero. Below midgap the donor-like states are filled and therefore neutral; above midgap the acceptor-like states are empty and therefore also neutral. Whatever the density, the contribution vanishes when the Fermi level at the surface crosses midgap.

The sign of the interface-trap contribution therefore depends on the channel type, because the two channel types are biased to opposite sides of midgap at threshold. An n-channel device inverts when the surface Fermi level moves up toward the conduction band, filling acceptor-like states above midgap, so the interface charge at threshold is negative and the threshold shift is positive. A p-channel device inverts when the surface Fermi level moves down toward the valence band, emptying donor-like states below midgap, so the interface charge at threshold is positive and the threshold shift is negative.

Combining this with §3 gives the sign structure of the whole of total ionizing dose in MOS devices. Oxide-trapped charge is positive and shifts the threshold negative in both channel types. Interface-trapped charge shifts an n-channel threshold positive and a p-channel threshold negative. A p-channel device therefore has two contributions pushing the same way and degrades monotonically; an n-channel device has two contributions in opposition, so its net shift can move negative early and return later as interface traps continue to build. The measured ΔVT is the sum, and M07 §9 is the technique for recovering the two terms from it.

6 · Subthreshold conduction and mobility

Below threshold the channel is not empty. The surface is depleted rather than inverted, but a small population of electrons is present, and their concentration at the source end depends exponentially on the surface potential. Conduction in this regime is by diffusion rather than drift, which makes the transistor behave much as a bipolar transistor does, and the drain current falls exponentially with gate voltage.

The steepness is quantified by the subthreshold swing: the gate voltage change required to change the drain current by one decade.

S = kTq ln10 (1 + Cd + CitCox) , Cit = q Dit
(6.4)
S subthreshold swing (V/decade) · kT/q thermal voltage, 0.02585 V at 300 K · Cd depletion capacitance per unit area (F/cm²) · Cit interface-trap capacitance per unit area (F/cm²) · Dit interface-trap density (cm⁻²eV⁻¹) — with the density expressed per electronvolt, the conversion is a single factor of q = 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ C, not q². The ideal minimum, with Cd and Cit both negligible, is S0 = (kT/q)ln10 = 59.5 mV/decade at 300 K. On the formula sheet as (6.4).

The course states this without the depletion term, as S = S0(1 + qDit/Cox), and homework solutions use that form. It isolates the radiation-induced term, which is what the problems are about, but it predicts exactly 59.5 mV/decade for an unirradiated device where a real transistor measures higher because Cd is never zero. Use the course form for course work and expect the discrepancy against measured pre-irradiation data.

Two properties make the subthreshold region the most informative part of the transfer characteristic for radiation work. Its slope depends on Dit and on nothing else that radiation changes, so stretchout measures interface traps directly. And it spans many decades of current, so a threshold shift of a few tens of millivolts displaces it visibly. The leakage current of an integrated circuit at zero gate bias is read off the same curve, which is why a negative threshold shift in an n-channel device translates immediately into standby supply current.

Mobility is the third quantity radiation changes. Carriers in the inversion layer travel within a few nanometres of the interface, so charged centres at or near that interface scatter them by Coulomb interaction, and the effective mobility falls as their density rises. Deck 04 gives the empirical form used in the field.

µ = µ01 + αit ΔNit + αot ΔNot
(6.5)
µ effective channel mobility after irradiation (cm²/V·s) · µ0 pre-irradiation mobility (cm²/V·s) · ΔNit, ΔNot radiation-induced areal densities of interface traps and of oxide-trapped charge (cm⁻²) · αit, αot empirical scattering parameters (cm²), fitted per technology, with αit the larger of the two. Both populations scatter channel carriers, so both appear. Deck 04 notes the assumption the expression rests on: the effect of oxide charge on mobility is projected onto the interface and falls off strongly with distance of the charge into the oxide, which is what allows ΔNot — a charge distributed through the oxide — to be written as a single areal density at all. Empirical: a fitting form, not a derivation.

7 · The bipolar transistor

A bipolar junction transistor operates on an entirely different principle from a MOSFET and fails under radiation for entirely different reasons, so the course treats both. It is two junctions sharing a thin central region: in an n–p–n device, an n-type emitter, a thin p-type base, and an n-type collector. In forward-active operation the emitter–base junction is forward biased and the base–collector junction reverse biased. Electrons injected from the emitter into the base diffuse across it, and because the base is thin most arrive at the collector junction and are swept across. The fraction that does not arrive is lost to recombination in the base.

Current gain is the ratio of the current that reaches the collector to the current the base terminal must supply.

β = ICIB , IB = IB1 + IB2 + IB3
(6.6)
β common-emitter current gain (dimensionless) · IC collector current (A) · IB base current (A). The three base-current components, in Deck 04's numbering: IB1 holes injected from the base back into the emitter · IB2 recombination inside the emitter–base depletion region · IB3 recombination with electrons crossing the neutral base. Total ionizing dose acts principally on IB2. Gain falls because the denominator grows, not because the numerator shrinks. On the formula sheet as (6.6).

The three components are distinguishable by how they scale with base–emitter voltage, and that is how a measurement separates them. Recombination in the neutral base tracks the injected carrier concentration, so IB3 ∝ exp(qVBE/(kT)), an ideality factor of 1. Recombination inside the depletion region is fastest where the electron and hole concentrations are equal, and at that crossover point the product of the two concentrations gives IB2 ∝ exp(qVBE/(2kT)), an ideality factor of 2. Plotting collector and base current logarithmically against base–emitter voltage — a Gummel plot — displays both as straight lines of different slope, so a rise in the ideality-2 component is visible as an excess at low bias while the collector current is unchanged. Deck 04 records that the ideality factor of the radiation-induced base current is usually between 1 and 2.

The reason the surface matters so much is that recombination there is governed by the interface traps of §5 rather than by bulk defects. In the bulk, recombination through a single trap level follows the Shockley–Read–Hall rate, R = (pn − ni²) / [τn0(p + p1) + τp0(n + n1)], which is largest where the electron and hole concentrations are comparable. At the surface the same expression is integrated over the continuum of interface states, and to a good approximation the surface recombination rate is the excess carrier concentration times a surface recombination velocity, Rs ≈ s Δns, which is the form Deck 04 gives. That velocity is itself proportional to the areal density of interface states, s = σ vthNit, with σ the capture cross-section (cm²) and vth the carrier thermal velocity (cm/s) — note that this uses the integrated density Nit in cm⁻², not the per-energy Dit. More interface states means a higher surface recombination velocity, a larger IB2, and a lower gain. This is the mechanism, and it is a different mechanism from the threshold shift that dominates a MOSFET even though both originate in the same interface states.

Geometry decides which bipolar devices are most exposed to it. An n–p–n transistor is usually vertical, with current flowing down from a surface emitter into a buried collector, so only the perimeter of the emitter–base junction touches the oxide. Deck 04 lists three p–n–p arrangements — vertical, lateral and substrate — and singles out the lateral p–n–p as typically very sensitive, because its carriers travel laterally just below the surface and therefore spend their transit adjacent to the oxide rather than crossing it once.

Oxide charge acts on a bipolar device as well, but electrostatically rather than through recombination, and its effect has opposite signs in the two families. Positive charge in the oxide above an n–p–n transistor's p-type base depletes that surface, widening the region where electron and hole concentrations are comparable and so increasing the area over which fast recombination occurs. Above a lateral p–n–p transistor's n-type base the same positive charge accumulates the surface, which suppresses recombination and partly offsets the degradation. The two mechanisms — interface traps raising the recombination velocity, oxide charge changing the area over which it acts — must be kept separate, because they do not even share a sign.

One further behaviour has no MOSFET counterpart. Deck 04 records that bipolar degradation at a given total dose is often worse when the dose is delivered slowly, which is enhanced low-dose-rate sensitivity. Laboratory irradiations commonly run above 50 rad(SiO₂)/s to keep test time manageable, while space dose rates are typically below 0.01 rad(SiO₂)/s, so a part qualified quickly can be substantially worse in orbit than the test predicted. The oxides at risk are thick, low-quality and at low electric field — bipolar isolation oxides of hundreds of nanometres rather than gate oxides. The mechanism belongs to M08; what matters here is that it exists and that it inverts the usual expectation that slower is gentler.

8 · Isolation structures

The gate oxide is not the only oxide on a chip, and in a modern process it is not the one that fails first. Transistors must be electrically isolated from their neighbours, and the isolation is made of the same silicon dioxide — but hundreds of nanometres thick rather than a few, grown or deposited under conditions chosen for planarity rather than for electrical quality, and covered by whatever interconnect happens to pass overhead.

Positive charge trapped in a field oxide acts on the silicon beneath it exactly as gate charge acts on a channel: it drives the surface toward n-type. Where that silicon is p-type substrate separating two n-type regions, enough positive charge inverts it and opens a conducting path where the design intended none. Deck 04 identifies this as the primary total-dose failure mechanism in advanced CMOS integrated circuits, and its observable is an increase in supply current rather than a change in any transistor's characteristics.

The countermeasure at process level is a channel-stop implant: extra doping at the silicon surface beneath the isolation, which raises the threshold voltage of the parasitic field-oxide transistor so that more trapped charge is required to invert it. Deck 04 shows the effect directly as a comparison between low and high p⁺ doping under the same trapped-hole density.

Silicon-on-insulator technology introduces a second isolation oxide with the same vulnerability. An SOI transistor is built in a thin silicon island sitting on a buried oxide that separates it from the substrate. That buried oxide is thick, and positive charge trapped in it inverts the underside of the silicon island — creating a back channel that connects source to drain along a path the front gate does not control. Deck 04 gives the diagnostic signature: back-channel leakage is nearly independent of gate voltage, while leakage along the gate-controlled edge of the island does increase with gate voltage, so the combined characteristic of an irradiated SOI transistor can show a distinct step.

9 · Charge-coupled devices

A charge-coupled device is the third structure the course examines, and it is built directly from the capacitor of §1. An array of MOS gates over a common substrate holds signal charge in potential wells formed beneath positively biased electrodes. Clocking the electrode voltages in sequence moves each charge packet from one well to the next, down a row, to a single output amplifier. Deck 03 describes a three-phase device, in which each pixel has three electrodes, and implanted channel stops confine the packet to one row as it travels.

Two design features set the radiation response. The signal charge travels in a buried channel — a doped layer holding the packet slightly below the silicon surface rather than against it — specifically to keep it away from interface traps. And the device reads out a very small current through a very long chain of transfers, so every inefficiency in a single transfer is multiplied by the number of transfers the packet makes.

That multiplication is what makes the governing figure of merit a per-transfer quantity.

CTI = 1 − CTE , QoutQin = CTEn ≈ 1 − n · CTI
(6.7)
CTE charge transfer efficiency, the fraction of a packet delivered in one transfer (dimensionless, just below 1) · CTI charge transfer inefficiency, the fraction left behind (dimensionless, typically 10⁻⁵ or smaller before irradiation) · n number of transfers the packet makes, which is of order the array dimension · Qin, Qout packet charge at the start and end of the chain. The approximation holds while n · CTI ≪ 1. On the formula sheet as (6.7).

Deck 03 adds a behaviour that is counter-intuitive until the trap picture is in place: deliberately introducing a background charge improves transfer efficiency, because the background fills the traps and leaves them unable to interact with the signal packet. The improvement depends on signal size, and it usually comes at the cost of additional noise. The measured example is an EEV CCD02 exposed to a fluence of 7.2×10⁹ 10 MeV protons/cm², corresponding to 4 krad(Si) — the proton energy is part of the datum, since a fluence converts to a dose only at a stated energy.

A charge-coupled device is therefore sensitive to both cumulative effect classes at once, by two independent routes. Total ionizing dose acts through the gate oxides in the way §3 and §5 describe, shifting flat-band voltages and raising dark current through interface traps. Displacement damage acts through the silicon, creating bulk traps in the buried channel that intercept and delay signal charge and so degrade transfer efficiency directly. Deck 04's summary separates them cleanly: dark current increases from interface traps, charge transfer inefficiency from traps that delay charge transfer, producing streaks and noise. M12 develops the displacement side.

Two n-channel transistors are made in the same process with gate oxides of 10 nm and 40 nm. Both are irradiated to the same dose, and in each case the trapped holes end up near the silicon interface. How much larger is the threshold shift of the thicker device, and which two factors in (6.1) and (6.2) produce that ratio?

About sixteen times larger. Two factors of four multiply. The number of electron–hole pairs generated in the oxide is proportional to the oxide volume, so at fixed dose the areal density of trapped charge Qox scales with tox — one factor of four. And Cox = εox/tox is inversely proportional to thickness, so the same charge produces a larger voltage in the thicker oxide — a second factor of four. Since the shift is Qox/Cox with the centroid at the interface, the product gives a tox² dependence.

Deck 04 calls the first of those two factors a very simple model, and the hedge is worth keeping: measured exponents run between about 2 and 3 rather than exactly 2. Below roughly 5 nm a second mechanism takes over entirely, since electrons tunnelling from the silicon neutralize traps close to the interface, and thin oxides fall off far faster than any power law. M07 §3 assembles both. None of this says anything about isolation oxides, which did not thin with the gate — which is why §8's leakage mechanism became dominant as gate oxides stopped being the problem.

Where this leaves us

Charge placed inside an oxide shifts the flat-band and threshold voltages by Qox/Cox, weighted by how far the charge sits from the gate, so positive trapped charge near the silicon interface produces the largest possible negative shift. States at the interface itself add a second contribution whose sign depends on channel type, because they are neutral at midgap and fill with electrons as an n-channel device is driven toward inversion. Both are read from the same measurement: the first as a translation of the characteristic, the second as a change of its slope. Mobility falls as the density of interface-referred charge rises.

The same interface states reach a bipolar transistor by a different route entirely — raising the surface recombination velocity where the emitter–base junction meets the oxide, which increases base current and lowers gain, worst at low injection. Isolation and buried oxides carry the same trapped charge and control parasitic channels that no terminal switches off. A charge-coupled device is exposed at its gate oxides and in its buried channel at once, and multiplies any per-transfer loss by the length of its readout chain.

Every one of those statements begins with charge already present in an oxide. M07 supplies its origin: how much charge a given dose generates, what fraction of it survives prompt recombination and what controls that fraction, how the surviving holes reach the interface and how long they take, what traps them when they arrive, and how the interface states themselves are created by a process that takes thousands of seconds to complete.

Quick reference

  • MOS capacitor regimes (p-substrate): accumulation below VFB, depletion between VFB and VT, inversion above VT. Depletion width stops growing at Wmax once inversion is established.
  • Cox = εox/tox, F/cm², with εox = 3.9ε0 = 3.45×10⁻¹³ F/cm. tox in cm. Check: 5 nm gives 6.9×10⁻⁷ F/cm² = 6.9 fF/µm².
  • C–V signatures: oxide charge translates the curve; interface traps stretch it in depletion. Low frequency returns to Cox in inversion, high frequency does not, deep depletion falls below both.
  • Four oxide charge populations: QM mobile ions (hysteresis), QF fixed charge near the interface, Qit interface traps (bias-dependent), ρox oxide-trapped charge (what radiation makes).
  • Position matters: ΔVFB = −(Qox/Cox)(x̄/tox), with x measured from the gate. Charge at the gate has no effect; charge at the silicon interface has maximum effect.
  • Sign structure: positive oxide charge shifts VT negative in both channel types. Interface traps shift n-channel positive and p-channel negative, because acceptor-like states above midgap fill as an n-channel inverts. Net interface charge is zero at midgap.
  • Transfer characteristic: threshold shift translates it, interface traps stretch it, mobility loss scales it down. VT extraction by constant current at 100 nA·(W/L), or gm peak minus 0.5VDS.
  • Subthreshold swing: S0 = (kT/q)ln10 = 59.5 mV/decade at 300 K; Cit = qDit with Dit per eV. The course omits Cd.
  • BJT: β = IC/IB; dose acts on IB2, recombination in the emitter–base depletion region, ideality factor 2, worst at low bias. Surface recombination velocity s ∝ σvthDit. Lateral p–n–p devices are the most sensitive geometry.
  • Oxide charge on a BJT is electrostatic and signed: it depletes an n–p–n p-type base surface (aggravating) and accumulates a lateral p–n–p n-type base surface (opposing).
  • ELDRS: more bipolar degradation at low dose rate. Lab typically >50 rad(SiO₂)/s, space typically <0.01 rad(SiO₂)/s. Risk oxides are thick, low quality, low field.
  • Isolation leakage is the primary total-dose failure mechanism in advanced CMOS: trapped positive charge inverts the p-type surface under a field oxide and raises supply current. Channel-stop implants raise the parasitic threshold.
  • SOI back channel: charge in the buried oxide inverts the underside of the body. Back-channel leakage is nearly gate-voltage independent; edge leakage is not.
  • CCD: CTI = 1 − CTE, and a packet making n transfers retains CTEn. TID raises dark current through interface traps; displacement damage degrades CTI through bulk traps. A background charge fills traps and improves CTI at the cost of noise.
