Module 08

TID Effects on Devices and ICs

What the two trapped-charge densities do once the device is operating: which terms of the threshold voltage they move and which they leave untouched, how the transfer characteristic deforms, why the isolation oxide fails before the gate oxide, what layout does about it, and how the whole picture changed as gate oxides thinned.

Prerequisites M06, M07 Read time ~55 min Sources Deck 08; Deck 04 pp. 20–31, 37–43; Lecture 6

M07 ended with two numbers. A dose delivered to an oxide leaves behind an areal density of trapped holes, ΔNot, concentrated within a few nanometres of the silicon interface, and an areal density of interface traps, ΔNit, distributed in energy across the silicon bandgap at that same interface. This module spends those two numbers. It asks what a transistor built around that oxide does differently afterwards, then what a circuit built from those transistors does, and finally which part of the structure fails first once both are accounted for.

The answer is not the one the previous two modules set up. Almost all of M06 and M07 concerned the gate oxide, because that is where the physics is cleanest and where the measurements are made. In a manufactured integrated circuit the gate oxide is rarely what fails. The oxides that isolate one transistor from the next are one to two orders of magnitude thicker, they are grown or deposited to a lower standard, and they sit at low electric field — and every one of those three properties makes them trap more charge and convert it into more volts. Deck 04 states the conclusion directly: isolation-related leakage is the primary total-dose failure mechanism in advanced CMOS integrated circuits. Sections 5 to 7 are about that failure, and sections 1 to 4 are what is needed to describe it precisely.

One organising idea runs through the whole module. Radiation does not change a semiconductor's doping, and it does not change a metal's work function; the chemistry and the impurity profile of a finished device are set during fabrication and survive the exposure. What radiation changes is the charge stored in the dielectric and the electronic states at the dielectric–silicon boundary. Every device-level consequence in this module is a consequence of those two quantities moving while everything else holds still.

1 · What radiation changes, and what it leaves alone

The threshold voltage of a MOS transistor is a sum of four physically separate contributions, and separating them is what makes the radiation response predictable. The gate and the silicon are different materials, so their Fermi levels differ and a contact potential appears between them before any bias is applied. Charge stored in the oxide images itself on the gate and on the silicon and contributes a further term. The silicon surface must be bent far enough to invert, which costs the surface potential at strong inversion. And the depletion charge exposed in the silicon by that band bending must itself be supported by the gate.

Vth = φMS − Qot + QitCox + 2φF + |Qdep|Cox
(8.3)
Vth threshold voltage of an n-channel transistor on a p-type substrate (V) · φMS gate–semiconductor work-function difference (V), fixed by the two materials and their doping · Qot areal oxide-trapped charge (C/cm²), positive after irradiation · Qit areal interface-trap charge at threshold (C/cm²), negative for an n-channel device and positive for a p-channel device · Cox oxide capacitance per unit area (F/cm²) from (6.1) · φF bulk Fermi potential (V), set by substrate doping · Qdep = −qNAWdm depletion charge at strong inversion (C/cm²). Strong-inversion definition, Ψs = 2φF. The oxide-charge term carries the centroid weighting of (6.2); it is written here without it for compactness because radiation-induced holes sit close to the interface, where that weighting approaches one. On the formula sheet as (8.3).

Of those four terms, ionizing radiation moves exactly two. The work-function difference depends on the gate material and the substrate doping, neither of which a photon or an electron alters at the fluences this course considers. The bulk Fermi potential depends on the substrate doping alone, so it is fixed by the same argument, and the depletion charge depends on doping and on φF, so it is fixed in turn. Trapped oxide charge and interface-trap charge are the only free variables, which reduces the entire device-level problem to ΔVth = −(ΔQot + ΔQit)/Cox.

The same reduction is what makes the midgap technique of M07 §9 legitimate. Because only Qot and Qit move, a measured shift of the whole characteristic is a measurement of those two densities and nothing else, and a second reference point on the same curve is enough to separate them.

2 · Reading the transfer characteristic

Nearly every experimental result in total-dose work is a drain current plotted against gate voltage, measured before irradiation and again after, at fixed and small drain bias. The curve is called the transfer characteristic. It is drawn twice, on linear and on logarithmic current axes, because the two axes expose different physics and a result quoted from one describes a different quantity from a result quoted from the other.

On a linear current axis the useful region is above threshold, where ID is proportional to μCox(W/L)(VGS − Vth) by (6.3). Two independent things can happen to that line: it can move sideways, and its slope can fall. A sideways translation is a threshold shift and nothing else. A reduced slope is a reduced μCoxW/L, and since the geometry and the oxide capacitance are fixed by fabrication, it is a reduced channel mobility.

On a logarithmic current axis the useful region is below threshold, where the drain current is a diffusion current controlled exponentially by the surface potential. A decade of current costs a fixed increment of gate voltage — the subthreshold swing of (6.4) — so the characteristic is a straight line on log axes. Here the two independent changes are again a translation and a slope, but the slope now carries different information: a shallower subthreshold line is a larger swing, which by (6.4) is a larger interface-trap density. The deformation is called stretchout, because the curve is drawn out along the voltage axis rather than displaced along it.

The off-state current deserves its own statement, because it is the quantity that ends the life of a digital part. In the subthreshold region the drain current depends exponentially on the gate overdrive, so a shift of the characteristic multiplies the current at fixed gate voltage rather than adding to it.

ID ≈ I0 exp [VG − VthnVT]  ⟹  ratio = exp (ΔVnVT) = 10ΔV/S
(8.1)
ID subthreshold drain current (A) · VG gate voltage (V) · Vth threshold voltage (V) · n ideality factor, dimensionless, typically 1.3–1.5 · VT = kT/q = 0.0259 V at 300 K · ΔV magnitude of the threshold shift (V) · S subthreshold swing (V/decade). The two ratio forms are equivalent because S = nVT ln10; use whichever the problem supplies. Valid below threshold only. The ratio as written is an increase, and applies to a shift toward turn-on — negative for an n-channel device. A shift in the other direction divides the current by the same factor, which is what makes a rebounded n-channel part and an irradiated p-channel part leak less, not more. On the formula sheet as (8.1).

A 0.15 V negative shift at n = 1.5 multiplies the off-state current of an n-channel transistor by about 47. The same shift is invisible on a linear plot whose full scale is several volts. That asymmetry — a negligible change in the quantity a designer watches, accompanied by a factor of fifty in the quantity that sets standby power — is why total-dose failure so often arrives without warning from the parameters on a specification sheet.

Deck 08 develops the same point statistically. On rad-hard Honeywell wafers irradiated to 3 Mrad(SiO₂) with 10 keV X-rays, the distribution of n-channel threshold shifts across a wafer is narrow, yet the distribution of drain-current changes is broad, because the exponential of a narrow distribution is a wide one. Static supply leakage of the finished circuits follows the transistor leakage closely, which is what makes a single wafer-level transistor measurement a usable predictor of whole-circuit behaviour.

3 · The two components in service

The two trapped-charge densities move the characteristic in opposite directions in an n-channel transistor, and they do so on different timescales. Trapped holes in the oxide are positive. They attract electrons to the silicon surface, so the surface inverts at a less positive gate voltage and the threshold moves negative. Interface traps in an n-channel device are predominantly acceptor-like in the upper half of the bandgap: at threshold, with the surface Fermi level near the conduction band, they are filled with electrons and therefore negatively charged. Negative charge at the interface repels channel electrons, so the threshold moves positive.

ΔVth = ΔVot + ΔVit,    ΔVot < 0 always,    ΔVit > 0 (n-channel), ΔVit < 0 (p-channel)
(8.4)
ΔVth net threshold shift (V) · ΔVot = −qΔNot/Cox, contribution of trapped holes (V), negative for both channel types because the trapped charge is positive in both · ΔVit contribution of interface traps (V), of magnitude qΔNit/Cox over the part of the gap that charges, with a sign that follows the channel type because the position of the surface Fermi level at threshold decides which states are occupied. In a p-channel device both terms are negative and they add; in an n-channel device they oppose. On the formula sheet as (8.4).

That sign difference produces the two channel types' opposite fates. In a p-channel transistor the two contributions add, both negative, and the device becomes progressively harder to turn on: the threshold moves away from zero, the drive current falls, and the device switches off more firmly than it did when new. Deck 08's 0.25 µm p-channel data show exactly this, with no leakage problem arising. In an n-channel transistor the two contributions oppose, and what the device does depends on which is larger at the moment of measurement.

Which is larger changes with time, and the direction of that change is fixed by the kinetics of M07. Trapped hole charge is at its maximum immediately after the exposure ends and anneals away afterwards by tunneling and thermal emission. Interface traps are at their minimum immediately after the exposure and continue to build for thousands of seconds, because their formation waits on hydrogen transport rather than on the ionizing event. The negative component therefore decays while the positive component grows. An n-channel threshold that moved negative during irradiation turns around, returns through its pre-irradiation value, and continues positive — a behaviour called rebound, or superrecovery.

Rebound is why a total-dose qualification cannot end when the irradiation does. Deck 08 describes the accepted procedure: after the specified dose, the part receives an additional 50% of that dose and then a 168-hour biased anneal at 100 °C. What the bake accelerates is the annealing of the trapped holes — the slow, roughly logarithmic-in-time process of M07 §7, which in orbit would run for months or years. Removing the negative component in a week leaves the interface-trap contribution uncompensated, which is exactly the positive-threshold worst case the test exists to find. Hardness assurance develops this in M15.

4 · Mobility, drive and timing

Interface traps do more than shift a voltage. Once created, they are charged centres sitting within a nanometre or two of the channel, and every carrier moving from source to drain passes through their Coulomb potential. The scattering that results reduces the effective channel mobility, and because it is scattering from fixed charges rather than from lattice vibrations, it becomes relatively more important at low temperature and at low vertical field, where phonon and surface-roughness scattering are weak.

μeff = μ01 + αΔNit
(6.5)
μeff post-irradiation effective channel mobility (cm²/V·s) · μ0 pre-irradiation value (cm²/V·s) · ΔNit radiation-induced interface-trap areal density (cm⁻²) · α empirical scattering parameter (cm²), fitted per technology. Empirical rather than derived; it folds charge trapped at several depths into one interface-referred density, which works because the Coulomb effect falls off steeply with distance from the channel. Introduced in M06 §6 and repeated here because it drives three separate circuit-level failures. On the formula sheet as (6.5).

Three consequences follow, and Deck 08 treats them as one group because all three trace to ΔNit rather than to ΔNot. Transconductance falls, since gm is proportional to μCoxW/L and only the mobility is free to move. Output drive current falls with it, by (6.3). And propagation delay rises, because the time a logic stage takes to swing the next stage's input through a voltage ΔV is CΔV/I, so it is inversely proportional to the drive current. A measured delay chain therefore degrades in proportion to the interface-trap buildup, which makes timing — not threshold — the parameter that fails first in a hardened process where leakage has already been designed out.

Low-frequency noise rises after irradiation as well, but it is not a second symptom of the same defect. Electrons tunnel back and forth between the channel and traps lying a short distance inside the oxide — the border traps that M07 §9 separates from true interface states by charge pumping. Each such trap captures and re-emits on its own time constant, set by how far into the oxide it sits, and the superposition of many of them over a broad distribution of distances produces a noise power spectral density varying roughly as the inverse of frequency. Fleetwood's measurements establish that the post-irradiation excess noise tracks the oxide-trap charge rather than ΔNit, which makes the noise measurement complementary to the mobility measurement rather than redundant with it: one counts states at the interface, the other counts states just behind it, and a process change can move the two independently.

5 · Leakage through the isolation oxides

A chip is not a transistor. Between neighbouring transistors there must be a region where no channel can form, and that region is defined by a thick oxide grown or deposited into the silicon surface. Two structures are used. Local oxidation of silicon, or LOCOS, grows the thick oxide through a mask, which leaves a tapered transition where the thick oxide thins gradually into the gate oxide — a shape called a bird's beak. Shallow trench isolation, or STI, etches a trench and fills it, which gives a sharp boundary between thick and thin oxide but fills it with a deposited oxide of lower quality than a thermally grown one, rich in the oxygen-vacancy precursors that M07 §6 identified as hole traps.

Both structures create a transistor that no one designed. The polysilicon that forms the gate of the intended device runs over the edge of the isolation oxide and continues across it; the silicon beneath is the same p-type substrate the intended channel sits in; and the source and drain diffusions terminate against that isolation. The result is a parasitic field-effect transistor whose gate dielectric is the isolation oxide, connected in parallel with the device the designer drew and sharing its gate, source and drain.

The parasitic device is far more sensitive than the intended one, and the reason is geometric rather than chemical. The sensitivity compounds twice, for the same two reasons as the tox² law of M07 §3. A given areal density of trapped charge produces a voltage equal to the sheet charge divided by the oxide capacitance per unit area, and that capacitance falls as the thickness rises, so voltage scales with thickness at fixed areal charge. But the areal charge does not stay fixed: a thicker oxide presents a longer column of material in which to generate pairs, so the trapped areal density rises with thickness as well. The isolation oxide is one to two orders of magnitude thicker than the gate oxide, and the two effects multiply, so the same dose develops something closer to two to four orders of magnitude more voltage across it.

σs = qNox,    E = σsεox,    V = E · tFOX
(8.2)
σs areal sheet charge (C/cm²) · Nox trapped charge areal density (cm⁻²) · E field in the oxide (V/cm) · εox = 3.45×10⁻¹³ F/cm · tFOX field-oxide thickness in cm (0.50 µm = 0.50×10⁻⁴ cm) · V voltage dropped across it (V). The silicon beneath inverts when V exceeds the parasitic device's own threshold, 2φF + |Qdep|/CFOX, and the second term dominates because CFOX is small: at NA = 1015 cm−3 under 500 nm of oxide, 2φF is 0.60 V but |Qdep|/CFOX is 2.0 V, and at a channel-stop doping of 1017 cm−3 the second term reaches 24 V. On the formula sheet as (8.2).

The consequence is quantitative and severe. Deck 08 records that commercial CMOS integrated circuits may fail at total doses as low as 5 krad(SiO₂) through this mechanism, and that shallow trench isolation can be inverted between 50 and 100 krad(SiO₂). Measurements on a minimum-geometry, standard-edge 0.25 µm n-channel transistor show the off-state drain current rising by roughly six orders of magnitude over the dose range while the device's own threshold barely moves — the deformation of Figure 2's log panel, driven entirely by a parallel path the designer never drew.

The same measurement shows how strongly the result depends on what happens between irradiation and measurement. Deck 08 reproduces two runs on the same transistor type, one with an average of 30 minutes out of the irradiator between dose steps and one with 15 minutes. The slower sequence shows significant leakage only above 500 krad; the faster one shows it from 100 to 250 krad. Nothing about the physics changed — the extra time allowed more of the trapped hole charge to anneal before each measurement, by the mechanism of M07 §7. A total-dose number quoted without the measurement timing is incomplete.

A labelling discrepancy on Deck 08 p. 28. The slide reports a threshold of 42 V before irradiation falling to 0.46 V at 100 krad, and captions the measurement as a 0.25 µm n-channel transistor. A 0.25 µm gate-oxide transistor has a threshold below one volt, as p. 20 of the same deck shows directly, and the plot on p. 28 sweeps the gate to 70 V. The 42 V figure therefore belongs to the parasitic field-oxide transistor that shares the gate, not to the intended device, and what the slide shows is that the isolation inverts at essentially zero gate bias after 100 krad. The physics is right and the caption is loose; read the 42 V as a property of the thick oxide.

Three distinct leakage paths follow from the same mechanism, and Deck 08 enumerates them on a two-input NAND gate. The first runs beneath the field oxide between an n-channel source and the n-well, a path that carries current only because the well and the source sit at different potentials. The second runs from drain to source at the ends of the transistor, where the thin gate oxide transitions into the thick field oxide — the edge path of Figure 5. The third runs between two different gates, from the drain output of one NAND to the source of the adjacent one, and it is a path between devices rather than within one.

The relative severity of the LOCOS and STI geometries follows from their shapes. In LOCOS the transition region has continuously varying oxide thickness and high mechanical stress, and the polysilicon overlaps that edge, so a significant electric field appears exactly where the oxide is of intermediate thickness. That is the worst place for it to appear. Charge yield rises with field, while the fraction of surviving holes that is trapped near the interface falls as roughly Eox−1/2, so by M07 §4 the net trapped charge is largest at intermediate fields rather than at high ones — and the graded region sits there. In STI the transition is abrupt, which removes the graded region, but the trench fill is a deposited oxide of poorer quality and higher hole-trap density than thermal SiO₂. Neither geometry removes the parasitic device; they move the difficulty between the field term and the trap-density term of the same calculation.

6 · Hardening the leakage path by layout

Because the parasitic transistor is created by the geometry, it can often be removed by the geometry, and that is the attraction of the family of techniques called radiation hardening by design: much of it can be applied on an unmodified commercial line. Not all of it, as the individual cases below show — a dedicated channel-stop implant and a nested source/drain both need mask or process changes, while an enclosed layout and a lengthened parasitic channel are pure layout. Deck 08 treats the two leakage paths of §5 separately, and so does this section, because a remedy for one does nothing for the other.

The inter-device path — source to neighbouring well, beneath the field oxide — is attacked in the silicon rather than in the oxide. A heavily doped implant of the same type as the substrate is placed under the field oxide, forming a channel stop; the same structure drawn as a closed ring around a device is a guard ring. Raising the surface doping raises the threshold of the parasitic field-oxide transistor, and it does so almost entirely through the depletion-charge term |Qdep|/CFOX of (8.3): Qdep grows as the square root of the doping while the field-oxide capacitance stays very small, so the threshold reaches tens of volts. The Fermi potential 2φF rises too, but only logarithmically — roughly 0.12 V per decade of doping — and is a minor correction. Trapped positive charge that would have inverted a lightly doped surface then cannot come close. The same structure has a second benefit belonging to a later part of the course: guard rings reduce the gain of the parasitic bipolar transistors inherent in a CMOS well structure and control the potential along the latch-up path, which lowers single-event latch-up susceptibility as well.

The intra-device edge path is attacked by redrawing the transistor outline, and Deck 08 gives four alternatives against the standard two-edged baseline. Nesting the source and drain diffusions inside the thin-oxide region removes their contact with the field oxide, but requires a separate diffusion mask that many processes do not offer. Wrapping the gate around the source produces a ringed-source device, at the cost of substantial added gate capacitance. Lengthening the parasitic path, by drawing a longer effective channel for it, improves hardness only by a factor of two to three. The enclosed layout transistor draws the gate as a closed ring with one diffusion inside it and the other outside, so the gate never crosses from thin oxide to thick oxide and the path does not exist at all. Only the last of these removes the path rather than weakening it; the deck separates the others by cost and by what a given process will support, not by a monotonic ranking.

The enclosed layout transistor costs area and constrains design. Deck 08's estimate of its effective width is the perimeter of a rectangle drawn through the middle of the gate ring — a first-order rule, and the one to use for the course's calculations. It is known to overstate the result, because it counts the four corners of the ring at full weight when their source-to-drain distance is not the drawn length; the corner-weighted models fitted to measured drain current roughly halve that contribution. Either way the ring cannot be drawn arbitrarily small, so there is a minimum width-to-length ratio and a minimum drive strength below which the layout cannot go, the aspect ratio follows from the geometry rather than being chosen freely, and the two diffusions are not interchangeable, since the inner one has the smaller perimeter. The whole ring conducts, which gives good drive per device — but not per unit area at small sizes, where the minimum achievable aspect ratio is several times that of a two-edged transistor and the gap widens as the technology scales. The penalty falls hardest exactly where minimum-size logic dominates.

Deck 08 quantifies the broader cost of hardening with two figures from different sources, and they do not agree. The deck states that guaranteed radiation-hardened processes run at least three to four generations behind their commercial counterparts, because commercial foundries have little reason to modify a production line for a small number of hardened designs. The microprocessor-performance plot it reproduces from Lacoe et al. gives a smaller gap — about seven years, or two or more technology generations — and that plot is a snapshot of the state of the art around 2000 rather than a standing property of the industry; the deck carries an updated version of it for 2022. Treat them as two independent estimates of a gap that is large and that moves. Hardening by design rather than by process exists largely to close it by using a commercial line unmodified.

7 · Silicon on insulator and the buried oxide

Silicon on insulator replaces the bulk substrate with a thin silicon film sitting on a buried oxide layer, itself on a silicon handle wafer. The transistor is built in the film, and the buried oxide isolates it from everything below. The structure was adopted partly for radiation hardness, and for single-event effects it delivers: the volume of silicon from which a passing ion's charge can be collected is limited to the film thickness rather than extending micrometres into a substrate, which M11 develops. For total dose it introduces a new oxide, and a new parasitic transistor to go with it.

The buried oxide is thick — Deck 08's three-dimensional device data quote 150 nm, and older technologies used considerably more — and it traps holes by the same mechanism as any other oxide. Those holes invert the bottom surface of the silicon film, creating a conducting layer along the underside that connects source to drain. This is back-channel leakage. A second path runs along the edges of the silicon island, where the film is bounded laterally by oxide, and that path is gate-controlled because the gate wraps over the island edge.

In a partially depleted film the two paths are distinguishable in a single measurement, and the way they differ is the diagnostic. A partially depleted film keeps a neutral, undepleted region of silicon between the front depletion layer and the back interface, and that neutral body screens the back interface from the top gate. Back-channel leakage is therefore nearly independent of top-gate voltage, while edge leakage increases with it because the gate does control the island edge, so a characteristic containing both shows a step: a gate-independent floor set by the back channel, with a gate-dependent rise superimposed on it.

A fully depleted film has no neutral body to screen with, and the conclusion inverts. Front and back gates are electrostatically coupled, so charge trapped in the buried oxide does not merely add a leakage floor — it moves the front threshold voltage directly, and raises leakage at the same time. Fully depleted devices are accordingly more sensitive to buried-oxide charge than partially depleted ones unless the buried oxide is itself hardened, which is why rad-hard SOI was traditionally built partially depleted. That coupling is not a curiosity: it is the mechanism behind both the FinFET and the three-dimensional results of §8.

The structural fix follows the diagnosis. A body-under-source field-effect transistor, or BUSFET, extends the body region so that the source does not reach down to the buried oxide: the source diffusion is bottomed by body silicon rather than by the oxide, which interrupts the back-channel path at its source end. The path can no longer connect source to drain, whatever charge the buried oxide holds. The remaining route is to modify the buried oxide itself so that it traps less, which is a process solution rather than a layout one and belongs to M09.

8 · Scaling, and where the vulnerability moved

Every quantity in this module depends on oxide thickness, and thickness has fallen by orders of magnitude over the period the field has existed. The result is that the dominant failure mechanism has changed identity, and a statement about total-dose hardness that holds for a 1 µm technology can fail for a 28 nm one.

For the gate oxide the scaling is strongly favourable, and it acts twice over. M07 §3 established that the threshold shift from trapped holes varies as the square of the oxide thickness: one power because a thinner oxide presents a smaller volume in which to generate pairs, and a second because a thinner oxide has a larger capacitance and so converts a given charge into a smaller voltage. Halving the thickness therefore quarters the shift. The exponent is a first-order prediction rather than a law — other thickness dependences have been reported for particular oxides, and the canonical confirming data were taken at 80 K precisely so that the tunnel annealing described next was frozen out and could not corrupt the measurement. It also belongs to the threshold shift specifically: the hole transit time of M07 §5 carries a much stronger dependence, roughly tox⁴. Below about 5 nm a third effect takes over and removes the problem almost entirely. Electrons tunnel into the oxide from both bounding interfaces — from the silicon and from the gate electrode — neutralizing trapped holes behind a front that advances from each side at roughly 0.2 nm per decade of elapsed time. In a thin enough oxide the two fronts meet near the middle, so no net positive charge survives anywhere in the layer rather than merely less of it surviving. That is why radiation-induced threshold shift in a modern gate oxide is not small but essentially absent.

Isolation has scaled far more slowly. What sets a trench's depth is the need to exceed the source/drain junction depth, to hold lateral punch-through and well-to-well isolation margin, and to survive the fill and planarization steps — none of which relaxes when the gate stack thins. Isolation has come down from roughly a micrometre of LOCOS field oxide to a few hundred nanometres of shallow trench, a factor of a few, against a hundred-fold reduction in gate oxide thickness. The two trends therefore cross. In older technologies the gate oxide dominated the total-dose response and threshold shift was the failure mode; in modern technologies the gate oxide contributes little and the surviving failure is leakage through shallow trench isolation and, in silicon on insulator, through the buried oxide — which Deck 08 puts at 5 krad(SiO₂) for field-oxide and buried-oxide leakage, and at 50 to 100 krad(SiO₂) for inversion of the trench itself.

Three-dimensional device architectures change the picture again, because they change how much of the channel the gate can reach. In a fin field-effect transistor the channel is a narrow upright ridge of silicon and the gate wraps its top and both sidewalls, so the fin is controlled from three sides at once. Deck 08 reports measurements on 20-fin devices of 40, 65 and 80 nm fin width, with 2 nm gate oxide, 150 nm buried oxide and 100 nm gate length: both the threshold shift and the subthreshold-swing shift are smaller for the narrower fins. The charge responsible sits in the buried oxide beneath the fin, where it acts as a back gate on the fin body — the same coupling §7 described for a fully depleted film. In a wide fin the material farthest from the gated sidewalls is poorly controlled, and trapped charge below it moves its potential; in a narrow fin the two sidewall gates hold the potential across the whole width and the back gate cannot compete. Reducing the fin width is thus a hardening measure that arrives as a by-product of scaling.

Stacking devices vertically introduces coupling that has no two-dimensional analogue. In three-dimensional sequentially integrated fully depleted silicon on insulator, a second device layer is built above the first with an intermediate dielectric between them. Deck 08 reports X-ray irradiations to 2 Mrad(SiO₂) at about 525 rad(SiO₂)/s on such structures, in which the threshold of a top-layer device depends on the bias applied to the gate of the device beneath it. The mechanism is the field dependence of M07 §2 and §4 acting at a distance: a positive bias on the bottom gate raises the field in the intermediate dielectric and directs it toward the upper device, which both increases the charge yield there and sweeps the surviving holes toward the upper device's back channel. Charge generated in a layer belonging to neither device degrades one of them, and which one depends on how the other is biased.

At doses far above normal qualification levels, two further mechanisms appear that the preceding sections do not predict, and they have different geometry dependences. The radiation-induced narrow channel effect comes from charge trapped in the shallow trench isolation beside the channel, and it shifts the threshold of both polarities in a width-dependent way — but to opposite effect. In an n-channel device the trapped positive charge lowers the threshold at the channel edges and raises off-state leakage. In a p-channel device the same positive charge accumulates the well at those edges, so the threshold magnitude rises and on-current and transconductance fall, generally more severely than in the n-channel case. The radiation-induced short channel effect is separate and comes from charge trapped in the spacer oxides over the lightly doped drain extensions; it compensates the response in n-channel devices while adding to it in p-channel ones, which at high enough dose can drive a p-channel transistor to complete current collapse. Both are consequences of oxides that the designer does not think of as active devices behaving as gates once they hold charge.

9 · Bipolar and imaging devices under total dose

The MOS transistor fails through a voltage. The bipolar transistor fails through a current and the charge-coupled device through a transfer efficiency, and in both the damage is done by charge in an oxide the designer does not use as a gate. Which charge dominates is not the same answer in each case, and not even settled in the first: in a bipolar transistor interface traps raise the surface recombination velocity while trapped oxide charge acts electrostatically, and which wins depends on the base doping and the structure. In a charge-coupled device the two total-dose routes divide cleanly — trapped oxide charge shifts flat-band voltage, interface traps raise dark current — while the transfer efficiency that limits the device is degraded by displacement damage in the silicon rather than by either.

M06 §7 established that a bipolar transistor's current gain is the ratio of collector to base current, that the base current has three components, and that total ionizing dose acts principally on the component recombining inside the emitter–base depletion region. The mechanism is now specifiable, and it is addition rather than diversion. Interface traps formed where the emitter–base junction meets the passivating oxide act as recombination centres and raise the surface recombination velocity, opening a second recombination path in parallel with the transistor's normal operation. That path draws its own carriers from the emitter and the base terminal must balance them, so the base current rises without any carrier being taken from the flux that crosses to the collector. At a given base–emitter voltage the collector current is set by diffusion across the quasi-neutral base and is essentially unchanged, so the gain falls purely because its denominator grows: if the base current doubles, the gain halves.

Surface recombination is sharply peaked at the point where the electron and hole concentrations are equal, and at that point alone the ideality factor is exactly two — which is the exponent in Deck 04's surface component, JB2 proportional to vsurf exp(qVBE/(2kT)). A measured radiation-induced base current is collected over a region in which the two concentrations are not equal, and alongside components of ideality one from the neutral base and the emitter, so its effective ideality lands between one and two.

Trapped oxide charge acts through a second and separate route, electrostatic rather than recombinative, and its sign depends on the doping of the surface it acts on. Positive charge over the p-type base of an n-p-n transistor depletes that surface, which moves the region where electron and hole concentrations are equal and enlarges the area over which fast recombination occurs, worsening the degradation. The same positive charge over the n-type base of a p-n-p transistor accumulates the surface, which suppresses recombination there. Deck 04 concludes from this that vertical p-n-p transistors are often harder than vertical n-p-n transistors, and the hedge is worth keeping: in a p-n-p the same charge also extends the emitter–base depletion region into the lightly doped p-type emitter, where the depleted surface recombines faster, and an accumulated base surface supplies more electrons for back-injection. Both raise the base current, and interface traps raise the surface recombination velocity whatever the polarity. The observed hardness is the balance of these terms rather than accumulation acting alone.

Geometry matters more than polarity, and the published ordering of total-dose sensitivity runs the other way from the polarity argument. The lateral p-n-p is the most sensitive structure of all, because its carriers travel laterally directly beneath the oxide, through the region where the radiation-induced recombination centres sit; the substrate p-n-p is next; the vertical p-n-p is usually quite hard; and the vertical n-p-n is the least sensitive. Since lateral and substrate p-n-p transistors are what linear bipolar integrated circuits use at their inputs, most p-n-p structures a designer actually meets are softer than an n-p-n, not harder — which is why input bias current is the parameter that fails first in an irradiated operational amplifier.

That inversion is enhanced low-dose-rate sensitivity, and it is the most consequential difference between bipolar and MOS total-dose behaviour. Deck 04 records that it was unexpected on two counts: degradation was generally expected to be less at low dose rate because annealing has time to act during the exposure, and bipolar transistors had been believed relatively radiation hard. Neither held. The oxides at risk share three properties — they are thick, often hundreds of nanometres and sometimes composite; they are of low quality, being implant masks formed at high temperature rather than carefully grown gate dielectrics; and they sit at low electric field, since no electrode covers them and only fringing fields penetrate. The practical gap is large: laboratory irradiations commonly run above 50 rad(SiO₂)/s to keep test time manageable, while space dose rates are typically below 0.01 rad(SiO₂)/s — a factor of at least five thousand, or more than three orders of magnitude. Bipolar integrated circuits flown on the Microelectronics and Photonics Test Bed degraded at rates that tracked the periods of low dose rate and matched laboratory low-dose-rate data rather than laboratory high-dose-rate data.

Distinguishing a true dose-rate effect from a time-dependent one requires a specific control, and Deck 04 states it: irradiate at high dose rate, then anneal the samples for the same total elapsed time the low-dose-rate exposure would have taken. If the degradation then matches the low-dose-rate result, the effect is time-dependent — the damage simply needed time to develop, as interface-trap buildup does. If the degradation remains smaller, the effect is a true dose-rate effect, in which the instantaneous rate of energy deposition itself changes the outcome. MOS transistors usually show no true dose-rate effect; bipolar oxides do.

The mechanism behind it is not settled, and several families of model remain live. In the space-charge model the charge present in the oxide at any instant sets up a field that retards the transport of holes and protons toward the interface, with a critical dose rate above which the perturbation becomes significant; thermally stimulated current measurements complicate it, since they find a similar number of trapped holes at high and low rate, with the difference lying in how many are compensated as stable dipoles. In initial-recombination models the higher instantaneous density of electron–hole pairs simply increases recombination at the moment of generation, with no space-charge field required. Binary-reaction-rate models turn on two species with different transport times reacting at the interface, and hydrogen models on the release and cracking of hydrogen-bearing species — a route with direct experimental support, since the same part in a flat-pack and in a metal can differ by up to two orders of magnitude at the same low dose rate, through nothing but the hydrogen the package supplies. All of these predict qualitatively similar curves, which is exactly why choosing among them has proved difficult.

The charge-coupled device fails along both cumulative routes at once, as M06 §9 introduced. Total ionizing dose acts through the gate oxides of the transfer electrodes: flat-band voltages shift, which alters the clock voltages required to form and move the potential wells, and interface traps raise the dark current, since a trap at the silicon surface with an energy near midgap is an efficient thermal generation centre and every carrier it generates joins the signal packet as noise. Displacement damage acts through the silicon and degrades charge transfer efficiency by creating bulk traps in the buried channel that capture and delay signal charge. The two routes are separable in the image itself, and the discriminator is the shape of the dark-current distribution. Total dose creates interface traps under every gate in the array at much the same rate, so the whole distribution shifts and broadens — a raised pedestal. Displacement damage deposits a nuclear interaction in a small number of pixels, so it produces a high-side tail of hot pixels, often telegraphing between levels so that they cannot be calibrated out, and it degrades transfer efficiency, which appears as trailing streaks behind bright features because charge left behind by one packet is released into the ones that follow. M12 develops the displacement side.

Where this leaves us

Two trapped-charge densities have now been followed all the way to a failed part. They move exactly one term of the threshold voltage, which makes the device response calculable from the oxide physics of M07 and nothing else. They deform the transfer characteristic in two independent ways that separate cleanly on the two plotting conventions — translation from the net charge, stretchout and mobility loss from interface traps. They oppose each other in an n-channel device and reinforce each other in a p-channel one, and because they evolve in opposite directions after the exposure ends, the worst case for an n-channel part can arrive long after the irradiation.

The dominant failure, however, is none of these. It is the inversion of silicon beneath an oxide that was never meant to be a gate, in a transistor that was never drawn, and it is dominant because that oxide is thick. Layout removes the parasitic device; process changes what the oxide traps in the first place. M09 takes the second route: how oxide thickness, growth chemistry, annealing and applied bias during irradiation determine how much charge an oxide holds, and what a process engineer can do about it before any transistor is drawn.

Quick reference

  • Radiation moves one term: Vth = φMS − (Qot+Qit)/Cox + 2φF + |Qdep|/Cox. Work function, Fermi potential and depletion charge are set by materials and doping and do not change. ΔVth = −(ΔQot+ΔQit)/Cox.
  • Linear axis: sideways = threshold shift, slope = mobility. Log axis: sideways = threshold shift, slope = subthreshold swing = interface-trap density (stretchout). The failure metric is ID at VG = 0.
  • Leakage is exponential in the shift: ratio = exp(ΔV/(nVT)) = 10ΔV/S. A 0.15 V shift at n = 1.5 gives ×47. Narrow ΔVth distributions produce wide leakage distributions.
  • Signs: ΔVot < 0 always; ΔVit > 0 for n-channel, < 0 for p-channel. pMOS shifts add and the device turns off harder; nMOS shifts oppose and can rebound positive as ΔVot anneals while ΔVit grows. Rebound test: +50% dose, then 168 h biased anneal at 100 °C.
  • Interface traps drive mobility, drive current and timing through μeff = μ0/(1 + αΔNit); delay is CΔV/I, so it rises as drive falls. 1/f noise is the exception — it tracks oxide-trap and border-trap charge, not ΔNit, so noise and mobility are complementary diagnostics rather than the same one twice.
  • Isolation oxide is the real failure: 200–1000 nm thick. V = qNoxtFOX/εox, and because the areal charge also grows with thickness the penalty goes as t2. It inverts when V exceeds 2φF + |Qdep|/CFOX — the depletion term dominates: 0.60 + 2.04 V at NA = 1015, 0.83 + 24.1 V at a channel-stop 1017, under 500 nm. Commercial ICs can fail at 5 krad(SiO₂); STI inverts at 50–100 krad(SiO₂).
  • Three leakage paths: source to n-well under the field oxide; drain to source at the transistor edge; drain of one gate to source of the next. LOCOS risk is the graded bird's-beak at high field; STI risk is poor-quality deposited oxide rich in hole traps.
  • Anneal time between dose steps changes the answer: the same 0.25 µm transistor leaks above 500 krad at 30 min out of the irradiator and from 100–250 krad at 15 min.
  • Layout hardening, by path. Inter-device (source to well, under the field oxide): p⁺ channel stop / guard ring, which works through |Qdep|/CFOX and also reduces latch-up. Intra-device edge: nested source/drain, ringed source, longer parasitic L (only ×2–3), and the enclosed layout transistor — only the last removes the path rather than weakening it. Edgeless 0.25 µm nMOS: no leakage increase to 2 Mrad(SiO₂). ELT effective W = perimeter through the middle of the gate ring (the course's rule; it overstates the corners).
  • SOI depends on which kind. Partially depleted: the neutral body screens the back interface, so back-channel leakage is nearly gate-independent and adds a floor under the gate-controlled edge leakage — a stepped characteristic. Fully depleted: no neutral body, front and back gates couple, so buried-oxide charge moves the front threshold directly and FD is the more sensitive of the two. BUSFET bottoms the source in body silicon to break the back-channel path.
  • Scaling: gate-oxide shift falls as tox² (a first-order rule; the confirming data are at 80 K) and vanishes below ~5 nm because tunnelling fronts advance from both interfaces at ~0.2 nm per decade and meet. Isolation scales far more slowly — trench depth is set by junction depth and process, not by the gate stack — so the failure mode changed identity. Narrower FinFET fins shift less, because the two sidewall gates outcompete the buried oxide acting as a back gate. 3D stacked FDSOI couples layer to layer.
  • Bipolar is addition, not diversion: interface traps open a recombination path in parallel, drawing its own carriers, so IB rises while IC is untouched and β falls as 1/IB. Ideality is exactly 2 only at the n = p crossover; measured over a region it lands between 1 and 2. Sensitivity ordering: lateral pnp > substrate pnp > vertical pnp > vertical npn — geometry beats polarity, and linear ICs use the soft ones at their inputs.
  • ELDRS: more degradation at lower dose rate. Oxides at risk are thick, low quality, low field. Lab >50 rad(SiO₂)/s against space <0.01 rad(SiO₂)/s — a factor of ≥5,000. Discriminator: anneal the high-rate samples for the same elapsed time — equal damage means a time-dependent effect, less damage means a true dose-rate effect. MOSFETs usually show no true dose-rate effect. The mechanism is unsettled: space-charge (retarded transport), initial recombination, binary reaction rate, and hydrogen models all fit the data, and package hydrogen alone moves the result by up to 100×.
