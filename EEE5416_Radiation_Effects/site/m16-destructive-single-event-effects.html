Module 16

Destructive Single-Event Effects

How one ion destroys a device: the avalanche and thermal feedback that turn a few picocoulombs into a melted filament, latchup in CMOS, burnout and gate rupture in silicon power MOSFETs, the new failure modes of silicon carbide and gallium nitride, and how test data with few or no events become a safe operating voltage for a mission.

Prerequisites M11 Read time ~55 min Sources Deck 14 parts 1–2 (52 slides); lectures of 19 and 21 Oct; Shenai, Galloway and Schrimpf (2004); IEEE NSREC 2024 Short Course

M11 followed a single ion from the charge it deposits to the bit it flips, and its §10 introduced the events that do more than flip a bit. Those events are this module's subject. They share one structure: the ion supplies a trigger of a few picocoulombs, and the circuit's own power supply supplies the energy that does the damage, through a feedback loop that the trigger switches on. Whether the loop closes depends on the device structure, on the voltage across it and on the charge the ion delivers; whether the device survives depends on how quickly the current is removed. Power devices hold the highest voltages across their high-field regions when they are off, so the destructive events concentrate there, and the wide-bandgap materials that make power devices smaller make the problem sharper.

The module runs from the shared physics to the engineering decision. §1 separates recoverable from permanent effects and locates the energy that destroys a part. §2 gives the two feedback mechanisms, avalanche multiplication and thermal runaway. §3 treats latchup in CMOS and §4 burnout and gate rupture in silicon power MOSFETs. §5 turns to silicon carbide and gallium nitride. §6 covers how these events are measured and what few or zero events can establish, §7 how test data become a safe operating area and a flight decision, and §8 how a destructive event propagates through a system and why a mission is qualified against total dose, single events and displacement damage together.

1 · From upset to destruction

Deck 14 opens by placing the three radiation effects on one continuum (pp. 3–4). Total ionizing dose degrades a device gradually through trapped oxide charge and interface traps. A soft single-event effect is a transient or recoverable fault. A hard single-event effect is a permanent failure. This section defines each effect in the second group, sorts them by whether the part survives, and shows where the destructive energy comes from.

The family of single-event effects

Deck 14 p. 7 lists nine effects in order of increasing severity. The first four are soft. A single-event transient (SET) is a voltage pulse on a node; a single-event upset (SEU) is a changed stored bit; a multiple-cell upset (MCU) is several bits changed by one particle; a single-event functional interrupt (SEFI) is a loss of a circuit function, such as a control state machine that stops responding, that a reset restores. The remaining five are the hard errors of this module. Single-event latchup (SEL) is a sustained high-current state through a parasitic four-layer structure. Single-event leakage current (SELC) is a permanent step increase in a device's leakage current. Single-event burnout (SEB) is the thermal destruction of a power device by a regenerative current. Single-event gate rupture (SEGR) and single-event dielectric rupture (SEDR) are the breakdown of a gate dielectric and of another dielectric layer, such as the field-plate dielectric of a gallium nitride transistor (§5), along the ion track.

"Hard" and "destructive" are not the same property, and the sources sort the borderline cases differently. Latchup is destructive if the current persists, yet removing power clears it and the part usually returns to service (§3), so it is a recoverable effect that can become permanent. Leakage-current events are permanent, yet the part keeps working with a higher leakage. Deck 14 counts SELC among the hard errors (pp. 7, 52); the IEEE NSREC 2024 Short Course lists it among the non-destructive effects. The two lists sort different properties: the damage is permanent, but the device still functions. The useful classification uses two questions: does the damage persist after power is cycled, and does the part still meet its function?

The supply, not the ion, destroys the part

An ion deposits a fixed, small energy: its linear energy transfer times the density times the path length (M04 §2). A destructive event dissipates far more. The difference comes from the circuit: once the ion's charge switches on a low-impedance path, the supply drives current through it for as long as the path persists. Deck 14 p. 52 writes the sequence as ionization, charge collection, avalanche multiplication, thermal runaway and structural failure. The first two steps are the ion's; the last three are powered by the supply. The comparison below puts numbers on that division.

Energy from the ion against energy from the supply

Given A heavy ion with LET 40 MeV·cm²/mg crosses 10 µm of silicon (density 2.329 g/cm³) and triggers a latchup that draws 0.2 A from a 3.3 V supply for 1 ms before a protection circuit removes power. The numbers are illustrative. Find the energy the ion deposits, the charge it generates, the energy the supply dissipates, and their ratio.

Plan. The deposited energy is LET × density × path length; the density must be in mg/cm³ so that the mass units cancel. The supply's energy is power × time. The ratio says which one can melt anything.

Energy deposited by the ion. Edep = LET · ρ · L = 40 MeV·cm²/mg × 2329 mg/cm³ × 1.0×10⁻³ cm = 93.2 MeV. In joules: 93.2×10⁶ eV × 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ J/eV = 1.49×10⁻¹¹ J, about 15 pJ. Why 2329 mg/cm³: the LET is per milligram per square centimetre, so the density must be per milligram for cm² · mg⁻¹ × mg · cm⁻³ × cm to leave MeV.

Charge generated. Silicon takes 3.6 eV per electron–hole pair (M04 §4): 93.2×10⁶ eV ÷ 3.6 eV = 2.59×10⁷ pairs, and 2.59×10⁷ × 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ C = 4.1×10⁻¹² C, or 4.1 pC. This agrees with the rule that an LET of 97 MeV·cm²/mg generates 1 pC/µm in silicon: 40/97 × 10 µm × 1 pC/µm = 4.1 pC.

Energy from the supply. Esup = V I t = 3.3 V × 0.2 A × 1.0×10⁻³ s = 6.6×10⁻⁴ J.

Ratio. 6.6×10⁻⁴ J ÷ 1.49×10⁻¹¹ J = 4.4×10⁷.

Edep ≈ 15 pJ and 4.1 pC; Esup ≈ 0.66 mJ, some forty million times larger. Check: a track 1 µm wide and 10 µm long holds 1×10⁻¹¹ cm³ × 2.33 g/cm³ = 2.3×10⁻¹¹ g of silicon; with a specific heat of 0.71 J/(g·K) the 15 pJ raises it by about 1 K, more than a thousand times too little to melt it. Every destructive effect therefore needs a path through which the supply can deliver current.

The same division explains which variables control the outcome. The ion's LET sets whether the trigger is large enough to switch the path on. The applied voltage sets both whether the path can sustain itself and how much power flows once it does. The time before the current is removed sets whether the dissipated energy reaches the level that melts metal or silicon. The 19 Oct lecture asked which factors decide between no error, a soft error and a hard error, and this is the answer: the charge delivered, how fast the circuit removes it, and how much power the supply can push through the struck structure.

A latchup in a 1.8 V logic chip draws 0.5 A. Detection and power removal take 5 ms in one design and 50 µs in another. By what factor does the energy dissipated differ, and why does the faster design matter even if both parts survive?

The dissipated energy is VIt. Slow design: 1.8 V × 0.5 A × 5×10⁻³ s = 4.5×10⁻³ J. Fast design: 1.8 V × 0.5 A × 50×10⁻⁶ s = 4.5×10⁻⁵ J. The ratio is 5×10⁻³ / 5×10⁻⁵ = 100. The ion's energy is the same in both cases and is irrelevant to the comparison. Survival is not the only criterion: a latchup that is cleared can still leave latent damage, such as melted and voided metal lines, if the current lasts too long (§3), so a hundredfold reduction in the dissipated energy also reduces the latent damage a cleared event can leave.

2 · Avalanche multiplication and thermal runaway

Two feedback mechanisms turn the ion's trigger into the supply-powered event of §1. Avalanche multiplication multiplies the collected charge inside a high-field region; thermal runaway couples current to temperature until the material fails. Deck 14 p. 21 names both, and the 19 and 21 Oct lectures returned to them for every effect in the module. This section states each mechanism and the condition under which it becomes unstable.

Assumes: impact ionization and avalanche breakdown of a reverse-biased junction

In a reverse-biased junction the depletion region holds a field that accelerates carriers. Between collisions with the lattice an electron or hole gains kinetic energy from the field. When that energy exceeds a threshold of the order of the bandgap, a collision can lift an electron from the valence band to the conduction band and create a new electron–hole pair: impact ionization. The rate is described by ionization coefficients αn and αp, the number of pairs a carrier creates per unit distance, which rise steeply with field. Each new carrier is accelerated and can ionize in turn. The junction reaches avalanche breakdown when the multiplication diverges, which for equal coefficients is the condition that the ionization integral across the depletion width, ∫α dx, reaches 1. Electrons ionize more readily than holes in silicon (Sze and Ng, Physics of Semiconductor Devices).

Avalanche multiplication

Deck 14 p. 21 describes the avalanche as the slide states it: a carrier with enough energy collides and creates pairs, which collide again and create more, so the current grows exponentially. Below breakdown the multiplication is finite. A current entering the high-field region, such as the charge from an ion track, leaves it multiplied by a factor M that depends on how close the junction voltage is to its breakdown voltage. The empirical form in general use is due to Miller.

Thermal runaway

Current through a region with a voltage across it dissipates power density J · E, current density times field, as heat. Deck 14 p. 21 states that impact ionization together with this Joule dissipation may lead to thermal breakdown. The instability arises because heating increases the current. In a reverse-biased silicon junction the generation current is proportional to the intrinsic carrier concentration ni, which rises exponentially with temperature (M06); a hotter region therefore conducts more, dissipates more and heats further. When the heat generated rises faster with temperature than the heat conducted away, the temperature has no stable value. The current then concentrates into the hottest filament, because that filament conducts best, and the local temperature climbs until silicon or metal melts and resolidifies as a permanent short. Johnston describes burnout in the same terms: the current in small inhomogeneous regions becomes high enough to cause local melting (Reliability and Radiation Effects in Compound Semiconductors, 2010).

A second, slower damage mechanism acts in metal. A sustained high current density in an interconnect transfers momentum from the conduction electrons to the metal ions, the electron-wind force, and the ions drift with the electron flow. This is electromigration, a transport of atoms, not of electrons alone, as the 19 Oct lecture stressed in answer to a question. Where the atomic flux diverges, voids open and hillocks grow. Combined with Joule heating, a latchup current held for long enough melts metal lines and leaves voids, which the IEEE NSREC 2024 Short Course shows in a scanning electron micrograph of a line after latchup. A part that survives such an event may fail later from the weakened line (§3).

Using (16.1) with n = 5, by what factor does the multiplication rise when a junction is taken from 85% to 95% of its breakdown voltage? Explain why derating a power device below a fixed fraction of its rating is effective against this mechanism.

At 85%: 0.85⁵ = 0.4437, so M = 1/(1 − 0.4437) = 1/0.5563 = 1.80. At 95%: 0.95⁵ = 0.7738, so M = 1/0.2262 = 4.42. The factor is 4.42/1.80 = 2.46. The multiplication depends on how close the voltage is to breakdown, and the dependence is steepest in the last few percent; keeping the operating voltage at a fixed fraction well below breakdown keeps the device on the flat part of the curve, where the collected charge is barely multiplied and the avalanche loop cannot close. The fraction that is safe in practice is far lower than this static picture suggests, because the track's charge drives the parasitic bipolar transistor that closes the loop (§4); that is why the derating rules of §7 are set from measured burnout thresholds, not from (16.1).

3 · Single-event latchup in CMOS

M11 §10 gave the structure: bulk CMOS contains a p⁺–n-well–p-substrate–n⁺ path that acts as a vertical pnp and a lateral npn transistor, each feeding the other's base, and a strike that injects enough current into the well or substrate can switch it into a self-sustaining low-impedance state. Deck 14 p. 20 states the consequence: the thyristor holds a low-voltage, high-current state, the component stops functioning, and the current may lead to thermal wear-out. This section gives the electrical condition for the latched state, how a system clears it, and what clearing it does not undo.

The latched state and its holding point

Once latched, the four-layer path conducts as a forward-biased thyristor: the voltage across it falls to a low holding voltage, and it stays latched as long as the circuit can supply at least the holding current. Both are properties of the parasitic structure. The latched state is stable only if the supply can maintain a current above the holding current at a voltage above the holding voltage; if the supply voltage is below the holding voltage, or a series resistance limits the current below the holding current, the structure cannot remain latched and returns to its blocking state when the strike's current has passed. That gives two design levers against latchup that do not touch the silicon: the supply voltage and the current the supply can deliver.

Clearing a latchup, and what clearing leaves

Removing power collapses the latched state, which is why spacecraft electronics carry latchup protection: a current sensor on the supply line detects the over-current, a switch removes power, and power is restored after the structure has returned to blocking (M11 §10). The design question is the time between the strike and the cut, because the energy dissipated grows linearly with it (§1). The 19 Oct lecture used the same picture: a latchup that is cut before it heats the structure is recoverable by a reboot; one left on long enough overheats the junction and the metal and becomes permanent. The IEEE NSREC 2024 Short Course defines the boundary the same way: if power cycling does not restore normal operation, the loss of function is permanent. A recovered part is not necessarily an undamaged one. Melted and voided metal lines left by a latchup that was cleared can shorten the part's life, so a latchup that was detected and cleared in flight is a reliability event, not only an availability one.

What sets the sensitivity

The loop sustains itself when the product of the two parasitic transistors' current gains, as modified by the well and substrate shunt resistances, exceeds unity (M11 §10). Anything that raises the gains or the shunt resistances makes latchup easier. Temperature does both: bipolar current gain rises with temperature and so does the resistivity of the lightly doped well and substrate, so the latchup threshold LET falls as a part runs hotter: Johnston, Hughlock, Baze and Plaag measured threshold LETs about 2.5 times lower at 125 °C (IEEE Trans. Nucl. Sci. 38, 1435, 1991), which is why latchup is tested at the highest temperature the part will see. The hardening measures are the ones M11 §10 listed: an epitaxial layer on a heavily doped substrate to lower the substrate resistance, guard rings and channel stops, and silicon on insulator, which removes the four-layer path entirely. The scaled-node variant, micro-latchup in FinFET circuits, belongs with rad-hard design for single events (M14). Deck 14 p. 48 adds two power devices with their own regenerative paths: the insulated-gate bipolar transistor (IGBT), whose internal npnp structure can trigger latchup or burnout depending on bias, and the power bipolar transistor, a three-layer device that a strike in its base can drive into avalanche and burnout. The IEEE NSREC 2024 Short Course calls IGBTs highly prone to latchup and unsuitable for space for that reason.

A latchup-susceptible ASIC is to be powered either directly from a 3.3 V rail or through a series resistor sized so that the supply cannot deliver more than half of the parasitic structure's holding current. Which configuration can latch, and what does the resistor cost in normal operation?

The latched state needs a current at or above the holding current at a voltage at or above the holding voltage. Directly on the rail, the supply can deliver any current the latched branch draws, so if the rail exceeds the holding voltage a stable latched point exists and the part latches. Behind the resistor, the load line reaches the latched branch only below the holding current, so no stable latched point exists: a strike can switch the structure on momentarily, but it returns to blocking once the strike's current has passed. The cost is that the same resistance sits in series with the chip's normal supply current, producing a voltage drop IR that rises with the chip's activity and reduces the voltage its transistors see, so the method works only where the normal current is small compared with the holding current. Where it is not, the alternative is detection and fast power cycling (Figure 6).

4 · Burnout and gate rupture in silicon power MOSFETs

Deck 14 p. 12 asks why power devices are the ones that fail, and gives three reasons: they hold high voltages and currents, so a strike that disturbs the local field can trigger a destructive current surge; they contain parasitic bipolar structures; and the surge can produce a local hot spot that their heat removal cannot handle. This section develops those reasons for the vertical power MOSFET, which M11 §10 drew: the device, the two destructive mechanisms, how each depends on bias, LET and design, and the empirical expression for gate rupture.

Why the off state is the vulnerable one

A vertical double-diffused MOSFET carries current from its n⁺ sources through a channel in the p body and down through a lightly doped n⁻ epitaxial drift region to the n⁺ substrate, which is the drain. The device is built as many such cells in parallel to give a wide channel and a low on-resistance. Its lightly doped epitaxial layer, 10 to 120 µm thick in silicon parts, exists to hold the blocking voltage across a wide depletion region (IEEE NSREC 2024 Short Course). In the on state the drain voltage is low and the field is small. In the off state the gate is at 0 V, the p-body–n-epitaxial junction is reverse-biased, and nearly the full drain voltage stands across the depleted drift region. The 21 Oct lecture worked through this point with the class: in a power switch the source is grounded, the high voltage is on the drain, and the off state puts that voltage across a reverse-biased junction, which is where both destructive mechanisms need it. A power MOSFET is most vulnerable to both events when off, and sensitive to burnout only when off (IEEE NSREC 2024 Short Course).

Single-event burnout

Deck 14 p. 25 assigns the parasitic transistor's terminals: the n⁺ source is the emitter, the p body the base, and the n⁻ epitaxial layer the collector. It is held off in normal operation because the source metallization shorts the body to the source. The slide gives the mechanism in order, starting from the static avalanche characteristic, and Shenai, Galloway and Schrimpf give the same sequence for the strike:

  1. The ion's track crosses the source, body and drift region. Its holes flow through the body toward the source contact; its electrons are collected at the drain.
  2. The hole current flows laterally through the body beneath the source, and the body's resistance converts it into a voltage. When that voltage forward-biases the source–body junction enough for it to inject, about 0.6–0.7 V in silicon (Deck 14 p. 25 writes the condition as VBE > Vbi), the parasitic npn transistor turns on.
  3. The transistor injects electrons from the source into the body; they cross it into the high-field drift region, the collector.
  4. In the drift region the electrons impact-ionize. The generated holes drift back into the body and add to the lateral current that forward-biases the source–body junction.
  5. Every hole returned to the base increases the injection, which increases the avalanche current, which returns more holes. When the loop gain exceeds one the device enters second breakdown: the drain current rises while the drain voltage collapses, the device can no longer block, and the current filaments and melts a permanent source–drain short.

The signature at test (Deck 14 p. 22) follows from the mechanism: a large increase in drain current with no change in gate current, and, after the package is opened, damage visible in the drain area. The drain voltage falls at the same moment; the 21 Oct lecture warned that this snapback in the measured voltage is the instrument's current limit acting on a shorted device, not a recovery. Two dependences follow from the loop as well. P-channel power MOSFETs are less susceptible because holes have lower impact-ionization rates and lower mobility than electrons, so the avalanche current that closes the loop is smaller (Deck 14 p. 23); Shenai, Galloway and Schrimpf note that p-channel burnout had not been reported at all at the time of their review. And the burnout threshold voltage does not depend on the off-state gate bias (Deck 14 p. 23), because the gate takes no part in the loop.

The burnout cross-section has a threshold and a saturation in drain voltage. Below a threshold drain voltage, Vth|SEB, no burnout occurs; above it the cross-section rises with VDS, steeply at first, then levels off (Deck 14 pp. 23, 28). The LET moves both: a higher LET lowers the threshold voltage and raises the cross-section at a given voltage. Deck 14 p. 28 shows this for a 55 V commercial MOSFET tested with xenon, argon and carbon ions at several energies (Fernández-Martínez and Papadopoulou): the higher-LET ions give larger cross-sections and reach them at lower bias, and the slide states that no burnout occurred below 50% of the rated voltage for this part (its first xenon events sit at about half the rating). The 21 Oct lecture read the same kind of plot: a change of a few volts near threshold moved the cross-section by several orders of magnitude.

Deck 14 p. 23 states that LET "increases both bias threshold voltage and the cross-section itself for a given voltage". The cross-section half is right; the threshold half is reversed. A higher LET lowers the drain voltage at which burnout begins: Deck 14 p. 28 shows the higher-LET curves starting at lower bias, the 21 Oct lecture said that a lower-LET ion lets the voltage be raised further, and the burnout failure voltage of Deck 03 p. 32 falls from about 200 V to about 75 V as the LET rises (M11 §10). The slide most likely means that LET affects both. Read it as: higher LET, lower threshold, larger cross-section.

The design features that move the threshold act on the two quantities the loop needs, the parasitic transistor's gain and the field in the drift region (Deck 14 p. 24). A heavily doped p⁺ plug under the source lowers the body resistance, so a larger hole current is needed to forward-bias the source–body junction; it prevents bipolar turn-on in normal operation and reduces, without eliminating, the burnout sensitivity under irradiation. A more heavily doped buffer layer between the drift region and the substrate lowers the maximum field where the track's carriers pile up and raises the burnout threshold. Lighter doping of the epitaxial layer, the usual way to raise a part's voltage rating, increases the burnout susceptibility. Trench-gate architectures may be less sensitive. The slide marks the most sensitive area for an ion strike at the channel end of the source, where the track feeds the parasitic base; the IEEE NSREC 2024 Short Course names the channel region as the most sensitive to burnout.

Single-event gate rupture

Gate rupture needs no parasitic transistor; it is a dielectric breakdown. Deck 14 p. 32 gives the mechanism for an ion that strikes through the neck, the region between body wells where the drift region reaches the surface under the gate:

  1. The dense electron–hole plasma along the track separates under the drain field. Electrons are swept to the n⁺ substrate, where they no longer affect the device.
  2. Holes drift toward the surface end of the track and pool against the silicon–oxide interface under the gate, before moving radially through the surface accumulation layer to the p body, where they are collected.
  3. The pooled holes induce an image charge on the gate electrode, which raises the field across the gate oxide transiently. The field grows as holes pool and falls as they diffuse laterally and are collected.
  4. The raised field in the oxide reduces recombination of the pairs the ion generated in the oxide itself, increasing hole trapping in the oxide and the leakage current toward the source electrode.
  5. If the field exceeds what the weakened oxide can hold, the oxide breaks down, the collected holes discharge through it and heat it locally, and a breakdown current that lasts long enough leaves a permanent short through the oxide.

The signature differs from burnout's (Deck 14 p. 30): the gate current rises sharply, usually followed by a similar rise in drain current, and there is often no damage visible on inspection. Any measurable rise in gate current during irradiation indicates that a rupture may have occurred. The dependences follow from the mechanism (Deck 14 p. 31). Raising the magnitude of VGS (negative, to hold an n-channel part off) or VDS raises the rupture cross-section, the first by adding to the field the oxide already holds, the second by driving more holes to the interface. N-channel and p-channel parts are about equally sensitive, which is consistent with a mechanism that involves no carrier multiplication. A higher LET causes rupture at lower VDS and VGS. The worst case occurs when the Bragg peak lies near the interface between the epitaxial layer and the substrate, where the deposited charge is largest within the depleted region. On oxide thickness, the slide states that thick oxides (above about 30 nm) need a higher gate voltage to rupture as they thicken, and that thinner ones (below about 30 nm) appear less susceptible because of their higher dielectric strength. M11 §10 showed the thin-oxide case and its precursor damage; the breakdown of thin oxides under electrical stress is treated in EEE6338 M06 §5.

Gate rupture also leaves damage that does not fail during the test. Deck 14 p. 33 recommends a post-irradiation gate stress (PIGS) test: if the gate bias during irradiation was small, a strike may produce a latent defect instead of a rupture, and stressing the gate after irradiation exposes it. The same latent-damage question returns for silicon carbide in §5, and its statistics in §7.

A power MOSFET under heavy-ion test shows a sudden rise in drain current with no change in gate current; a second sample of the same part, at a higher gate bias, shows the gate current rising first. Identify each event, and state which test circuit can let the first part survive and why it cannot help the second.

The first is burnout: the parasitic npn conducts from drain to source and the gate is not involved, so only the drain current changes and the gate-bias dependence is absent. The second is gate rupture: the oxide has broken down, so current flows through the gate first, and the higher gate bias raised the oxide field, which is the dependence rupture shows and burnout does not. A series resistance in the drain path, with a capacitor to carry the event's signal to an oscilloscope (§6), lets a burnout be detected while the resistance pulls the drain voltage down and stops the runaway before the filament melts; the part survives and can be struck again, which allows a cross-section to be measured on one device. A rupture is complete once the oxide has broken down — the short exists before any external circuit can respond — so no protective circuit can save the part, and each rupture costs a device.

5 · Silicon carbide and gallium nitride

Wide-bandgap semiconductors are replacing silicon in power conversion because they hold a given voltage in a far thinner, more heavily doped layer, which cuts on-resistance, size and mass. For spacecraft that saving in size, weight and power is the attraction (Deck 14 p. 18). The same property, a much higher field in the blocking layer, is what makes their destructive single-event response worse than their material constants suggest. This section compares the three materials, works out what the higher field means for a drift layer, and then gives the observed failure modes of each material.

Silicon carbide: leakage, then burnout

Silicon carbide power MOSFETs and diodes show three destructive or permanent effects under heavy ions: gate rupture, single-event leakage current and burnout (Deck 14 p. 39). Their onset depends on the applied voltage as a fraction of the rating, and diodes and MOSFETs fail at similar fractions, which points to the field in the shared drift region, not the gate or channel, as the controlling quantity (Deck 14 p. 39). The response divides into three regions as the reverse bias is raised (Deck 14 pp. 39–41, 48; Martinella et al. 2022):

  • Charge collection only. At low bias each strike produces a current transient and the leakage returns to its previous value.
  • Single-event leakage current. At moderate bias each strike can leave a small permanent increase in drain and gate leakage, so the leakage climbs with fluence. Deck 14 p. 41 labels it heavy-ion displacement damage formed under the bias during irradiation, located in the p–n-junction and JFET regions of a MOSFET, and notes that the growth with fluence complicates the assessment of the tested parts.
  • Burnout. At higher bias a single strike causes immediate catastrophic failure.

Deck 14 p. 41 calls the leakage damage displacement damage. The papers it draws on describe a thermal mechanism instead: Martinella et al. (2022) attribute it to localized Joule heating along the track that leaves extended defects, and the IEEE NSREC 2024 Short Course to thermal spikes. Read "displacement damage" on the slide as permanent lattice damage along the track, not as the non-ionizing-energy-loss mechanism of M12.

The thresholds are low fractions of the voltage a device is built to block, and the sources normalize them in two ways, to the rating and to the measured breakdown voltage, which differ. In 1200 V junction-barrier Schottky diodes the burnout threshold is about 40% of the rated voltage, about 500 V (Deck 14 p. 40, Sengupta et al. 2023). In the diodes rated 650–3300 V that Deck 14 p. 45 plots, immediate failure occurs between 30% and 47% of the measured breakdown voltage, even at the lowest LET tested, 2.9 MeV·cm²/mg with neon. The heavy-ion response saturates at LETs well below those that typical mission requirements specify (Deck 14 p. 45); the saturated burnout threshold is about 500 V for 1200 V parts and about 800 V for 3300 V parts, roughly 30% and 20% of their avalanche breakdown voltages, or about 42% and 24% of their ratings (M11 §10). Voltage rating, epitaxial thickness and doping move the thresholds substantially, which Deck 14 p. 46 states as a call for structural and process optimization. Martinella et al. sectioned a failed 1.2 kV MOSFET from Wolfspeed's second generation with a focused ion beam and imaged it with secondary and backscattered electrons (Deck 14 pp. 43–44). That device had survived the heavy-ion exposure and failed afterwards, in a post-irradiation drain-voltage measurement: latent damage. The failure left a hole about 30 × 20 µm across and about 18 µm deep through the epitaxial layer, where the silicon carbide had decomposed.

Strikes that neither burn out the device nor raise its leakage much still weaken it. Deck 14 p. 42 reports time-dependent dielectric breakdown failures in gate-oxide stress tests after irradiation, with a dependence on the drain bias during irradiation and on the LET, and states that heavy-ion-induced damage degrades long-term reliability; the paper it cites, Niskanen et al. (2023), shows the effect after 53 MeV proton irradiation. Martinella et al. (2022) titled their heavy-ion study for the same finding, latent damage. A part that survives a heavy-ion test is not shown by that survival to be undamaged, which §7 turns into a statistical question.

Gallium nitride: no junction, but high-field corners

A gallium nitride high-electron-mobility transistor (HEMT) conducts through a two-dimensional electron gas (2DEG), a sheet of electrons confined at the heterojunction between an AlGaN barrier and the GaN layer (Deck 14 p. 35), where the difference in polarization between the two materials induces it without doping (Johnston 2010). The device is lateral: source, gate and drain sit on the surface, over a GaN buffer grown on silicon or sapphire. A plain HEMT is normally on, because the 2DEG exists at zero gate bias; power switches are made normally off by a p-type GaN layer under the gate that depletes the 2DEG beneath it, the enhancement-mode or e-mode device. The structure has no p–n junction in the current path, so it has no parasitic bipolar transistor and no avalanche breakdown in the silicon sense; Deck 14 p. 35 puts it as "no junctions, no avalanche". It still holds the drain voltage across short regions, at the drain-side edge of the gate and at the ends of the field plates that spread that field, and across the buffer to the substrate. Those regions fail.

Deck 14 p. 36 lists the three modes, with the caveat that their mechanisms are active research. Burnout is a fully destructive drain–substrate short with a large rise in drain current; it needs a high voltage, but its cross-section is not negligible (Zerarka et al., from device simulation). Single-event leakage current is a moderate but instantaneous rise in drain current from permanent damage at the drain–substrate interface (Abbate et al.). Single-event dielectric rupture is breakdown of the dielectric under the field plate between gate and drain, which raises both drain and gate current; its cross-section is low. Deck 14 p. 37 summarizes tests at CERN with high-energy neutrons and protons at 90–100% of the maximum bias: 100 V parts showed no burnout, and 600 V parts could burn out, with lower cross-sections than the silicon MOSFETs tested alongside. Deck 14 p. 38 shows heavy-ion leakage and burnout in e-mode devices (Billa et al., NSREC 2025), and the 21 Oct lecture described the pattern seen in the course's own heavy-ion data: drain leakage rising in steps at successive strikes without recovering, then a sudden burnout. The lecture's group tested these devices without the current-limited burnout circuit of §6, since no bipolar mode needs separating, and without a gate load, since the p-GaN gate has no gate dielectric to rupture.

Explain why a 1200 V silicon carbide MOSFET can burn out at a lower fraction of its rating than a 1200 V silicon MOSFET, even though silicon carbide needs more than twice the energy to create an electron–hole pair.

Burnout depends on the field the ion's charge lands in, not only on the amount of charge. Charge per micrometre at equal LET is LET × density ÷ pair energy: 10.4 fC/µm in silicon and 6.6 fC/µm in silicon carbide, so the trigger is only about a third smaller. The drift region that holds 1200 V is about 8 µm thick in silicon carbide at a peak field near 3 MV/cm, against about 80 µm and 0.3 MV/cm in silicon (16.3). A track through the silicon carbide layer therefore places its carriers in a field up to ten times higher, and the power density J·E that heats the filament is up to ten times higher for the same current; the IEEE NSREC 2024 Short Course identifies this heat-generation density, not impact ionization, as the reason silicon carbide diodes burn out. Measured burnout thresholds of silicon carbide devices, near 40% of rating and 20–47% of breakdown depending on the device and the normalization, reflect this; the pair-energy advantage is outweighed by the field.

6 · Measuring destructive events

A destructive event is measured by counting events per unit fluence, the cross-section of M11 §6, but three things make it harder than counting upsets: each event can cost a device, the counts are small, and the ion must reach a sensitive region tens of micrometres deep. This section gives the standard test, the conditions the beam must meet, and the statistics of small and zero counts.

The test

Burnout and gate-rupture tests of power MOSFETs follow MIL-STD-750 Method 1080 (Deck 14 pp. 26, 33). For burnout, a drain–source voltage is applied with the gate well below threshold, normally at 0 V, so the device is off; the gate and drain currents are monitored; and the part is irradiated until it fails or until a maximum fluence, 1×10⁷ ions/cm² for heavy ions, is reached. For gate rupture the gate bias is a test variable as well. Deck 14 p. 27 gives the burnout test circuit. A series resistor R1 in the drain supply limits the current during an event, so the drain voltage collapses before the filament melts; a capacitor C1, with a series resistor Rp to limit its discharge, carries the event's pulse to an oscilloscope; a terminating resistor R3 prevents reflections on the line; and two switches move the circuit between test and characterization modes.

The protective circuit makes non-destructive burnout counting possible, and Deck 14 p. 26 lists what that buys and what it risks. Fewer parts are needed, which matters most when low-energy heavy ions require the parts to be delidded; a cross-section, events per fluence on one device, is a more direct and more accurate measure of sensitivity than the fluence to the first failure; and more development testing becomes affordable. The risks are that the circuit may suppress events that would have been destructive, and that a counted event may not be a real burnout. Gate rupture has no protective circuit (Deck 14 p. 33): many devices are needed to build a cross-section, and each data point needs fresh parts.

What the beam must reach

The ion must deposit its charge in the sensitive region, which for a power device means through the drift region to the substrate. Deck 14 p. 51 states the requirement directly: ions with enough energy to penetrate into the substrate. The LET of an ion is not constant along its path; it rises to the Bragg peak near the end of its range (Deck 14 p. 10; M04 §2), so the same species at different energies probes different depths with different LETs. On Deck 14 p. 28 the lowest-energy xenon beam gave the largest burnout cross-section at a given bias; the 21 Oct lecture explained that its Bragg peak lay in the sensitive region, while the higher-energy xenon beams passed through it at a lower LET. For gate rupture the worst case is a Bragg peak near the interface between the epitaxial layer and the substrate (§4). Range in a given material is calculated with SRIM (EEL5385 M13).

Four further conditions are listed on Deck 14 p. 51. Because the heavy-ion response saturates at LETs below typical requirements, tests with lighter ions at lower LET are needed to see differences between designs and to support on-orbit risk assessment. The angle of incidence, the sample temperature and the sample preparation all change the result. And the test fluence is itself a problem in silicon carbide: leakage degradation accumulates with fluence, so the fluence needed to establish a burnout threshold also damages the part, which Deck 14 p. 51 calls the most significant challenge to testing silicon carbide power devices. Proton and neutron tests are indirect: the destructive events come from nuclear reactions of the incident particle, whose recoils carry the high LET (M11 §11).

Cross-sections from small counts

The cross-section at each bias is the count divided by the fluence. A count of N independent events is a sample from a Poisson distribution, so its standard deviation is √N and its relative uncertainty is 1/√N: a count of 4 carries a 50% uncertainty, a count of 100 a 10% one. When no events occur, the count gives no estimate at all, only an upper bound. If the true cross-section is σ, the expected number of events in fluence Φ is σΦ, and the Poisson probability of observing none is exp(−σΦ). The one-sided upper bound at confidence level C is the cross-section for which observing zero events would be that improbable: exp(−σΦ) = 1 − C.

Zero events does not prove immunity, for two reasons. Statistically, it bounds the cross-section at 3/Φ, and a larger fluence would lower the bound but never to zero. Physically, the bound holds only for the conditions tested: one ion, one energy and range, one angle, one temperature and one bias. A different ion that places its Bragg peak deeper, or a bias a few percent higher on the steep part of the curve, can produce events where none were seen.

Comparing devices of different ratings needs the voltage normalized to the rating, VDS/Vrated. A device is designed so that its drift-region field approaches the critical field at its rated voltage (16.3), so equal fractions of rating correspond to roughly comparable peak fields in different devices, and thresholds stated as fractions of rating — no burnout below 50% in the silicon MOSFET of Deck 14 p. 28, about 40% in the 1200 V silicon carbide diodes of p. 40 — can be compared across ratings and technologies. A fraction of the measured breakdown voltage is a different scale, because parts break down above their rating. The normalization is approximate: the saturated burnout threshold of silicon carbide is a smaller fraction for 3300 V parts than for 1200 V parts (M11 §10), because drift-region thickness and doping do not scale exactly with the rating.

7 · From test data to a flight decision

A single-event upset rate can be calculated from a cross-section curve and the environment's LET spectrum (M11 §8). For burnout and gate rupture no such method has been validated (Deck 14 p. 50). The test data are used instead to draw a boundary — a safe operating area — and the part is operated inside it with a margin. This section gives that procedure, the probability arithmetic that supports it and its limits, the statistics of latent damage, and the levers available when the safe area is too small.

The safe operating area and derating

Deck 14 p. 50 states the practice. Test standards and guidelines set worst-case test conditions to bound the on-orbit susceptibility; because there is no validated rate prediction for burnout or gate rupture, the heavy-ion data define a response curve that delineates a safe operating voltage area for the beam conditions tested, and devices are operated below the threshold biases for failure. Burnout and gate-rupture test-method standards for silicon carbide are still being developed. In practice the boundary is the threshold drain voltage (for burnout) or the (VDS, VGS) rupture boundary (for gate rupture) against LET, up to the highest LET the mission must survive, and the operating point is placed below it by a derating margin.

Derating rules summarize such curves. For silicon, Deck 14 p. 28 notes that no burnout occurred below 50% of the rated voltage in its data, so derating to below 50% is usable as a rule of thumb, with the slide's warning that the radiation engineer's judgement must never be dropped. The warning has force: the silicon-against-silicon-carbide MOSFET plot on Deck 14 p. 45, which carries no reference, shows silicon parts failing at 20–40% of their normalized voltage above an LET of about 25 MeV·cm²/mg, so the 50% figure is one part's result, not a property of silicon. Johnston states that burnout of silicon power devices usually requires an LET above 20 MeV·cm²/mg when the devices are not operated near their voltage limits, and that accepted practice for space derates by at least 20%. For atmospheric neutrons, Normand concluded from beam tests that burnout is a concern only in devices rated above about 400 V, and becomes negligible when such devices are derated to below about 300 V (Normand, in Schrimpf and Fleetwood, 2004). Silicon carbide needs deeper derating: thresholds near 40% of rating, and 20–47% of breakdown (§5), mean a 1200 V part may be usable only at a few hundred volts in a heavy-ion environment, which the 19 and 21 Oct lectures gave as the reason silicon carbide power MOSFETs have not yet been widely flown. The derating gives back much of the voltage advantage that made the material attractive.

The arithmetic is simple and its assumptions are not, which is why Deck 14 p. 50 declines to call it a rate prediction. Seven limits apply. The space environment is a spectrum of ions over all LETs and angles, and collapsing it to one fluence "at the tested condition" is the step no validated method supports. The cross-section has the statistical uncertainty of a small count, and a decision should use its upper bound. The test used one ion, energy, range and angle, and a flight ion with a deeper Bragg peak or a grazing path can be worse. Temperature in flight can differ from the test. Parts vary from device to device and lot to lot, and a few devices per condition sample that variation poorly. The flight bias is not constant: switching transients can carry the drain voltage above its nominal value onto the steep part of the cross-section curve. And the Poisson model assumes independent events at a constant cross-section, which fails in silicon carbide, where leakage damage accumulates with fluence and changes the device during the mission.

Latent damage in the survivors

A part that survives irradiation may carry damage that shortens its life (§4, §5). The test for it compares irradiated survivors with unirradiated controls under the same accelerated stress, such as a gate-oxide stress that drives time-dependent dielectric breakdown. The question is whether a difference in failure fractions between two small groups could have arisen by chance, and Fisher's exact test answers it without large-sample approximations: given the total number of failures, it computes the probability that they would fall as unevenly as observed, or more so, if irradiation had no effect.

Irradiated survivors against controls

Given Twelve silicon carbide MOSFETs that survived a heavy-ion exposure and twelve unirradiated controls from the same lot undergo the same accelerated gate stress. Four irradiated devices fail early; no control fails. The numbers are illustrative. Find the failure fractions, the probability of so uneven a split by chance, and a 95% upper bound on the controls' failure probability.

Plan. Under the hypothesis that irradiation has no effect, the four failures are four devices drawn at random from twenty-four; the chance that all four are irradiated ones is a ratio of combinations. Zero failures among twelve controls bounds their failure probability as a binomial zero count.

Failure fractions. Irradiated 4/12 = 0.33; controls 0/12 = 0.

Ways to place the failures. The number of ways to choose which 4 of the 24 devices fail is C(24, 4) = 24·23·22·21/(4·3·2·1) = 10 626. The number with all 4 among the irradiated is C(12, 4) × C(12, 0) = 495 × 1 = 495.

Probability of a split this uneven. No split is more extreme than 4–0, so p = 495/10 626 = 0.047 (one-sided). Why one-sided: the hypothesis being tested is that irradiation increases failures, not that it changes them in either direction.

Bound on the controls. With true failure probability f, the chance of no failures in 12 is (1 − f)¹². Setting it to 0.05: 1 − f = 0.051/12 = exp(ln 0.05 / 12) = exp(−0.250) = 0.779, so f95 = 0.22.

0.33 against 0; p = 0.047; the controls' failure probability is below 0.22 at 95% confidence. Check: p is just under 0.05, so even a 4–0 split in groups of twelve is barely significant at the 5% level; a 3–0 split would give C(12,3)/C(24,3) = 220/2024 = 0.11 and would not be.

What such a comparison can establish is limited, in both directions. It can show that the irradiated group fails early more often than chance would explain, which is evidence of latent damage under the stress applied. It cannot, from a dozen devices, give the size of the effect with any precision: the irradiated failure fraction of 0.33 has a wide confidence interval. It cannot locate or identify the defect, which needs failure analysis of the failed parts. It cannot translate an accelerated-stress result into a lifetime at flight conditions without an acceleration model for the failure mechanism. And it cannot rule out a difference in handling between the two groups, which is why the controls must come from the same lot and see every step except the beam. A difference that does not reach significance is not evidence of no damage either; it means the sample was too small to see it.

Levers when the safe area is too small

When derating would remove the performance the part was chosen for, the alternatives act on the device, the test or the circuit. In the device, the levers are those of §4 and §5: a buffer layer that lowers the peak field where the track's carriers pile up, a p⁺ plug or lower body resistance that keeps the parasitic transistor off, a trench gate, and in silicon carbide an optimized epitaxial thickness and doping, which Deck 14 pp. 46–47 identify as the route to tolerant designs; in gallium nitride, gate and field-plate design that spreads the drain-side field. Choosing a part with a higher rating and operating it at the required voltage is the simplest form of the same idea, as the 21 Oct lecture recommended: a higher-rated part at a fixed voltage sits at a lower fraction of its rating. In qualification, the levers are tests that cover the conditions the bound depends on: ions with the range to reach the substrate, several LETs including light ions below saturation, the worst-case angle and temperature, enough devices per condition to sample part-to-part variation, post-irradiation gate stress against controls, and fluences high enough to make the zero-event bounds meaningful. In the circuit, current limiting and redundancy (§8) contain an event the device cannot prevent.

Device simulation is how the device levers are found before parts exist. Technology computer-aided design (TCAD) solves Poisson's equation and the electron and hole continuity equations on a two- or three-dimensional model of the device, with impact ionization, and for burnout the lattice heat equation as well; the ion is represented as a column of generated pairs whose density follows its LET along the track. Two investigations follow from this module's mechanisms. The first scans strike position, LET and depth at fixed bias to find where a strike triggers burnout and at what voltage; it monitors the electric-field distribution and where its peak moves during the transient, the impact-ionization generation rate, the electron and hole densities and current density along the track, the lattice temperature against the melting or decomposition temperature of the material, and in silicon the base–emitter voltage of the parasitic transistor. The second adds defects, as traps at the interfaces or in the buffer that displacement damage could create, to test whether they reproduce the permanent leakage of SELC; it monitors the leakage current path, the trap occupancy and the field at the defect location. Deck 14 p. 36 cites simulations of this kind for gallium nitride (Zerarka et al.).

Eight irradiated devices and eight controls are stressed; two irradiated devices fail early and no control does. A colleague concludes that the radiation caused latent damage. Compute the one-sided Fisher probability and say what the result does and does not support.

Under no effect, the two failures are two devices drawn from sixteen. C(16, 2) = 16·15/2 = 120 ways; with both among the irradiated, C(8, 2) = 28 ways. p = 28/120 = 0.23. A split at least this uneven would occur by chance nearly a quarter of the time, so the data do not support the conclusion at any conventional significance level. They do not refute it either: two failures in eight is consistent with a real effect that a sample of eight is too small to detect. The defensible statement is that the test is inconclusive, and the next step is more devices per group, failure analysis of the two failed devices to identify the failure site, and controls that share every step except the beam.

8 · The system and the mission

A destructive event begins in one device but does not stay there. Deck 14 p. 49 traces how latchup and burnout currents disturb the rest of a spacecraft, and its last slide (p. 52) states the conclusion the course has been building toward: effective hardness assurance unites total-dose, single-event and displacement-damage testing with hardening by design and system-level mitigation. This section gives the propagation paths, the mitigation, and the argument for qualifying against all three effects together.

How one event reaches the rest of the system

Latchup and burnout events often propagate through shared power domains, affecting several integrated circuits or power converters (Deck 14 p. 49). A latched or shorted part draws current from a rail it shares with others; the rail's source impedance turns that current into a voltage sag, and the other parts on the rail see a brownout, reset, or shut down. The fast current step also disturbs circuits that share no rail. Any loop that carries it has inductance — bond wires, package leads, board traces, cable harnesses — and an inductance L carrying a current that changes at rate di/dt develops a voltage L di/dt across it, which appears as ground bounce or supply noise wherever the loop is shared, and the loop radiates. Deck 14 p. 49 calls this radiation-induced electromagnetic interference (RIEMI) and lists its coupling paths as power and ground networks and decoupling loops, package inductance and board traces, and cable harnesses and connectors. Its consequences are false switching, logic resets and corrupted communication between subsystems.

Ground bounce from a current step

Given A burnout in a converter switch drives a 2 A current step with a 20 ns rise through a shared ground return whose inductance is 5 nH. The values are illustrative. Find the voltage developed across the return, and compare it with a 1.8 V logic swing.

Plan. V = L di/dt, with the step approximated as a linear ramp over its rise time.

Rate of change. di/dt = 2 A / 20×10⁻⁹ s = 1.0×10⁸ A/s.

Voltage. V = 5×10⁻⁹ H × 1.0×10⁸ A/s = 0.50 V.

V ≈ 0.5 V for the duration of the edge, more than a quarter of a 1.8 V logic swing. Check: henries × amperes per second = volt-seconds per ampere × amperes per second = volts. A logic gate referenced to the bouncing ground can switch falsely, which is the mechanism behind the false switching Deck 14 p. 49 lists.

The mitigations Deck 14 p. 49 lists act on these paths: local current limiting, which stops the event's current at the struck part; EMI shielding; segregated grounds, so that one subsystem's return current does not flow through another's reference; and a robust power-distribution network, with low-inductance decoupling that supplies fast current locally and ferrite isolation between domains. Deck 14 p. 5 adds redundancy and hardening by design: a redundant converter or switch that takes over when one fails turns a destroyed part into a recoverable system event, and circuit techniques that keep a struck node from reaching the conditions of §§2–4 reduce the events at their origin (rad-hard design for single events, M14).

Why a mission is qualified against all three effects together

The three effects are measured separately, with gamma rays for total dose, heavy ions for single events and protons or neutrons for displacement damage, but in orbit a part receives them together, and each changes the response to the others. A prior total dose changes single-event sensitivity: in floating-gate memories, prior dose amplifies heavy-ion upset sensitivity because the threshold shifts from the two exposures add (IEEE NSREC 2025 Short Course, Part III-A), and in static memories the leakage that dose causes changes the upset response (Schwank et al., 2006). Heavy ions also leave permanent lattice damage in silicon carbide, the leakage-current degradation of §5, so a single-event test there accumulates a cumulative degradation as it runs (Deck 14 pp. 41, 51). Protons, which deliver dose, displacement damage and single events at once, degrade the gate oxide's long-term reliability (Deck 14 p. 42; Niskanen et al. 2023). A part qualified to a dose specification alone, or to each effect in isolation, can therefore fail in a way none of the separate tests revealed. M05 §7 compared the three effects side by side; total-dose assurance is M15, displacement damage M12.

A power subsystem uses one 28 V bus for three converters. One converter's switch is susceptible to burnout at the bus voltage. Name the propagation paths that put the other two converters at risk and one mitigation for each.

First, the shared bus: a burned-out switch shorts its converter's input, the bus current surges and the bus voltage sags through its source impedance, so the other converters brown out or shut down. Local current limiting or a fuse or switch at the failed converter's input isolates the short, and a redundant converter restores its function. Second, inductive coupling: the fast current step through shared ground returns and package and board inductance develops L di/dt voltages that shift the other converters' references and can switch their control logic falsely; segregated grounds and low-inductance local decoupling reduce it. Third, radiation from the current loop and harness couples into control and communication lines; shielding and ferrite isolation reduce it. The root measure is to derate the switch so that the bus voltage sits inside its safe operating area (§7).

Where this leaves us

A destructive single event is a feedback loop that an ion switches on and a power supply drives. The ion supplies picojoules and picocoulombs; the destruction is millijoules of supply energy delivered through a parasitic thyristor, a parasitic bipolar transistor with avalanche multiplication, or a dielectric pushed past its breakdown field, and heated until it melts. Power devices are exposed because they hold the highest fields when off, and the wide-bandgap materials hold ten times higher fields still, which outweighs their larger pair energies. Because no validated rate method exists for burnout or gate rupture, the engineering answer is a safe operating area drawn from tests and a derating margin below it, with small-count statistics deciding what the tests can claim. Rad-hard design for single events (M14) takes up the circuit techniques; M12 continues with displacement damage.

Summary

  • Soft against hard: permanence and loss of function are separate questions. SEL is cleared by power cycling if cut in time; SELC is permanent but the part works; SEB, SEGR and SEDR destroy it.
  • Energy: ion ≈ 15 pJ and 4 pC (LET 40, 10 µm Si); supply ≈ mJ. Damage scales with the time before the current is cut.
  • Avalanche (16.1): M = 1/[1 − (V/VBR)n]; thermal runaway when heating outruns conduction; electromigration in metal.
  • SEL: needs supply above VH and current above IH; limit current or detect and cycle power; worse hot; latent metal damage.
  • SEB (Si): off state; parasitic npn (source–body–drift) plus avalanche; drain current only; threshold falls and σ rises with LET; p⁺ plug and buffer layer help, lighter epi hurts; p-channel resistant.
  • SEGR: holes pool under the neck oxide; gate current first; no protective circuit; (16.2) Vcrit = EBDtox/(1 + Z/44); PIGS for latent damage.
  • WBG: (16.3) a 1200 V drift layer is 80 µm at 0.3 MV/cm in Si, 8 µm at 3 MV/cm in SiC; charge per µm per unit LET 10.4 (Si), 6.6 (SiC), 11.1 (GaN) fC/µm. SiC: transients → SELC → SEB, burnout near 40% of rating (20–47% of breakdown), saturating with LET; latent damage in survivors. GaN: no junction or avalanche, but SEB, SELC and SEDR at high-field corners.
  • Statistics (16.4, 16.5): σ = N/Φ, ±1/√N; zero events → σ95 = 3.0/Φ, a bound, not immunity; P(≥ 1) = 1 − exp(−σΦ); normalize to V/Vrated; latent damage by survivors against controls (Fisher).
  • Flight: no validated SEB/SEGR rate method; safe operating area plus derating (Si < 50% for one part, not a rule of the material; SiC lower); RIEMI through rails, L di/dt and radiation; qualify TID, SEE and DD together.
