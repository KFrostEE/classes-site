Module 12b

Displacement Damage: Measurement, Modelling and Assurance

What displacement damage does to each class of device, how the damage is measured — from I–V and C–V curves to deep-level spectroscopy and photoconductive decay — how generation turns into dark current and noise, what it does to imagers and solar cells, how it is modelled from particle transport to a pixel's dark current, why a small bipolar transistor departs from Messenger–Spratt, how one cascade changes one nanoscale device, and how a part is qualified for a mission.

Prerequisites M12, M06 Read time ~65 min Sources Deck 15 pp. 3, 6, 14, 16–46; Deck 03 pp. 42–45; Deck 02 p. 25; Deck 04 p. 47; lecture of 28 Oct; course textbook; NSREC short courses 1999, 2021, 2024, 2025

M12 followed displacement damage from a collision to the carriers of the crystal: stable defects with levels in the gap generate, recombine, trap and remove carriers and shorten the scattering time, so lifetime, carrier concentration, mobility and bipolar gain all change, and NIEL scales the damage from one particle to another where the defect inventory does not depend on the recoil spectrum. This module takes that physics into the laboratory and the mission. It asks how each change shows up in a device and how it is measured, how the damage is predicted when it cannot be measured, and how a part is shown to survive a given orbit.

The order follows the chain an engineer works along. §1 maps each damage process to the device that shows it. §2 sets out the measurements, each of which reads one part of the defect picture: terminal currents, doping, the defect's energy level and concentration, or the lifetime. §3 develops the most widely recorded effect, generation current, together with the noise it adds and the universal damage factor that predicts its mean. §4 applies the chain to imagers and solar cells, the devices whose useful life it most often sets. §5 lays out the modelling tools, from ion transport to molecular dynamics, and what each can and cannot predict. §6 corrects the Messenger–Spratt relation for small transistors, §7 takes the limit in which one cascade is as large as the device, and §8 assembles the qualification: mission dose, equivalent test fluence, case studies where NIEL equivalence breaks, and the controls a test plan needs.

1 · What the damage does to each device

Every device consequence of displacement damage is one of the five processes of M12 §3, or the shortened scattering time of its (12.5), acting where a device depends on it. Deck 15 p. 6, after E. Auden, lists the electrical outcome. Increased: leakage or dark current, power consumption, channel on-resistance and noise. Decreased: charge transfer efficiency, carrier lifetime, carrier mobility and gain. Changed: carrier concentration. The susceptible parts are diodes, bipolar transistors, solar cells, MOSFETs and power MOSFETs, and pixel arrays — charge-coupled devices, active pixel sensors and other detectors. The diode is the simplest case and the one in which a single particle becomes visible. Deck 15 p. 17, after Auden et al. (2012), shows the reverse current of a silicon diode exposed to a californium-252 source, which emits alpha particles and fission fragments, the fragments being energetic heavy ions. The reverse current carries transient pulses as these particles deposit charge, and after the strike the slide labels as one heavy ion the baseline steps up and stays up, from about 10 fA to about 20 fA on the slide's left panel: a discrete, permanent increase in leakage from a single heavy ion. Over 120 hours of exposure the steps add into a rising leakage current. Both data panels are reverse currents; the slide draws the forward-biased diode only as a cross-section. Leakage is measured in reverse because there the depletion region is swept of carriers and every pair a defect generates is collected, whereas in forward bias injected carriers swamp generation. The 2024 NSREC Short Course states the mechanism: in reverse bias electrons and holes are generated at defect sites and collected. The same mechanism raises the dark current of CCD pixels (sister course EEL5385 M21 §9).

The MOSFET row needs the ordering of M12 §3. A MOSFET conducts by majority carriers through a channel only nanometres deep, so it does not need a long lifetime, and at the fluences that ruin a bipolar transistor's gain it is barely affected. Deck 15 p. 19 shows what happens at high proton fluence, after Faccio et al. (2009), whose devices are high-voltage power MOSFETs: the on-resistance rises — about twofold by about 9×10¹⁴ p/cm² and by roughly three orders of magnitude by about 5×10¹⁵ p/cm², read from the slide — and the output conductance degrades. Both follow from (12.5) of M12: carrier removal lowers the electron concentration in the lightly doped drift and drain regions, and added scattering centres lower the mobility, so the resistance of every series region rises.

A spacecraft carries a silicon PIN photodiode, a discrete n–p–n transistor and a CMOS logic chip behind the same shielding. Rank them by how early displacement damage changes a parameter the circuit uses, and name that parameter for each.

The photodiode first: it is a large depleted volume held in reverse bias, so every defect generates, and its dark current rises in proportion to displacement damage dose times depleted volume from the first exposure (§3). Its responsivity follows later, as the lifetime of carriers collected by diffusion falls. The bipolar transistor next: its gain depends on the lifetime of the electrons crossing the base, and 1/β rises linearly with fluence from the start; a high-gain, low-cutoff-frequency part shows it soonest (M12 §3). The CMOS logic last: it conducts by majority carriers in a shallow channel, needs no long lifetime, and changes only when carrier removal and mobility loss set in at very high fluence. Logic usually fails first from total dose instead. The ranking follows the order in which the damage constants act: lifetime and generation before carrier concentration before mobility.

2 · Measuring the damage

Deck 15 p. 16 lists the measurements and what each yields. I–V curves show changes in leakage or dark current and in gain. Deep-level transient spectroscopy (DLTS) and deep-level optical spectroscopy (DLOS) give a defect's energy level, its concentration and its capture rates. Photoconductive decay gives the minority-carrier lifetime. C–V measurements, which Deck 15 names among its objectives (p. 3) and which are standard for oxide charge (M06 §2), give the doping profile of a junction and therefore carrier removal. Each reads a different link of the chain from defect to device, so they complement rather than duplicate one another: a terminal measurement shows that a part has changed, and a spectroscopic one shows which defect changed it.

I–V: leakage and gain

The terminal current is the cheapest measurement and the one closest to the application. Deck 15 p. 16 reproduces the reverse I–V curves of a neutron-irradiated diode after Khanna, Pepper and Stone (1992): as the fluence rises from 10¹³ to 3×10¹⁵ cm⁻², the reverse current at a given bias rises by more than two orders of magnitude, while the breakdown near 6 V stays where it was. The slide does not name the mechanism. Generation current, which §3 quantifies, rises with fluence; but every curve climbs by orders of magnitude with bias, far more steeply than a generation current growing with the depletion width, so near breakdown field-assisted emission and tunnelling through the defect levels (M12 §3, process 5) contribute as well. In a bipolar transistor the same measurement is a Gummel plot, collector and base current against base–emitter voltage: displacement damage lifts the base current and leaves the collector current, and the gain at each bias is their ratio (M06 §7). An I–V curve says how much a part has changed. It does not say which defect changed it, and it mixes displacement damage with any ionization damage the same exposure delivered.

DLTS and DLOS: the defect's level and concentration

DLTS measures the defects themselves. The 2025 NSREC Short Course describes the sequence: a diode is held at a steady reverse bias, and a short electrical pulse reduces the field in the depletion region, letting free carriers from the neutral material flow in and fill the defects there. When the reverse bias returns, the trapped carriers are re-emitted by thermal emission, and the capacitance relaxes as they leave. A pulse that only reduces the reverse bias fills majority-carrier traps; to fill minority-carrier traps the pulse forward-biases the junction and injects minority carriers, the "injection pulse" printed on Lang's spectrum below. The emission rate is thermally activated: it rises as exp(−Ea/kT), where Ea is the level's energy separation from the band it emits to — the conduction band for an electron trap, the valence band for a hole trap — and a factor T² enters from the temperature dependence of the thermal velocity and of the band's density of states. Lang's method, cited on Deck 15 p. 16, records the capacitance at two times t1 and t2 after each pulse and plots their difference against temperature. The difference is largest at the temperature where the defect's emission rate equals the rate window that the pair of times selects.

e(T) ∝ T2 exp(−EakT),    emax = ln(t2/t1)t2 − t1

DLOS replaces heat with light. The 2024 NSREC Short Course defines it as the measurement of carriers emitted from defects as a function of photon energy, which also gives the defect's optical ionization energy; both DLTS and DLOS can use capacitance or current transients. The reason for the second method is the exponential in (12b.2). At 300 K, with the same prefactor, a level 1.5 eV deep emits 1.6×10⁻¹⁸ times as fast as a level 0.44 eV deep, so a deep level in a wide-bandgap material such as GaN or SiC empties far too slowly to measure at room temperature. Heating helps — by (12b.2) such a level emits a few hundred times a second near 700 K, and high-temperature DLTS reaches levels near 1.5 eV in silicon carbide — but a photon of energy above the level's depth empties it directly at any temperature, and the photon energy at which emission begins measures the depth.

Photoconductive decay: the lifetime

The lifetime itself is read from the conductivity. A short light pulse with photon energy above the bandgap creates equal excess densities of electrons and holes, Δn = Δp, and by (12.5) of M12 the conductivity rises by q(μn + μp)Δn. When the pulse ends, recombination removes the excess at the rate Δn/τ, so the excess conductance decays exponentially with the recombination lifetime as its time constant.

After a proton exposure, a silicon solar cell's short-circuit current has fallen and its reverse current in the dark has risen. Which two measurements would most directly tell whether one defect causes both changes, and what result would show it?

DLTS and photoconductive decay, measured on the same material at several fluences. DLTS identifies the levels present and their concentrations, and gives each one's depth from the Arrhenius slope; photoconductive decay gives the recombination lifetime, whose reciprocal rises with the recombination-centre density. If one DLTS peak, near midgap, grows in proportion to fluence while 1/τ − 1/τ0 grows in the same proportion, and the leakage increase tracks it too, one level is generating and recombining: a level near midgap is the most effective at both, as M12 §3 explains. A third measurement, I–V against temperature, would add the activation energy of the leakage itself, which for midgap generation in silicon is about 0.63–0.65 eV, a little above half the 1.12 eV gap (§3). C–V would show whether the same exposure also removed carriers. Two measurements of the terminal current alone would not separate one defect from two.

3 · Generation current, dark current and noise

Generation acts wherever a reverse-biased depletion region exists: in every photodiode and pixel, in every isolation junction and in every detector. This section quantifies it, follows it into the noise it adds, and states the universal damage factor that predicts its mean from the displacement damage dose. The 2024 NSREC Short Course's warning applies throughout: the generation lifetime is a different quantity from the recombination lifetime, and the two are not interchangeable.

In a depletion region, carrier densities are far below ni, and the SRH rate of M12 §3 becomes a generation rate. For a level at midgap with equal capture rates for electrons and holes, it reduces to ni/(2τg) per unit volume, and every pair generated is collected, so the current is that rate times the charge and the depleted volume.

A dark current is a stream of independent charges, so it carries shot noise: the number of electrons collected in a fixed time fluctuates with a variance equal to its mean. Goiffon's 2021 NSREC Short Course states it for an image sensor — the standard deviation of a shot noise equals the square root of its mean, so a pixel collecting N dark electrons in an integration has a dark-current noise of √N electrons. The increase degrades an image in two separate ways. The added dark signal takes up part of each pixel's full-well capacity and adds a pedestal that a dark frame can subtract. The added shot noise cannot be subtracted: it is a random fluctuation, different in every frame. Goiffon shows that the loss of dynamic range in a damaged sensor comes mainly from the rise in dark-current shot noise, and that once detail is lost below that noise, removing the mean dark offset does not recover it. Deck 04 p. 47 lists the combined impact of total dose, displacement damage and single events on a CCD: reduced signal-to-noise ratio, image streaks, hot pixels and loss of faint-object detection. The streaks belong to transfer loss (§4) and blooming; the reduced signal-to-noise ratio and the lost faint objects follow from the dark-current rise. The faint object is lost first because its signal is smallest against a noise floor that has risen as the square root of the dark current.

The universal damage factor

Srour and Lo (2000) found that, in silicon, the increase in thermal generation rate per unit volume is proportional to the displacement damage dose with one constant across devices, particles and doping. Deck 15 p. 22 quotes their definition — the increase in thermal generation rate per unit deposited displacement damage dose — and shows the data: the generation-rate increase per unit fluence against the dose per unit fluence, which is NIEL, for electrons, cobalt-60 gamma rays, protons, neutrons, pions and heavy ions, over more than seven decades of NIEL. Protons, neutrons, pions and heavy ions lie on one line of unit slope. Cobalt-60 gammas and the lowest-NIEL electron points fall below it, on a steeper, quadratic line, while the highest-NIEL electron point already sits on the linear one — the same point-defect regime in which M12 §4 found NIEL scaling failing for electrons in p-type silicon.

Linear scaling with displacement damage dose fails under conditions that each follow from the physics. The factor is a mean, so it says nothing about the distribution, whose tail is set by rare inelastic events and by fields (below). It applies at about 300 K and one week after exposure; generation is thermally activated, so at another temperature the factor changes, and a reading taken before short-term annealing is complete exceeds what the factor predicts, because the factor is defined after about a week of annealing. It assumes no strong field: emission enhanced by a field of about 10⁵ V/cm raises the rate and lowers its activation energy. It fails at the low-NIEL end, where electrons and gamma rays produce point defects whose generation rate lies below the linear line, so the factor over-predicts. It assumes no ionizing contribution, whereas total dose adds surface generation through interface traps. And it assumes the damage is many small events in each volume; when a single cascade dominates a pixel or a device, §7's single-particle picture replaces it.

Hot pixels and random telegraph signals

The mean hides the feature that matters in an image. Displacement damage arrives as discrete events, a few of them large, so the dark current varies strongly from pixel to pixel. Pixels that received a nuclear reaction, or whose defect sits in a region of high electric field — about 10⁵ V/cm, where the field lowers the barrier for emission and multiplies the generation rate — become hot pixels, dark-current spikes an order of magnitude or more above the mean. Field-enhanced pixels show a lower activation energy, as low as about 0.4 eV against 0.63–0.65 eV for ordinary generation, so they fade less on cooling and dominate cold images (Hopkinson and Mohammadzadeh). The displacement damage dose sets the mean; the shape of the distribution depends on the recoil spectrum and on the field inside each pixel.

The shape follows from counting statistics. Goiffon models the number of damaging interactions in a pixel as Poisson-distributed, with a mean proportional to DDD × Vdep, and the dark current each contributes as exponentially distributed; a pixel's dark current is the sum. At low fluence most pixels receive no interaction or one, and the damaged pixels form an exponential tail above the undamaged population. As the number of interactions per pixel grows, the sum of many independent contributions approaches a Gaussian by the central limit theorem, and its width relative to its mean falls as the inverse square root of the number of events. Deck 03 p. 45 shows the approach, from Marshall and Marshall (after Marshall et al. and Dale et al.): dark-current histograms of a 256 × 256 charge-injection device after 12 MeV protons at 4×10¹⁰, 1×10¹¹ and 2×10¹¹ cm⁻², with N = 1967, 4918 and 9835 primary interactions per pixel and Ni = 1.8, 4.5 and 9.0 inelastic interactions per pixel. The thousands of elastic interactions add a nearly fixed amount to every pixel; the skew comes from the few inelastic reactions, each of which deposits a large damage energy in one pixel. The high-energy tail is conspicuous at the lowest fluence, and by the highest the distribution is nearly symmetric — the tail is the low-fluence signature of single-particle damage, not a feature that grows with exposure. Marshall and Marshall add that this nonuniformity is inherent in the statistics of the collisions and cannot be hardened against.

Between the lowest and highest fluence of Deck 03 p. 45 the fluence rises five-fold. By roughly what factor should the width of the dark-current distribution, relative to its peak position, fall, and does the slide bear it out?

The mean damage energy per pixel is proportional to the number of interactions, and so to fluence. For a sum of independent events whose number is Poisson-distributed, the variance is also proportional to that number, so the width grows only as its square root, and the width relative to the mean falls as one over the square root of the fluence: by √5 ≈ 2.2. Read from the slide, the full width at half maximum divided by the peak position is about 0.47 at 4×10¹⁰ cm⁻² and about 0.21 at 2×10¹¹ cm⁻², a factor of about 2.2.

The shape of the distribution also separates the two cumulative effects in an imager. Total dose raises the dark current of every pixel through interface traps at much the same rate, shifting and broadening the whole distribution as a pedestal; displacement damage produces the high-side tail of hot pixels (M08 §9).

Some damaged pixels do not hold a steady dark current. Their generation rate switches between two or more discrete levels at random times, a random telegraph signal, so a dark frame taken for calibration no longer predicts the next exposure. Hopkinson and Mohammadzadeh report it in all proton-irradiated CCDs and active pixel sensors examined, over proton energies from at least 1.5 to 60 MeV, with switching times near room temperature from seconds to hours, mostly of order minutes, lengthening on cooling. In a CCD the probability of such a defect is about 1.6×10⁻⁵ per incident 10 MeV proton, so after 10¹⁰ protons/cm² about 40% of 20 µm × 20 µm pixels show it. Goiffon adds that displacement damage is more effective than ionization at producing high-amplitude, multilevel switching. The defects responsible are bistable or metastable, and Hopkinson and Mohammadzadeh note that they had not been identified.

A pixel's depleted volume is halved in a new sensor design, and the sensor is flown at twice the displacement damage dose of the old one. How do the mean dark-current increase and its shot noise change, and why might the worst pixels change differently?

By (12b.6) the mean increase is proportional to dose times volume: 2 × ½ = 1, so the mean increase is unchanged, and so is its shot noise, which depends only on the mean through (12b.5). The worst pixels follow different statistics. The number of damaging events in a pixel is Poisson-distributed with a mean proportional to dose times volume, so that mean is also unchanged; but the halved volume packs a given cluster into a smaller region, and if the new design raises the field in the depletion region, field-enhanced emission makes the hot pixels hotter. A tail set by inelastic reactions keeps its shape only if the event count and the field are unchanged — the mean alone cannot show it.

4 · Imagers and solar cells

Imaging arrays and solar cells are the devices in which displacement damage most often sets the useful life. An imager reads out charge packets from millions of nominally identical pixels, so a single generation centre in one pixel, or a single trap in a transfer channel, is visible in the image. A solar cell converts light into current only through minority carriers that survive a diffusion path to its junction (the displacement-damage-dose treatment of space arrays is sister course EEL5385 M20 §4). Both depend on the silicon or GaAs bulk. Solar cells sit behind only a thin cover glass. Imagers in space are often heavily shielded, which shifts the protons that reach them to energies of typically 40–100 MeV (Marshall and Marshall), but they are sensitive enough that the protons that do get through still set their useful life. Each process of M12 §3 appears here in a specific device symptom.

Charge transfer in a CCD

M06 §9 introduced the charge-coupled device: signal charge held in potential wells under clocked gates and moved packet by packet along a buried channel to one output amplifier, with the charge transfer inefficiency CTI = 1 − CTE the fraction left behind at each transfer, compounded along the chain by (6.7). Displacement damage degrades a CCD in four ways: it lowers the CTE, raises the average dark current, creates individual pixels of very high dark current, and increases the noise of the output amplifier (Marshall and Marshall). Of these, CTI is the one most directly tied to displacement damage. Total dose acts on a CCD through its gate dielectrics: it shifts flat-band voltages and raises surface dark current through interface traps (M08 §9; Hopkinson and Mohammadzadeh §2). The traps that capture signal charge in the buried channel are bulk defects, which only displacement damage creates. The dominant such trap at moderately high operating temperatures has a level about 0.44 eV below the conduction band and is attributed to the E centre, on its energy and its annealing behaviour; it forms because the usual buried channel is n-type, doped with phosphorus (Hopkinson and Mohammadzadeh). The two dark-current effects, which displacement damage also produces directly, are those of §3.

A trap degrades transfer through a race between two time constants. Its capture time, τc = 1/(σvthns), with ns the local density of signal electrons, is shortest at the dense centre of a packet and depends only weakly on temperature. Its emission time grows as exp(Et/kT), with Et the depth of the level below the conduction band, and so depends strongly on temperature. Charge is lost from a packet when a trap captures within the packet's dwell time in the pixel and re-emits only after the packet has moved on; the released electrons join a later packet. The image effect is trailing: charge smeared behind bright features along the transfer direction, seen as streaks. Because capture needs the packet's own electrons, a small signal loses a larger fraction than a large one; because emission is thermally activated, the temperature and the clock rate decide which traps matter. A linear CCD clocked at 1 MHz or faster is relatively immune, since the E centre's capture is too slow for the short dwell time (Marshall and Marshall).

The tolerances are severe. Keeping signal loss below 10% over 1000 transfers requires a CTE of at least 0.9999 per transfer, which for a pixel of about 50 µm³ means fewer than one radiation-induced defect in every ten pixels (Marshall and Marshall), and CTI becomes a major effect at equivalent 10 MeV proton fluences of about 10¹⁰ cm⁻² (Hopkinson and Mohammadzadeh). Deck 03 p. 44 shows the dependence on signal size and on background for an EEV CCD02 after 7.2×10⁹ protons/cm² at 10 MeV, a fluence corresponding to about 4 krad(Si). With a dark-charge background of 150 electrons the CTI approaches 5×10⁻⁴ for the smallest signals and falls as the signal grows; a background of 16,000 electrons holds it near 7×10⁻⁵ at every signal size, because the background keeps the traps filled and they cannot interact with the signal packet. The improvement usually comes at the price of added noise, the background's own shot noise. Marshall and Marshall compare the two cumulative effects in scientific CCDs: flat-band shifts and dark-current increases at total doses below 10–20 krad(Si) are often manageable by adjusting voltages and temperature, while significant CTE loss appears at proton exposures of less than 1 krad(Si).

The countermeasures follow from the trap physics. Cooling, often to about −80 °C, lengthens the E centre's emission time until filled traps stay filled; a deliberately raised background charge in every pixel, called a fat zero, or a charge packet injected ahead of the signal, fills them electrically; a narrow implanted notch confines small packets to a smaller volume and so to fewer traps; a p-channel CCD, whose boron-doped channel contains no E centres, is harder; and an active pixel sensor, which reads each pixel in place instead of transferring charge along a register, does not compound a per-transfer loss over thousands of transfers (Marshall and Marshall; Hopkinson and Mohammadzadeh). A 4T pinned-photodiode pixel still makes one in-pixel transfer, which bulk traps seldom affect but total dose can degrade at high dose (Goiffon 2021). Where none of these suffices the remaining option is shielding. Behind a thick tantalum shield, the secondary neutrons produced in the shield itself come to dominate the displacement damage dose beyond about 2 cm (Marshall and Marshall).

Testing an imager has to keep the two cumulative effects apart. Hopkinson and Mohammadzadeh, in the course textbook, state the reason: proton beams are not good simulators for total-dose damage, and when dark current is tested the two damage components need to be separated carefully, with the ionization component tested using cobalt-60; in their account total dose does not affect CTE. Later work qualifies that last statement. The 2024 and 2021 NSREC Short Courses (Virmontois; Goiffon) describe total dose degrading charge transfer where the transferred charge is in contact with an oxide interface, as under a transfer gate: burying the transfer away from the interface is the mitigation (Virmontois), and in pinned-photodiode CMOS pixels the transfer-gate spacer region is where it fails, near 1 Mrad (Goiffon). The course follows the textbook's division — CTI is the effect most directly tied to displacement damage — and notes that a CTE change after a cobalt-60 test is not proof of displacement damage.

The proton hits in the SOHO images are ionization, not displacement damage. Deck 03 p. 43 appears among the displacement-damage slides, under the statement that protons can damage sensitive CCDs, and shows coronagraph images from the LASCO instrument on the SOHO spacecraft taken during the coronal mass ejection of 6 November 1997; protons from the event reach the CCD and pepper the lower two images. Those specks are single-event transients. Each proton generates electron–hole pairs along its track, the pixels it crosses collect that charge as though it were signal, and the event appears in the frame being integrated as a bright pixel or, for an oblique track, a short streak; when the charge exceeds a pixel's full-well capacity it spills along the column, which is blooming. The next readout clears it. Hopkinson and Mohammadzadeh describe such events as short-lived and confined to the exposure in which they arrive (M06 §9, M08 §9, and M11 §§1–2 for charge deposition and collection). The same protons do deposit displacement damage, but it appears as hot pixels that persist from frame to frame and as a gradual loss of transfer efficiency, not as the scattered specks of a single frame.

Solar cells

A solar cell collects the minority carriers that light generates within about a diffusion length L = (Dτ)1/2 of its junction; carriers generated deeper recombine before they reach it. Displacement damage shortens τ, the diffusion length falls as 1/L² = 1/L0² + Φ/(DKτ) by M12 (12.6), and the short-circuit current, and with it the output power, falls with fluence (IEEE NSREC 2024 Short Course; Deck 02 p. 24 for Jsc in GaAs cells). Solar arrays are the least shielded electronics on a spacecraft, behind only a thin cover glass, so they see low-energy protons that heavier shielding would stop, and energetic electrons as well: Johnston notes that the cover glass is ineffective against energetic electrons, which therefore damage solar cells along with protons.

The NIEL correlation for GaAs cells (M12 §4) is what makes displacement damage dose a workable basis for predicting their degradation. The proton spectrum reduces to one dose by M12 (12.1), the electron spectrum to one equivalent 1 MeV electron dose computed with NIEL raised to the power 1.7 (the correction in the box after M12 Figure 7), and measured characteristic curves of power against dose convert each dose into a power loss (Marshall and Marshall, after Walters et al., 1999). The 2024 NSREC Short Course cites Messenger et al. (2001) for the dose method, a study that compares it with the alternative of converting the environment into an equivalent fluence of 1 MeV electrons. At high electron fluences a second mechanism appears: carrier removal, process 4 of M12 §3, raises the base resistivity and widens the depletion layer, and Johnston, after Yamaguchi et al. (1999), shows that in silicon cells it can cause a catastrophic failure that models based on lifetime alone do not predict.

Other minority-carrier optoelectronics follow the same logic, with one extreme case. Most light-emitting diodes and laser diodes are relatively hard, because the carrier lifetime in their active regions is already very short (Marshall and Marshall). Amphoterically doped AlGaAs light-emitting diodes, used in optocouplers, are the exception: Johnston reports about half the light output lost after about 2×10¹⁰ protons/cm² at 50 MeV, a fluence equivalent to about 3 krad(Si), with the loss due entirely to displacement damage.

A silicon PIN photodiode of the kind M04 §8 uses for X-ray dosimetry, when it is exposed to protons, neutrons or energetic electrons, is damaged through the processes of this module; the 10 keV photons of M04 §8 cannot displace silicon atoms, and there its degradation is the total-dose leakage below. Its thick intrinsic region is a large depleted volume, so its dark current rises in proportion to displacement damage dose times that volume, by (12b.6); the lifetime of carriers collected by diffusion from the undepleted regions falls by M12 (12.6), so the response to light or particles absorbed outside the depletion region falls; and each particle that crosses it produces a transient pulse, superposed on the photocurrent. Total dose adds surface leakage at the passivating oxide. The dark-current rise sets the noise floor against which the photocurrent is read.

A cooled CCD and an active pixel sensor of the same pixel size fly side by side. After a solar particle event, which shows the larger loss of faint-object detection from displacement damage, and which mitigation applies to only one of them?

Both gain dark current and hot pixels in proportion to displacement damage dose times depleted volume, and both lose faint objects as the dark-current shot noise rises. The CCD has a second loss the APS does not: charge transfer inefficiency. Its signal crosses hundreds to thousands of pixels to reach one output amplifier, and each bulk trap along the way removes a fraction, compounded as (1 − CTI)N; a faint packet loses the largest fraction, because capture needs the packet's own electrons and a small packet fills fewer traps. So the CCD loses faint objects first. A fat zero or injected charge, which keeps the traps filled, helps only the CCD, since the APS reads each pixel in place and has no transfer register to protect. Cooling helps both: it slows generation, lowering the dark current, and in the CCD it lengthens the trap emission time so that filled traps stay filled.

5 · Modelling displacement damage

Prediction is needed wherever measurement cannot reach: a mission environment no beam reproduces, a device not yet built, a distribution whose tail a small test sample does not populate. Deck 15 pp. 23–26 present the tools — particle transport and recoil production, device response, mission-level degradation, and atomistic damage evolution — and the figure below puts them in the order a calculation runs, with the atomistic step between transport and device simulation. Each answers a different question. None predicts a device parameter from first principles alone; each passes its result to the next and is checked against a measurement.

SRIM follows ions through a target by Monte Carlo, treating each collision with a single atom as an isolated two-body event — the binary collision approximation (BCA) — and reports, against depth, the energy given to ionization and the vacancies produced by the ion and by its recoils. Deck 15 p. 23, after Messenger et al. (1999), shows the output for 20,000 histories of 10 MeV alpha particles in GaAs with 10 eV displacement thresholds: the ionization rate rises by less than a factor of three over the first 46 µm, while vacancy production by the ions and by their recoils rises toward the end of the range, near 48 µm, where the slowed ion's nuclear stopping is largest. The sister course's EEL5385 M13 sets out what TRIM assumes: an amorphous target, every ion entering undamaged material, and 0 K, so that nothing diffuses or anneals. SRIM therefore counts displacements before recombination and annealing, and says nothing electrical.

Geant4, from CERN, and MRED, Vanderbilt's Geant4-based package, provide Monte Carlo simulation of non-ionizing energy deposition, also in the binary collision approximation (Deck 15 p. 24), but with the full set of nuclear reactions and secondary particles, in a device's own geometry, event by event. Event-by-event output is what a distribution needs. Deck 15 p. 24 compares, after Marshall et al. (2007), the distribution of damage energy per pixel simulated with MRED and the measured distribution of pixel dark current in an irradiated detector array; the measured histogram extends further on both sides, and its high-current tail reaches well beyond the simulated damage energy. The same slide, after Jay et al. (2017), compares the number of secondary knock-on atoms per event from Geant4 and from molecular dynamics: the Geant4 mean is 16.89 over 10,000 events and the molecular dynamics mean 13.00 over 100, so the binary collision count runs about 30% above the atomistic one. (The slide's caption attributes the histogram to MRED; the plotted legends are MD-TTM and Geant4.)

TCAD solves the drift–diffusion and SRH equations of M06 on a meshed device. Displacement damage enters only as an input: a reduced lifetime, or defect levels with their densities and cross-sections, placed where the modeller puts them. The lecture stressed that point: the user must specify where each defect is and at what energy, so the method is close to curve fitting against measured data. Deck 15 p. 25 shows a successful fit, after Petrosyants, Kozhukhov and Popov (2018): the current gain of a silicon bipolar transistor against base–emitter voltage, before irradiation and after 4×10¹³ n/cm², simulated and measured, with the simulated peak gain falling from about 66 to about 37 and the measured from about 71 to about 38 (read from the slide).

SCREAM, the code of the NRL team in ESA's SPENVIS environment system, and JPL's equivalent fluence method carry device data to a mission. Both start from measured degradation curves and a mission spectrum behind shielding; SCREAM reduces the spectrum to a displacement damage dose (the method of M12 §4), and the JPL method to an equivalent fluence of 1 MeV electrons. Deck 15 p. 25 compares them for GaAs/Ge solar cells in a 700 × 12,050 km, 63.4° orbit for one year, an environment dominated by protons: the remaining fraction of maximum power and open-circuit voltage predicted by the two methods against coverglass thickness agree within a few per cent for covers of 6 mils and thicker, both rising toward 1 at 30 mils; below 6 mils the predicted maximum power diverges, about 0.41 (NRL) against about 0.23 (JPL) at 3 mils (read from the slide).

Monte Carlo plus molecular dynamics is the current research route to the missing link, from a recoil to a defect with a known level. Deck 15 p. 26, after Raine et al. (2017), lays out the steps for a neutron in silicon. Monte Carlo transport in the binary collision approximation gives the primary knock-on atom's energy and species. Molecular dynamics follows the displacement cascade atom by atom over picoseconds to nanoseconds. The kinetic activation–relaxation technique (k-ART) carries the damaged structure through annealing to a stable configuration over milliseconds to seconds. Ab initio calculation then gives the stable defect's energy levels in the gap, which make it a generation centre, and the result is compared with measured dark current. The 2024 NSREC Short Course adds that k-ART can identify several energy levels that a defect exhibits as it stabilizes. The lecture's assessment was that making the whole route agree with experiment is still an open problem.

The tools divide along the line that matters in practice, between the mean and the distribution. A mean degradation — average dark current, average gain loss, average solar-cell power — follows from the displacement damage dose and one measured constant, the universal damage factor or a damage factor per particle, and the mission codes work at that level. The tail of a distribution — the hot pixels, the weakest bits — comes from individual events: a rare inelastic reaction that puts a large damage energy in one pixel, a cluster in a high-field region, a defect configuration with an unusually effective level. Only event-by-event transport, with the atomistic step to tell which events leave which defects, can predict it, and the comparison on Deck 15 p. 24 shows that damage energy alone does not.

A silicon image sensor shows a long tail of hot pixels after a proton exposure. Which tools would predict the tail, which measurement would decide whether field-enhanced emission or nuclear-reaction clusters cause it, and what result would favour each?

The mean follows from (12b.6) with the dose; the tail needs Geant4 or MRED to give the event-by-event distribution of damage energy per pixel, including the rare inelastic reactions, and a device model — TCAD with the pixel's field map — to turn a defect in a given place into a generation rate. The discriminating measurement is dark current against temperature for the tail pixels. Ordinary generation through a midgap level has an activation energy near 0.63–0.65 eV; field-enhanced emission lowers it, to about 0.4 eV in the worst pixels (§3). Tail pixels with low activation energies that change with the pixel's bias favour field enhancement. Tail pixels with normal activation energies, whose number per unit fluence matches the computed rate of inelastic reactions per pixel and which shrink in relative weight as the fluence rises, favour cluster damage from nuclear reactions. Annealing behaviour adds a third test, since different defects anneal at different temperatures (M12 §5).

Two practical rules follow. A damage factor measured at one bias does not transfer to another: a transistor characterized at high current and used at low current is degraded more than the characterization predicts. And a 1 MeV equivalent neutron fluence (ASTM E722 gives the procedure, from the neutron spectrum and silicon's displacement damage function; 2024 NSREC Short Course) says nothing about bias; a gain prediction made from it with the original relation carries the bias assumption. Low-current analog circuits, the ones in which M12 noted displacement damage remains a concern for bipolar parts, are where the correction matters most.

Two transistors receive the same proton fluence. Both start with β₀ = 100; one has a base ten times thinner than the other. At low collector current, which loses more gain, and why does the original Messenger–Spratt relation give the opposite answer?

The original relation keeps only the neutral-base term, proportional to WB², so it predicts a hundred times less damage for the thin-base transistor — the M12 rule that a narrow base, short transit time and high cutoff frequency degrade less. At low collector current the emitter–base depletion-region term dominates. It scales only as WDWB, tenfold less for the thin base rather than a hundredfold, and it grows as exp(−VEB/2VT) as the bias falls. If it already dominates the thick-base device at that bias, the thin-base device's advantage shrinks from a factor of 100 toward 10; and if the thin base is paired with heavier base doping, the NB/ni factor can make its depletion-region term the larger, so the thin-base transistor loses more gain. The answer depends on the bias, which is the point of the correction: the original relation has no bias in it.

7 · Single-particle displacement damage in nanoscale devices

Every result so far has treated displacement damage as a population: many defects in a volume large enough that their number, not their placement, sets the parameter. That description fails when the device is no larger than one cascade. A single recoil then either forms its cluster inside the sensitive region or it does not, and the damage becomes a single-particle event produced by a cumulative-effect mechanism — the edge case of the classification in M05 §6.

The scales come from M12 §2. A 50 keV silicon recoil spreads its displacements over about 80 nm and concentrates them in terminal clusters about 5 nm across. An imaging pixel of 13–20 µm is 160 to 250 times the length of the whole cascade, so one cascade changes one pixel's dark current by a discrete step within a statistical distribution — which is how single-particle displacement damage was first seen, in the dark current of charge-injection-device pixels each exposed, statistically, to a single particle (IEEE NSREC 2024 Short Course, after P. W. Marshall et al., 1989). Reverse-biased diodes have since shown step changes in leakage current that coincide with the charge transients of single heavy ions (Auden et al., 2012). In a transistor large compared with a cascade, one cluster is too small to move any terminal quantity. Deck 02 p. 25 draws what scaling changed: a nanoscale transistor in an integrated circuit is comparable in size to a subcascade.

At that size one cluster changes one device. The 2024 NSREC Short Course collects the evidence. Heavy-ion displacement damage reduces the drain current of 14 nm FinFETs; weak bits in heavy-ion-irradiated SRAMs built with 5 nm FinFETs correlate with displacement damage rather than with total dose; and simulations of FinFETs from the 22 nm down to the 6 nm node show that a damage cluster at the top of the fin can severely degrade the drain current. In memories the symptom is a bit that no longer behaves. Displacement damage increases the number of stuck bits in SDRAM, and in DRAM both total dose and single-particle displacement damage produce stuck bits and intermittently stuck bits (Goiffon et al., 2020). A DRAM cell holds its bit as charge on a capacitor between refreshes, so a defect cluster that raises the generation current of the storage junction drains the cell early — the generation process of M12 §3, which makes a hot pixel in an imager, acting on a storage node. Deck 02 p. 25 draws the practical conclusion: bad or stuck bits are likely, and error detection and correction (EDAC) is required to avoid or recover from them.

EDAC stores check bits with each data word and recomputes them on every read, so that an error in one bit of the word is detected and corrected. A stuck bit is corrected on every read, but it occupies the code's correction capacity for that word for as long as it persists, and a second error in the same word — a single-event upset, for example — then exceeds what a single-error-correcting code can repair. Error-correcting codes and the other circuit-level defences against single particles belong to the single-event hardening module on the course roadmap.

Deck 02 p. 25 also warns that single-particle displacement damage is often difficult to distinguish from micro-dose and from single-event effects. All three leave one cell permanently or persistently changed after one particle, so the symptom at the memory's terminals is the same. Micro-dose is the ionizing counterpart: an ion crossing the gate oxide of one small transistor deposits enough dose in that oxide alone to shift that transistor, a total-dose effect confined to one device. The stuck bits heavy ions produce in memories have long been attributed to it (E. Normand's chapter in the course textbook; IEEE NSREC 2024 Short Course, Part III). A destructive single event, such as the gate rupture of M11 §10, leaves a third kind of trace, a damaged structure. The three differ in where the trace sits, and the evidence that separates them is the evidence that identifies each kind of trace.

Micro-dose sits in an oxide: trapped positive charge in the gate or isolation oxide of one transistor, which shifts that transistor's threshold. Single-particle displacement damage sits in the silicon: a defect cluster in the fin's channel or junction, which reduces drive current or raises generation leakage. The evidence follows the location. Oxide charge shows as a threshold shift, with the off-state leakage that follows from it and no loss of drive at a given overdrive; a cluster shows as reduced drive at an unchanged threshold, or as leakage with the activation energy of bulk generation. Recovery separates them as well: oxide charge anneals on the time and temperature scales of M09 §5 and depends on the bias held during the anneal, while bulk clusters anneal on the scales of M12 §5. The 2024 NSREC Short Course reports weak bits in 5 nm FinFET SRAMs correlating with displacement damage rather than total dose (Part I), and stuck bits in memories attributed to both mechanisms (Part III), so neither hypothesis can be set aside without these tests; a single result does not settle it for another process.

8 · On-orbit prediction and qualification

Qualification turns the physics into a decision: will this part, in this application, stay within its requirement for this mission? Deck 15 pp. 28–31 lay out the procedure, and pp. 35–45 test it on cases. The procedure rests on NIEL scaling: measure the damage with one particle at one or a few energies, and use NIEL to scale it to the mission's spectrum. The cases show where that step is reliable and where it is not.

The mission's displacement damage dose generalizes M12 (12.1) from one energy to a spectrum. Deck 15 p. 29 writes it as the integral of NIEL against the differential spectrum (plotted there as a daily flux, so multiplied by the mission length), taken behind the shielding as p. 28's flowchart specifies; dividing by the NIEL of a test beam gives the fluence of that beam which delivers the same dose, Deck 15 p. 31's equivalent test fluence.

(12b.9)

Deck 15 pp. 29–30 fill in the laboratory branch. A device property changes linearly with proton fluence over its working range, with a different slope at each energy; the slope is the damage factor, the change in the property per unit fluence. For a bipolar transistor the property is the reciprocal gain, so the damage factor is Δ(1/hFE)/ΔΦ, with hFE = IC/IB, the Messenger–Spratt slope of M12. Measured at several proton energies, the damage factor falls with energy as NIEL does, which is the energy dependence the flowchart needs. The mission branch then gives the parameter against time in orbit, and the prediction is compared with the application's requirement: the time at which the parameter crosses it is the part's lifetime in that application. In pass/fail testing the comparison is made in the laboratory instead, by exposing parts to the equivalent fluence of (12b.9), usually with a margin, and checking them against the requirement. Page 29 lists three cautions: measurements must be application-specific, possible total-dose effects must be considered, and some device properties are nonlinear in fluence.

The JWST example: an environment dominated by solar protons

Optocouplers and op-amps: total dose and displacement damage together

An optocoupler hybrid contains an LED, a coupling medium, a detector and amplification circuitry, and Deck 15 pp. 42–43 list why it is hard to qualify. Its mode of degradation depends on the device: displacement damage reduces the light output of amphoterically doped LEDs, the responsivity of the detector and the gain of a phototransistor, while total dose acts concurrently and may dominate in couplers whose LEDs are hardened. Component traceability and device-to-device variation are poor, so the slide calls for proton testing of the flight lot with good statistics. The figure of merit is the current transfer ratio, CTR, the detector's collector current divided by the LED's drive current (the slide prints it as "charge transfer ratio"). Deck 15 p. 44, after Reed et al. (1998), shows the result for a 4N49 coupler at IF = 1.1 mA and VCE = 5 V: under 195 MeV protons the normalized CTR falls to about 0.2 by about 6 krad(Si), while under cobalt-60 it is still about 0.46 at about 25 krad(Si) and reaches about 0.07 only at 100 krad(Si). Plotted against total dose, the proton curve falls far faster because the protons' displacement damage, which cobalt-60 delivers only in very small measure (through its Compton electrons), does most of the work. (The slide is titled "Op Amp"; it shows an optocoupler.)

Deck 15 p. 45, after Rax et al. (1998), shows the same effect in a linear integrated circuit: the maximum output sink current of an op-amp falls with total dose under both 52 MeV protons and cobalt-60, and the proton-irradiated part fails its −1.0 mA specification at 60% of the dose the cobalt-60 result would allow. A total-dose test with cobalt-60 alone would therefore have passed a part that fails in a proton environment — the same lesson as M12 §3's LM137 regulator, and the reason a bipolar linear circuit for a proton-rich orbit is qualified with protons as well as with the total-dose methods of M15.

A displacement-damage test plan

The cases fix what a test plan must control. Each item answers a way in which the measured damage could differ from the mission's.

  • Particle and energy. Choose the test beam against the shielded mission spectrum, and compute the fluence by (12b.9) with a margin. Where NIEL equivalence is doubtful — GaAs and other compound devices at high energy, electrons in p-type silicon, low-energy protons in n-type silicon — test at more than one energy, or at energies representative of the mission.
  • Fluence steps. Expose in several steps, measuring between them, because some properties are nonlinear in fluence and the damage factor is the slope of the linear part only.
  • Bias. Injection anneals amphoterically doped LEDs and laser diodes, so a part irradiated or measured under forward current shows less damage than one left unbiased (Johnston): irradiate unbiased, the worst case, unless the application drives the part continuously; measure with short pulses at a low duty cycle; and record the charge passed through the device between exposure and measurement, on which its recovery depends. Double-heterojunction LEDs are relatively insensitive to this annealing. Measure at the application's operating point, since the small-transistor correction of §6 makes gain loss bias-dependent.
  • Temperature. Control the temperature of irradiation and of measurement. Generation current is thermally activated, and the universal damage factor is defined at about 300 K.
  • Annealing. Record the time between exposure and measurement, and include a post-irradiation anneal or a defined wait, so that short-term annealing does not inflate the result (M12 §5).
  • Separation of total dose. Protons deliver ionizing and displacement damage together. The two interact rather than add (Pease). A companion set irradiated with cobalt-60 to the same total dose shows how much of the proton result is ionization; a laboratory simulation of the combined environment with separate sources must put both exposures, consecutively, on the same samples.
  • Samples and lot. Test several parts from the flight lot, with unirradiated controls, because device-to-device variation (hybrids especially) can be large (Deck 15 p. 42).
  • Application-specific limits. Measure the parameters the circuit uses, and judge them against the application's requirement, not the datasheet's.
  • Distributions as well as means. For imagers and memories, record the hot-pixel or weak-bit distribution, since NIEL and the universal damage factor predict only the mean (§§3, 5, 7).

Deck 15 p. 46 closes with the point that frames all of this: accurate on-orbit lifetime prediction requires coupling total ionizing dose, displacement damage and single-event effects, because a real orbit delivers all three to the same part at once. Total-dose assurance is the subject of M15, and single-event assurance, with its zero-event bound on the cross-section, of M16 §6.

A team proposes to qualify an AlGaAs-LED optocoupler for a low Earth orbit by a cobalt-60 test to twice the mission's total dose, followed by a 200 MeV proton exposure to the NIEL-computed equivalent fluence. Identify two ways this plan can pass a part that fails in orbit, and how to fix each.

First, the cobalt-60 test delivers almost no displacement damage, and in an amphoterically doped LED displacement damage is the dominant mechanism: the 4N49 data of Deck 15 p. 44 show the current transfer ratio falling to about 0.2 under protons by about 6 krad(Si), a level the cobalt-60 curve reaches only near 50 krad(Si). Passing a cobalt-60 test says nothing about the LED's lifetime loss. Second, the proton fluence is computed with NIEL at 200 MeV, where measured GaAs damage falls well below NIEL; Deck 15 p. 41 shows NIEL-based equivalent fluences several times too low at that energy, so the part is under-tested. Fixes: compute the equivalent fluence from measured damage factors, or test at a lower, mission-representative energy where damage and NIEL agree; expose the flight lot, several parts with controls, in fluence steps, unbiased (forward current anneals amphoterically doped LEDs, so a biased test under-states the damage), with low-duty-cycle pulsed measurements at the application's temperature; and since total dose and displacement damage interact, let the protons deliver both, or put the cobalt-60 and the displacement exposure on the same parts in sequence, rather than treating the cobalt-60 result as a separate pass.

Where this leaves us

The defect physics of M12 now reaches the bench and the mission. Each device shows the process its operation depends on: diodes and pixels show generation, bipolar transistors, solar cells and LEDs show recombination, CCDs show trapping, and majority-carrier devices change only once carrier removal and mobility loss set in. Each measurement reads one link of the chain: I–V the terminal change, C–V the doping and carrier removal, DLTS and DLOS the defect's depth and concentration, photoconductive decay the lifetime. Generation current rises as the generation lifetime falls, and its shot noise rises as the square root of the current, which is why a damaged imager loses faint objects first; the universal damage factor predicts the mean dark current from the dose, and the tail of hot pixels comes from single events.

The modelling chain runs from ion transport through atomistic damage evolution and device simulation to mission codes, and only its event-by-event links can predict a distribution. Small bipolar transistors add a bias-dependent recombination term to Messenger–Spratt that the original relation omits, and nanoscale devices reach the limit in which one cascade is one device. Qualification combines a measured damage curve with the mission dose through NIEL; the GaAs LED case shows where that equivalence under-tests a part, the optocoupler and op-amp cases show a cobalt-60 test missing the displacement damage that protons add, and together they show why protons, flight-lot statistics, bias, temperature, annealing time and a separate total-dose test all belong in the plan.

The next built module, M15, turns to total-dose hardness assurance: how parts are sampled and tested, and why the MOS test method fails for linear bipolar parts. The circuit-hardening modules on the course roadmap take up error correction and the other defences against single particles, and A1 §5 places the displacement-damage literature in the history of the field.
