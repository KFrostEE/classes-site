Module 12

Displacement Damage: Physics and NIEL

How the stopping of a particle divides into electronic and nuclear parts, how the kinematics of a single collision set the energy of the atomic recoils, how the recoil energy decides whether the damage is an isolated Frenkel pair, a cluster or a branching set of subcascades, what the surviving defects do to carrier lifetime, mobility, carrier concentration and bipolar gain, where the NIEL correlation holds and where it fails, and how the damage anneals.

Prerequisites M04, M05, M06 Read time ~55 min Sources Deck 15 pp. 5–15, 18, 20, 28; Deck 03 pp. 34–41; Deck 02 pp. 23–24; Deck 04 p. 35; lectures of 26 and 28 Oct; course textbook; Johnston (2010); NSREC short courses 1999, 2024

M04 §6 introduced the non-ionizing channel. A small share of the energy a particle deposits moves nuclei rather than electrons; an atom given more than 21 eV in silicon leaves its lattice site as a primary knock-on atom; about 90% of the vacancy–interstitial pairs created recombine, and the surviving 10% form stable defects whose accumulated measure is the displacement damage dose, fluence times NIEL. M05 §3 placed the effect in the classification: cumulative, non-ionizing, and located in the semiconductor bulk rather than in any insulator. This module opens the quantities those pages took as given. It asks what sets the energy of a recoil, what arrangement of damage a recoil of given energy leaves behind, and what the surviving defects do to the carriers of a semiconductor.

Displacement damage is decided in two steps. The incident particle decides the recoil spectrum through the kinematics of the collision, and the recoil spectrum decides the spatial structure of the damage — isolated point defects, a single cluster, or a tree of subcascades (§§1–2). The surviving defects then act through their energy levels in the bandgap: they generate, recombine, trap and remove carriers, and they scatter the carriers that remain, so lifetime, mobility, carrier concentration and bipolar gain all change (§3). NIEL compresses the first step into one number per particle, energy and target, and it succeeds to the extent that a device responds to the number of displacements rather than to their arrangement; §4 is about where that assumption fails. §5 follows the damage in time. The companion module, M12b, takes the same physics into the laboratory and the mission: how the damage is measured, how it is modelled, what it does to imagers, solar cells and nanoscale devices, and how a part is qualified against it.

One contrast runs through both modules. Total ionizing dose acts in the oxides and at the oxide–silicon interface (M07, M08). Displacement damage acts in the crystal itself — the base of a bipolar transistor, the buried channel of a charge-coupled device, the absorber of a solar cell — and nothing done to an oxide changes it. The devices it limits are therefore the ones whose operation depends on the silicon bulk: on minority carriers surviving a diffusion path, or on a depleted volume staying free of generation centres.

1 · Collision kinematics and the recoil spectrum

The first event in displacement damage is a two-body collision between the incident particle and a lattice nucleus. Its outcome is the kinetic energy T handed to the struck atom, and the distribution of T over many collisions — the recoil, or primary knock-on atom (PKA), spectrum — is set by the particle's mass, its charge and its energy. That spectrum is the input to NIEL and to everything that follows in this module. Deck 03 pp. 35–36 make the point in one line: the energy of the recoils is determined by the collision kinematics.

The collision is one of several ways a particle loses energy, and Deck 15 p. 5 lists them for an ion, after R. Reed, in two families. The first is stopping, the gradual loss along the path, and two of its entries matter here. Electronic stopping is the ion's interaction with the target's electrons — excitation and ionization of the target, excitation and ionization of the projectile itself, capture of electrons by the projectile — and produces the electron–hole pairs of total dose and single events. Nuclear stopping is the Coulomb recoil of target atoms: the ion's charge interacts with the screened charge of a nucleus and sets the whole atom moving. Electromagnetic radiation and chemical reactions complete the stopping list. The second family is nuclear reaction through the nuclear force — elastic scattering, inelastic reactions and fission — which is rare per unit path but transfers large energies. Nuclear stopping and the nuclear reactions are the two routes to a recoiling atom, and only a recoiling atom displaces the lattice; electronic stopping, however large, displaces nothing directly. The recoil then loses its own energy by the same two stopping processes, which is how one recoil produces others (§2).

Deck 15 p. 10 puts the two loss rates side by side for protons in silicon, after Dale et al. (1994): the ionizing loss rate, the LET, and the non-ionizing loss rate, NIEL, both in MeV·cm²/g. Across 1–1000 MeV the two curves sit three orders of magnitude or more apart, and both fall with energy. The same slide writes the two accumulated quantities in parallel: total ionizing dose is k · LET · fluence, in rad, with k the conversion from MeV/g, and displacement damage dose is NIEL · fluence, in MeV/g. Keeping the non-ionizing part as a quantity of its own is what separates displacement damage from total dose and single-event effects, which both follow from the ionizing part.

M04 §6 wrote the accumulated quantity as a fluence multiplied by the non-ionizing energy loss, the non-ionizing counterpart of dose written as fluence times LET.

Deck 03 p. 40 writes the definition with a prefactor 1/ρ, the inverse target density, and labels dσ/dT the differential scattering cross-section. The upper limit follows from conservation of energy and momentum. A particle of mass m and kinetic energy E transfers the most energy to a stationary nucleus of mass M in a head-on collision, in which the nucleus recoils along the particle's original direction. For an electron, whose kinetic energy in this subject is comparable with or larger than its rest energy, the relativistic form is required.

Tmax = 4mM(m + M)2 E  (proton, neutron),    Tmax = 2E(E + 2mec2)Mc2  (electron)
(12.3)

The first form is non-relativistic and holds for a proton to within about 5% up to 100 MeV; the second assumes M ≫ me and holds at any electron energy. Elastic collisions only; a nuclear reaction releases fragments whose energies these expressions do not bound. On the formula sheet as (12.3).

The mass factor is why an electron produces so little damage per particle. A 1 MeV neutron can give a silicon atom up to 133 keV and a 10 MeV proton up to 1.33 MeV. A 1 MeV electron, about 51,000 times lighter than the atom, can give it at most 155 eV — seven times the threshold, enough for one or a few displacements but not for a cascade — and below about 0.22 MeV it cannot displace a silicon atom at all. Electrons therefore produce only isolated point defects, and so do cobalt-60 gamma rays, which cause displacements only through the Compton electrons they liberate, of at most about 1 MeV. The largest recoil a 5 MeV proton and a 2 MeV electron can make in silicon

Given a 5 MeV proton and a 2 MeV electron, each striking a stationary silicon atom (28.09 u, Mc² = 26,166 MeV); proton mass 1.0073 u; mec² = 0.511 MeV; displacement threshold Td = 21 eV. Find each particle's largest recoil energy, the proton's mean Coulomb recoil, and whether either can start a cascade (recoil above about 1–2 keV, §2).

Plan. Two-body kinematics (12.3) bounds the recoil; the proton is non-relativistic at 5 MeV, the electron is not. The mean of a 1/T² spectrum is Td ln(Tmax/Td), so the upper limit enters only through a logarithm. Watch the units: Tmax comes out in MeV and the threshold is in eV.

Mass factor for the proton. 4mM/(m + M)² = 4 × 1.0073 × 28.09 / (29.097)² = 113.2 / 846.7 = 0.1337. The textbook figure of 0.133 quoted with (12.3) is this value truncated.

Proton limit. Tmax = 0.1337 × 5 MeV = 0.668 MeV = 668 keV — far above the cascade boundary.

Proton mean recoil. Td ln(Tmax/Td) = 21 eV × ln(668,000/21) = 21 eV × 10.37 = 218 eV. Why this matters: the limit allows a cascade, but the typical recoil is five to ten times smaller than the lower cascade boundary, so most of the proton's collisions leave isolated Frenkel pairs.

Electron limit, relativistic form. Tmax = 2E(E + 2mec²)/(Mc²) = 2 × 2 × (2 + 1.022) / 26,166 MeV = 12.09/26,166 MeV = 4.62×10⁻⁴ MeV = 462 eV. Why the relativistic form: the electron's kinetic energy is four times its rest energy, and the non-relativistic factor would understate its momentum.

Compare with the thresholds. 462 eV is 22 times Td, enough for a recoil that displaces a few further atoms by (12.4), but below the 1–2 keV lower cascade boundary.

Proton: Tmax = 668 keV, mean recoil ≈ 218 eV. Electron: Tmax = 462 eV. Only the proton can start a cascade, and it rarely does. Check: the electron's limit scales as E(E + 1.022) — at 1 MeV it gives 155 eV, the value in the text, and it reaches 21 eV at about 0.22 MeV, the electron threshold.

The upper limit bounds the spectrum; the cross-section fills it in, and here charged and uncharged particles differ in kind. A proton interacts with a nucleus through two forces. The Coulomb force between the two nuclear charges has long range, so a proton passing a nucleus at any distance is deflected slightly, and distant passes, which transfer little energy, far outnumber close ones. The cross-section for Coulomb (Rutherford) scattering to transfer energy T falls as 1/T², and the recoil spectrum is crowded toward the threshold. A neutron carries no charge and interacts only through the short-range nuclear force, in a close encounter or not at all. At low neutron energies these encounters approximate hard-sphere collisions, which spread the recoil energies uniformly from zero to Tmax: a flat spectrum. Deck 03 p. 35 draws the two shapes, a falling spectrum for low-energy protons and a flat one for neutrons, and assigns the first to isolated point defects and the second to cascades and multiple cascades.

The falling spectrum has a characteristic recoil energy that barely depends on the proton energy. For a 1/T² spectrum between Td and Tmax ≫ Td, the mean recoil energy is Td ln(Tmax/Td): about 230 eV for a 10 MeV proton in silicon and still under 300 eV at 100 MeV, since the upper limit enters only through a logarithm. Hopkinson and Mohammadzadeh, in the course textbook, quote an average proton-induced recoil of about 200 eV over a wide range of proton energies, and conclude that proton elastic collisions tend to produce isolated defects.

Counting recoils and counting energy give different pictures, and NIEL counts energy. Weighted by T, the 1/T² spectrum puts equal energy into every decade of recoil energy between threshold and Tmax, so the rare energetic Coulomb recoils carry as much transferred energy per decade as the numerous near-threshold ones. The flat neutron spectrum weighted the same way puts nearly all of its energy into the top decade below Tmax.

Two further channels open as the proton energy rises. Nuclear elastic scattering — the proton deflected by the nuclear force rather than by the Coulomb field — gives recoils whose spectrum resembles a neutron's, and nuclear reactions (inelastic collisions, including spallation, in which the struck nucleus is excited or broken up) release recoiling fragments with energies far above the elastic ones. Deck 03 p. 35 places the division for silicon: below about 10 MeV Coulomb scattering dominates and the recoils are of low energy; above about 20 MeV inelastic collisions tend to dominate. The slide marks the Coulomb regime as extending to higher proton energy in GaAs. A heavier nucleus is consistent with that on two counts. The Rutherford cross-section per target atom, at a given proton energy, scales as Z²/M, the square of the nuclear charge over the nuclear mass; for gallium (Z = 31) and arsenic (Z = 33) that is about twice silicon's (Z = 14). And the Coulomb barrier — the electrostatic repulsion a proton must overcome to come within range of the nuclear force — rises with the nuclear charge, which holds off the nuclear channels to higher proton energy.

Nuclear events are rare, and each one is large. For 63 MeV protons in silicon the textbook chapter gives elastic and inelastic cross-sections of 318 and 0.52 barns. In a pixel volume of 900 µm³ exposed to 2×10¹⁰ protons/cm² that amounts to about 300 elastic collisions and 0.5 inelastic ones — yet each inelastic collision deposits roughly three orders of magnitude more damage energy than an elastic one. The count of events and the damage they carry are dominated by different collisions, which is why Deck 03 p. 36 concludes that the nature of displacement damage depends strongly on particle type and energy.

A 20 MeV neutron and a 20 MeV proton each pass through the same thin silicon layer. Which one produces more recoils, which one produces the more energetic recoils, and why does neither answer follow from the particles' LET?

The proton produces far more recoils. Its charge interacts with every nucleus it passes through the long-range Coulomb force, so it scatters on almost every nucleus near its path, mostly transferring little energy: the 1/T² spectrum, crowded near threshold. The neutron has no charge and interacts only through the short-range nuclear force, in close encounters, so it produces few recoils. Those few are the more energetic: elastic nuclear scattering gives recoils up to Tmax = 0.1337 × 20 MeV ≈ 2.7 MeV, far more of them energetic than the proton's Coulomb recoils (the flat hard-sphere spectrum is the low-energy limit of this case), and at 20 MeV inelastic reactions add recoiling fragments and secondary particles, so a large share of the neutron's damage comes as clusters and subcascades. LET counts the energy given to electrons. A neutron has no electronic stopping at all, so its LET is zero while its NIEL is not, and a proton's LET is set by electronic stopping, which displaces nothing directly. Displacement is decided by the nuclear channels of Deck 15 p. 5, which LET does not measure.

2 · From recoil to defect: point defects, clusters and subcascades

A recoil's energy decides how the damage it leaves is arranged in space, and the arrangement decides how much of it survives and what it does. Three regimes follow one another as the recoil energy rises. Their boundaries are recoil energies, and Deck 03 p. 36 maps them onto the proton energies at which the collision channels of §1 take over.

A recoiling silicon atom is itself an energetic ion inside the crystal, and it loses energy the way any ion does (M04 §1). The faster it moves, the larger the share of its energy that goes to electrons; as it slows, the share that goes into nuclear collisions grows, and its elastic scattering cross-section is largest over the last few keV of its path. The displacements a recoil causes are therefore concentrated toward the end of its path, which the sister course develops as the end-of-range effect (EEL5385 M11 §5). How far the damage spreads, and whether it breaks into separate pieces, depends on how much energy the recoil started with.

The elementary unit of the damage is the Frenkel pair: a vacancy, the empty lattice site the struck atom left, and an interstitial, the same atom lodged between lattice sites (Deck 15 p. 7, after Marshall and Marshall, 1999). Any particle that can give an atom more than the displacement threshold can make one, and Deck 15 p. 7 lists them: light and heavy ions, helium ions and alpha particles, protons and deuterons, neutrons, electrons, gamma rays and pions. Gamma rays displace atoms mainly indirectly, through the Compton electrons they liberate. A point defect is an isolated vacancy, interstitial or Frenkel pair, or the small complex it forms with a neighbouring impurity; a defect cluster is a dense region of many displacements made by one energetic recoil (Deck 15 p. 8).

  • Free defects, below about 1–2 keV. The recoil displaces its own atom and has too little energy to displace others in a connected cascade. The vacancy and the interstitial it becomes form an isolated Frenkel pair, a few lattice spacings apart. This is the regime of Coulomb recoils, and so of electrons and low-energy protons.
  • One cascade, from about 1–2 keV to about 12–20 keV. The recoil displaces further atoms, which displace others, in a single compact cascade; the dense region it leaves is called a cluster. Nuclear elastic scattering produces recoils in this range.
  • Subcascades, above about 12–20 keV. The recoil travels far enough between its energetic collisions that the damage breaks into separate subcascades strung along its path, branching into a tree. Nuclear reactions produce recoils in this range.

Deck 03 p. 37 shows the scale. A 50 keV silicon recoil — typical, on that slide, of what a 1 MeV neutron or a high-energy proton produces — leaves a branching track about 800 Å (80 nm) long, with dense clusters at the ends of its branches. Marshall and Marshall's 1999 NSREC Short Course summarizes the finer structure from Monte Carlo cascade calculations: a single cascade typically carries two or three terminal clusters, each about 5 nm across, formed where a recoil spends the last 5–10 keV of its energy, joined by strings of dilute displacements; transmission electron microscopy of neutron-irradiated silicon finds damaged regions averaging about 4 nm. Above the upper boundary the damage is more of the same: Wood et al. showed that cascade damage looks alike however many subcascades it contains. The distinction that matters electrically is therefore between isolated point defects and clustered damage.

The number of displacements a recoil produces follows from the energy it spends on atomic collisions — its damage energy, the recoil energy multiplied by the Lindhard partition of (12.2) — divided by the energy each displacement costs.

(12.4)

The Lindhard partition also caps the damage. However energetic a silicon recoil is, no more than about 300 keV of its energy goes into displacements, because the rest is lost to ionization; Marshall and Marshall give that ceiling, and about 2 MeV for GaAs. By (12.4) a 300 keV damage energy in silicon corresponds to about 5,700 displacements, so even the largest cascade a single silicon recoil can form contains a few thousand vacancy–interstitial pairs before recombination.

Check: the regime boundaries of this section are recoil energies, and a 10 keV damage energy belongs to a somewhat larger recoil, since the Lindhard partition sends part of the recoil's energy into ionization. That recoil lies near the 12–20 keV upper boundary, so these 190 displacements form one cascade, close to the onset of subcascades, not 190 isolated pairs.

Of those pairs about 90% recombine promptly and about 10% survive (Deck 03 p. 34). The survivors do not stay as they were created. The vacancy in silicon is mobile even at liquid-nitrogen temperature, so surviving vacancies migrate until they meet another vacancy or an impurity atom and form a stable complex: the divacancy (V–V), the E centre (a vacancy bound to a phosphorus donor, V–P), and the A centre (a vacancy bound to an interstitial oxygen atom, V–O). These complexes, not the vacancies and interstitials themselves, carry most of the electrical activity of irradiated silicon. Which complexes form depends on the dopant and impurity content of the silicon, which is why the doping type returns in §4.

The recoil spectrum, not the identity of the incident particle, sets the defect structure. Marshall and Marshall, summarizing Dale et al., point out that the recoil spectrum of a 60 MeV electron resembles that of a 10 MeV proton more closely than a 10 MeV proton's resembles a 60 MeV proton's. A particle species is a poor label for displacement damage; the fraction of the non-ionizing energy delivered below and above the cascade threshold is a good one, and §4 uses it.

A 2 MeV electron beam and a fast-neutron beam are adjusted to deliver the same displacement damage dose to two identical silicon samples. Describe how the defects differ in the two samples, and name one measured property that could differ even though the dose is equal.

The electron sample holds only isolated Frenkel pairs: an electron's recoil limit, a few hundred electron-volts even at 2 MeV, is below the 1–2 keV cascade boundary. Each surviving vacancy migrates until it meets an impurity or another vacancy, so the sample fills with point-defect complexes — E centres in phosphorus-doped material, A centres where oxygen is present. The neutron sample receives energetic recoils spread up to Tmax, so most of its non-ionizing energy goes into cascades; its damage sits in clusters a few nanometres across, joined by dilute strings of displacements. Equal dose means equal energy in displacements, not equal defects. Any property set by a defect whose concentration differs between point and cluster damage can differ. The survival fraction is one: well-separated pairs recombine less than pairs inside a dense cascade. Annealing temperature is another, since electron-produced E centres anneal at a lower temperature than proton-produced ones (§5).

3 · Defects, carrier lifetime and bipolar gain

A stable defect matters electrically because it places allowed electron states inside the bandgap, where the perfect crystal has none. Those states exchange carriers with the conduction and valence bands, and the exchange takes one of five forms. Which form dominates depends on where in the gap the level lies and on the carrier populations around it — a depleted region, a neutral region under injection, a charge packet in transit — and each form maps onto a device parameter. This section names the five, derives the lifetime law that governs the most consequential of them, and carries it into the bipolar transistor, where it becomes the Messenger–Spratt relation.

The complexes of §2 each sit at a characteristic depth. The E centre lies about 0.44 eV below the conduction band, as does a level of the divacancy, and the A centre about 0.17 eV below it (Hopkinson and Mohammadzadeh). A level near midgap, about 0.55 eV from either band in silicon, is the most effective at generating and recombining carriers, because the two steps through it — valence band to level, level to conduction band — then cost equal energies, and neither is the bottleneck. Marshall and Marshall, after Gover and Srour, list the five processes by which such a level acts.

  1. Generation. An electron is thermally excited from the valence band to the level and then emitted to the conduction band, creating an electron–hole pair in two steps of about half the gap each. In a depletion region the field separates the pair before it can recombine, so every generated pair is current: leakage in a reverse-biased junction, dark current in an imaging pixel.
  2. Recombination. A carrier of one sign is captured at the level and, before it is re-emitted, a carrier of the other sign is captured as well, annihilating the pair. The minority-carrier lifetime is set by this rate, so it degrades every device that relies on minority carriers surviving a transit: bipolar gain, solar-cell current, photodetector responsivity.
  3. Trapping. A carrier is captured and later re-emitted to the band it came from. Nothing is lost permanently, but the carrier is withheld from its signal for the level's emission time — the mechanism of charge transfer inefficiency in a CCD (M12b §4).
  4. Compensation. Deep levels capture majority carriers supplied by the shallow dopants and remove them from conduction without any recombination. The net carrier concentration falls and the resistivity rises. This is carrier removal, and it acts first on lightly doped regions, such as the collector of a bipolar transistor.
  5. Tunnelling. A level in the gap of a high-field junction lets carriers cross the barrier in two shorter tunnelling steps, raising the reverse current. It matters most in small-bandgap materials and at high fields.

The five do not act at the same fluence. Semiconductor material is normally doped at least an order of magnitude more heavily than its density of recombination and generation centres, and irradiation adds to the second population, not the first. Minority-carrier lifetime and generation lifetime therefore degrade long before the majority-carrier concentration does, and mobility suffers only at very high damage levels (Marshall and Marshall). That ordering is why displacement damage degrades bipolar transistors, solar cells, photodetectors, imagers and some optocouplers, and leaves most MOS circuits — which conduct by majority carriers across a short channel — largely unaffected. Charge-coupled and charge-injection devices are the MOS exception, because they depend on generation lifetime and on trapping. In orbit, displacement damage usually sets the useful life of imagers and solar cells (M12b §4). A discrete bipolar transistor in orbit is usually degraded more by total dose than by displacement damage; its role in displacement-damage studies is that of a test vehicle, and among bipolar parts displacement damage remains a particular concern in analog integrated circuits (Marshall and Marshall). Deck 03 p. 34 names the affected applications as solar cells, CCDs and bipolar devices, and Deck 02 p. 23 adds other minority-carrier devices; the sister course's EEL5385 M11 §§6–7 tabulates the same mapping device by device.

Deck 15 p. 15 states the recombination–generation step in the form of Shockley and Read (1952): a defect level interacts with the bands in four ways — electron capture, electron emission, hole capture and hole emission — and the Shockley–Read–Hall (SRH) rate U is the net balance of the four. M06 §7 gave the single-level form (written R there), U = (pn − ni²)/[τn0(p + p1) + τp0(n + n1)]. Its sign carries the device consequence. Under injection, pn exceeds ni² and U > 0: a captured electron is followed by a captured hole, the pair is lost, and the minority-carrier lifetime falls. In a reverse-biased depletion region the field sweeps carriers out, so pn ≪ ni², U < 0, and the same level emits an electron to the conduction band and a hole to the valence band: generation, which appears as leakage or dark current. The 2024 NSREC Short Course keeps three lifetimes apart for this reason — a scattering time, a recombination lifetime and a generation lifetime — and warns that they are not interchangeable in the device models.

The scattering time decides mobility. A carrier accelerated by a field travels for a mean time τc between scattering events, and its mobility is that time multiplied by q/m*, with m* its effective mass (Deck 15 p. 13). Each defect, and each charged defect in particular, is one more scattering centre: more defects mean a shorter τc, so displacement damage lowers the mobility and raises the resistivity. Deck 15 p. 13 states both, through the two relations below.

The two factors do not move at the same fluence. Carrier removal, process 4 above, lowers the majority-carrier concentration as deep levels capture the carriers that the dopants supply. Johnston writes the change as linear in fluence, Δn = RcΦ, with a carrier removal rate Rc in cm⁻³ per particle·cm⁻² — usually quoted as cm⁻¹ — that usually stays constant while the change is below about 25%. Removal rates are roughly the same across materials: Johnston quotes about 5 cm⁻¹ for neutrons in silicon carbide JFETs and, from another study, a silicon carbide rate only about three times below silicon's. The mobility follows μ0/μ = 1 + KμΦ, but its damage constant is lower than those for lifetime and carrier removal, so mobility loss usually matters only at very high fluences. In a bipolar transistor the law acts on the base. M06 §7 divided the base current into back-injection into the emitter, recombination in the emitter–base depletion region, and recombination in the neutral base. Take the last. The minority carriers injected into the base form a stored charge QB that crosses the base in the transit time τt, so the collector current is QB/τt; the same charge recombines at the rate QB/τ, which is the neutral-base recombination current. Their ratio, τt/τ, is this component's contribution to 1/β. Substituting (12.6), and writing the transit time through the cutoff frequency, τt ≈ 1/(2πft) when base transit dominates the transistor's delay, gives the reciprocal-gain law in the form Deck 04 p. 35 and Deck 05 p. 6 state it, after Messenger and Ash.

Three consequences follow. The reciprocal gain, not the gain, is linear in fluence, so degradation data are plotted as Δ(1/β) against Φ and the damage constant is read from the slope, while the gain itself falls hyperbolically. The cutoff frequency sits in the denominator, so a transistor with a narrow base — short transit time, high ft — degrades less at the same fluence, which is Deck 04 p. 35's statement that high-speed bipolar transistors degrade less because the base is narrower. And nothing in the derivation is specific to neutrons: the same law describes displacement damage by protons, heavy ions and electrons, each with its own constant, which is what allows a damage factor to be expressed as a 1 MeV equivalent neutron fluence and converted between particles through NIEL (§4).

A transistor with β₀ = 100 falls to β = 50 after a fast-neutron fluence. A second transistor of the same base material and the same β₀, with ten times the cutoff frequency, receives the same fluence. What is its gain, and why is it not 95?

With K1 and Φn unchanged and ft ten times larger, Δ(1/β) is ten times smaller, 0.001, so 1/β = 0.011 and β ≈ 91. Check where the constant sits before substituting a tabulated value. The course, and Schrimpf's chapter in the course textbook, put the damage constant in the numerator, as in (12.7). The published Messenger–Spratt relation, and the damage constants tabulated from it, put it in the denominator: Δ(1/β) = Φn/(ωTK), with ωT = 2πft and K in n·s/cm², so K1 = 1/K. Marshall and Marshall absorb everything into one factor, Δ(1/β) = K(E)Φ. Substituting a tabulated K for K1 inverts the answer. The relation also has a regime: the 2024 NSREC Short Course lists heavy damage, a change of bias, operation at a temperature other than the irradiation temperature, short base widths, high doping and recombination in the emitter–base region as conditions under which it can mispredict; the last three are from Barnaby et al. (2017), whose modified relation for small transistors M12b §6 works through.

Displacement damage and total dose both raise the base current of a bipolar transistor while leaving the collector current alone, so both lower the gain, but they act in different places. Total dose acts at the surface: interface traps where the emitter–base depletion region meets the passivating oxide raise the surface recombination velocity (M08 §9), and the excess base current carries an ideality factor near 2. Displacement damage acts in the bulk: it shortens the lifetime throughout the neutral base, raising the ideality-1 component, and in the bulk of the emitter–base depletion region. Studies that isolate displacement damage use the separation deliberately. They pre-irradiate transistors with cobalt-60 to multi-megarad levels, to saturate the ionization damage before the particle exposure, and avoid low-current bias, where surface currents dominate (Marshall and Marshall).

Protons deliver both kinds of damage at once, and the two are not simply additive. Pease's chapter in the course textbook notes that a laboratory simulation with cobalt-60 and neutrons must irradiate the same samples consecutively for that reason, and Schrimpf's chapter records that proton irradiation can make linear bipolar circuits fail at much lower total doses than gamma irradiation, sometimes by a different failure mechanism (Rax, Johnston and Miyahira, 1999). Johnston gives an example: an LM137 negative voltage regulator irradiated with 50 MeV protons failed catastrophically at equivalent total doses of 18–35 krad(Si), through degradation of its start-up circuit, a failure mode that did not occur in the equivalent cobalt-60 tests.

Two regions on one irradiated silicon wafer are compared: a 10¹⁴ cm⁻³ n-type collector and a 10¹⁷ cm⁻³ p-type base. Both now show a shorter minority-carrier lifetime, but only the collector's resistivity has risen. Explain the difference, and say which change would appear first if the fluence were raised further in the base.

The lifetime falls in both because the same stable defects add SRH recombination paths wherever carriers are injected: recombination rates add, so 1/τ rises linearly with fluence by (12.6), independently of the doping. The resistivity depends instead on the majority-carrier concentration and the mobility, through (12.5). Carrier removal takes majority carriers out of conduction at a rate Δn = RcΦ that grows with doping only sub-linearly, so a given fluence removes a measurable fraction of the collector's 10¹⁴ cm⁻³ and a negligible fraction of the base's 10¹⁷ cm⁻³; the collector's ρ rises, the base's does not. Mobility has not moved in either: its damage constant is lower than those for lifetime and carrier removal, so it usually matters only at very high fluence. Raising the fluence further, the base first shows more lifetime loss — and so more gain loss — long before any resistivity change, because in heavily doped material carrier removal becomes significant only when the removed density approaches the doping, and mobility loss later still.

4 · NIEL scaling and where it fails

NIEL is worth tabulating only if device degradation scales with it, whatever particle delivered it. M04 §6 stated this NIEL scaling hypothesis. This section states what it assumes physically, where the course's sources find it holding, and the three places they find it failing.

(12.8)

NIEL measures the energy that goes into creating vacancy–interstitial pairs, and about 90% of those pairs recombine. For degradation to scale with it, two things must hold: the fraction of pairs that survives must not depend on the recoil energy, and a surviving defect must affect the device in the same way whether it came from an isolated pair or from inside a cluster (Marshall and Marshall, after Dale et al., 1988). Hopkinson and Mohammadzadeh put the condition parameter by parameter: scaling holds when the concentration of the defect that dominates the parameter is the same for point damage and for cluster damage. Huhtinen showed that it is for the divacancy, while higher vacancy complexes may be more common in clusters. The correlation is therefore an empirical result, checked for each material, device and parameter.

The course's sources record three departures, and all three involve the arrangement of the damage rather than its amount.

Electrons in silicon depend on the doping type (Deck 02 p. 24). Electron damage coefficients in n-type silicon lie on the extension of the proton line; in p-type silicon they lie below it and rise roughly as the square of NIEL. The exception arises under exactly the condition that tests the scaling assumption hardest. An electron's damage consists entirely of isolated point defects (§1), and what an isolated vacancy becomes depends on the impurities it meets as it migrates (§2), which differ between phosphorus-doped and boron-doped silicon.

Low-energy protons in n-type silicon damage more than NIEL predicts. A laboratory cannot reach the proton energy at which a transistor's damage constant was measured and substitutes a beam of higher energy, whose NIEL in silicon is one third as large. What fluence reproduces the original exposure, and under what circumstances would the transistors still degrade differently?

NIEL scaling says the degradation follows the displacement damage dose, Φ·NIEL by (12.1), so the substitute beam needs three times the original fluence: with one third the NIEL, three times the particles deliver the same non-ionizing energy per gram. Equal dose equalizes the energy that went into displacements, not the number or kind of defects that survive, and the two beams can differ there. The higher-energy beam delivers more of its damage through nuclear elastic scattering and reactions, so a larger share arrives as clusters, where pairs recombine more readily than the well-separated Frenkel pairs of low-energy Coulomb recoils; low-energy protons in n-type silicon are the documented case in which damage exceeds the NIEL prediction for that reason (§4). The transistors also receive a different ionizing dose at the two energies, which changes the surface component of the base current, and any difference in the time and temperature between exposure and measurement changes how much has annealed (§5). Where the sources show scaling holding, as for Summers' transistors, the threefold fluence reproduces the damage; elsewhere the substitution needs a check at both energies.

5 · Annealing of displacement damage

Displacement damage is not frozen at the moment of irradiation. The defect population keeps evolving after the exposure ends, and a device parameter measured after a burst of particles recovers in stages, quickly at first and then ever more slowly. Silicon anneals at room temperature; unbiased GaAs essentially does not. The time between exposure and measurement is therefore part of any displacement-damage result, and it decides how a test relates to the application.

The earliest stages are over before any electrical measurement can be made. Most close Frenkel pairs recombine in less than a millisecond, and the surviving vacancies, mobile even at liquid-nitrogen temperature, settle into stable complexes on a timescale of seconds (Marshall and Marshall). Deck 03 p. 38, after Srour, Marshall and Marshall (2003), follows what comes next in a device held at room temperature. After a neutron burst the carrier lifetime, or the gain of a transistor, drops abruptly and then recovers. The recovery period, short-term annealing, begins shortly after the damage is created and is essentially complete within several minutes to one hour. A long-term anneal then recovers part of the remaining damage slowly, over a period the slide carries out to a year. The slide puts the damage left after short-term annealing in quotation marks as "permanent" because it goes on recovering, only slowly, and it names the mechanism: recombination, or annihilation, and rearrangement of defects.

Two clocks for "short-term". Marshall and Marshall use the phrase for the evolution of Frenkel pairs into stable room-temperature defects and put it within about a second, while Deck 03 p. 38 shows device parameters still recovering for minutes to an hour after a neutron burst. Recovery at the device level depends on temperature and on carrier injection, which accelerates annealing (below), so the time over which it is observed depends on the device and its operating state. This page follows Deck 03 p. 38 for device-level recovery.

The stable complexes differ in thermal stability — Marshall and Marshall give the divacancy as stable to about 300 °C, the E centre to about 150 °C and the A centre to about 350 °C — so heating removes them selectively, and the defect a parameter depends on sets the temperature needed to recover it. Annealing out the traps that degrade charge transfer in a CCD requires more than 100 °C (Hopkinson and Mohammadzadeh). The same defect can also anneal differently according to how it was made: E centres produced by 1 MeV electrons anneal at a significantly lower temperature than those produced by protons (Marshall and Marshall), a difference between point and cluster damage that NIEL, which counts only energy, cannot see. The material matters as well. Silicon anneals at room temperature, while GaAs does not anneal appreciably unless heated to 200 °C or more (IEEE NSREC 2024 Short Course, after Swift et al., 2003).

Current through a forward-biased junction accelerates annealing in silicon, GaAs and GaN (IEEE NSREC 2024 Short Course). In GaAs light-emitting diodes and laser diodes the effect is decisive: little or no annealing takes place until current is applied, and recovery begins when it is (Johnston). Marshall and Marshall attribute this to recombination-enhanced defect reactions — the energy released when a carrier pair recombines non-radiatively through the defect moves the defect itself — rather than to heating of the junction. A laser diode operated under forward bias during irradiation therefore anneals while it is being damaged, and degrades less at a low particle flux than at a laboratory one.

Silicon partially recovers through room-temperature annealing, drawn as the Srour, Marshall and Marshall burst-and-recovery curve; GaAs recovers only after thermal or charge-injection-induced annealing, shown with GaAs devices after Johnston et al. Held at a forward current of 5 mA after irradiation, the three parts show no recovery for the first tens of seconds of bias; their light output then rises over 10³–10⁵ s of applied bias, in the best case (the OD880) to about 1.27 times its post-irradiation value at 10³ s, 1.50 at 10⁴ s and about 1.7 at the last point. The lecture added the thermodynamic reading: returning a displaced atom to a lattice site takes energy, which either temperature or the energy of recombining carriers must supply.

Three consequences follow for testing. First, a damage constant is incomplete without the time and temperature of its measurement: a reading taken within the short-term window shows more damage than persists, and the universal dark-current damage factor of M12b §3 is defined at 300 K one week after irradiation, after most of the annealing has run. Second, the test and the application can run on different clocks. A pulsed exposure such as a neutron burst is followed at once by the circuit's operation, so the early, unannealed damage is what the circuit experiences; a spacecraft accumulates its fluence over months, and each increment completes its short-term recovery long before the mission ends. Third, because long-term room-temperature annealing is usually small, a device can often be exposed passively and characterized in the laboratory later, and its degradation is generally independent of bias during irradiation. Compound-semiconductor photonic devices, whose degradation and annealing depend on bias, are the stated exception (Marshall and Marshall), and an unbiased GaAs device keeps its damage until it is powered.

Two laboratories measure the gain degradation of the same bipolar transistor type after the same neutron fluence. One reads the parts within ten minutes of a pulsed exposure, the other a week later. Which reports the larger degradation, which number applies to a spacecraft, and why?

The laboratory that reads within ten minutes reports more damage. After a burst the gain drops abruptly and then recovers through short-term annealing, which runs for minutes to about an hour at room temperature in silicon; a reading inside that window catches defects that will anneal. The week-later reading sees the damage left after short-term annealing, less the small part already removed by the slow long-term anneal. A spacecraft accumulates its fluence over months, so each increment completes its short-term recovery long before the mission ends; the week-later number is the one that applies. The early number applies to a pulsed environment in which the circuit must work immediately after the burst. A damage constant without its measurement time and temperature is therefore incomplete — the universal dark-current damage factor is defined at 300 K and about one week after irradiation for this reason (M12b §3).

Where this leaves us

Displacement damage has now been followed from a single collision to the carriers of a damaged crystal. Of the ways a particle loses energy, only nuclear stopping and nuclear reactions set atoms moving. Kinematics fix the largest recoil a particle can make, and the cross-section fixes how often it makes each smaller one: charged particles make mostly low-energy recoils by Coulomb scattering, neutrons and energetic protons make energetic recoils by nuclear scattering and nuclear reactions, and electrons of a few MeV make only point defects. The recoil energy decides whether the damage is an isolated Frenkel pair, a cluster or a tree of subcascades, and about a tenth of the pairs survive to form stable complexes whose levels in the gap generate, recombine, trap, compensate and assist tunnelling.

Those processes act on three carrier properties, in order of fluence: lifetime first, by the reciprocal law (12.6); then the majority-carrier concentration through carrier removal; and mobility last, through the scattering time. Through the lifetime they reach bipolar gain by the Messenger–Spratt relation (12.7), linear in reciprocal gain. NIEL scaling compresses particle and energy into one number, and it works because, to first order, the surviving fraction and the defect inventory do not depend on the recoil spectrum. It fails where they do: electrons in p-type silicon, low-energy protons in n-type silicon, and GaAs at high proton energy; electron damage in GaAs solar cells tracks NIEL only after a fitted exponent. The damage anneals in silicon at room temperature and in GaAs only with heat or injected current, so every damage constant carries a time and a temperature.

M12b takes this physics into practice: how each of these changes is measured, from I–V curves to deep-level spectroscopy; what generation, trapping and recombination do to imagers, solar cells and optocouplers; how the damage is modelled from particle transport to dark current; why a small bipolar transistor departs from Messenger–Spratt; how one cascade becomes a stuck bit in a nanoscale memory; and how a part is qualified for a mission. A1 §5 places the displacement-damage literature cited here in the history of the field.

Quick reference

  • Stopping (Deck 15 p. 5): electronic stopping (excitation, ionization) → electron–hole pairs, TID and SEE; nuclear stopping (Coulomb recoil of a target atom) and nuclear reactions (elastic, inelastic, fission) → recoiling atoms → displacements. LET and NIEL of a proton in Si sit ≥10³ apart (Deck 15 p. 10). TID = k·LET·Φ (rad); DDD = NIEL·Φ (MeV/g).
  • Kinematics (12.3): Tmax = 4mM/(m+M)²·E ≈ 0.133E for protons and neutrons in Si (0.1337 unrounded): 1 MeV n → 133 keV, 5 MeV p → 668 keV, 10 MeV p → 1.33 MeV. Electrons, relativistic: Tmax = 2E(E+2mec²)/Mc², Mc²(Si) = 26,166 MeV: 1 MeV e⁻ → 155 eV, 2 MeV → 462 eV; no displacement below ~0.22 MeV. Electrons of a few MeV and cobalt-60 gammas make point defects only (Tmax reaches 1 keV near 3 MeV).
  • Recoil spectra: Coulomb (Rutherford) dσ/dT ∝ 1/T², mean ≈ Td ln(Tmax/Td) ≈ 200–300 eV, equal energy per decade of T. Neutron (hard sphere): flat to Tmax, energy at the top. Si: Coulomb dominates below ~10 MeV protons (higher in GaAs); nuclear reactions dominate above ~20 MeV. 63 MeV p: σel = 318 b, σinel = 0.52 b, but each inelastic event carries ~10³× the damage energy.
  • NIEL (12.2) = (NA/A)∫T·L(T)·(dσ/dT)dT with a per-atom cross-section; Deck 03 p. 40's 1/ρ needs a macroscopic one. DDD (12.1) = Φ·NIEL, MeV/g; ×1.602×10⁻¹⁰ for Gy. 100 MeV·mb = 2.144 keV·cm²/g (Si).
  • Frenkel pair = vacancy + interstitial. Regimes: free defects below El ≈ 1–2 keV; one cascade to Eu ≈ 12–20 keV (Deck 03: 10–20); subcascades above. Proton markers: 6–10 MeV and >20 MeV. 50 keV Si recoil → cascade ~80 nm, 2–3 terminal clusters ~5 nm. Damage-energy ceiling of one recoil: ~300 keV (Si), ~2 MeV (GaAs).
  • NRT (12.4): Nd = κEd/(2Eth), κ = 0.8 (universal), Eth = 21 eV: 10 keV damage energy → 190. ~90% recombine, ~10% stable. Complexes: divacancy V–V; E centre V–P, Ec − 0.44 eV; A centre V–O, Ec − 0.17 eV; stable to ~300, ~150, ~350 °C.
  • Five processes: generation → leakage, dark current · recombination → lifetime, gain, solar-cell current · trapping → CTI · compensation → carrier removal · tunnelling → junction leakage. SRH (Deck 15 p. 15): electron capture, electron emission, hole capture, hole emission; U > 0 recombines (pn > ni²), U < 0 generates (depletion). Scattering, recombination and generation lifetimes are not interchangeable.
  • Mobility and resistivity (12.5): μ = qτc/m*, ρ = 1/(qμnn + qμpp): DD lowers μ, raises ρ. Carrier removal Δn = RcΦ, linear to ~25%, Rc similar across materials (SiC JFETs ≈ 5 cm⁻¹; SiC ~3× below Si), sub-linear in doping; μ0/μ = 1 + KμΦ usually only at very high fluence. Order: lifetime, then carrier concentration, then mobility; light doping first.
  • Lifetime (12.6): 1/τ = 1/τ0 + Φ/Kτ; halved at Φ = Kτ/τ0. Si base, 50 MeV p: Kτ ≈ 7×10⁵ cm⁻²·s. 1/L² = 1/L0² + Φ/(DKτ).
  • Messenger–Spratt (12.7): 1/β = 1/β0 + K1Φn/(2πft); course form 1/β − 1/β0 = KΦ (K in cm²). K1 = 1/K of the tabulated denominator form (n·s/cm²). DD raises recombination in the neutral base and the E–B depletion region (Deck 15 p. 18); Messenger and Spratt 1958 (Ge and Si; the slide's figure is Ge). Reciprocal linear; high ft degrades less. TID acts at the surface, DD in the bulk; protons do both, not additively. Small geometry → M12b §6.
  • NIEL scaling (12.8): ΔP = kdamage·DDD. Holds: transistor lifetime factors for p, d, α (Summers 1987, Deck 15 p. 28); GaAs/Ge solar-cell Pmax for p and e⁻ against DDD (Messenger 1999, Deck 15 p. 20); GaAs Jsc close to one line on a log-log plot (course version). But GaAs-cell electrons fit NIEL1.7; log-log plots hide ×2–3. Fails: e⁻ in p-type Si (∝ NIEL², ~10× below n-type at 1 MeV); low-energy protons in n-type Si; GaAs above ~40 MeV (Deck 15 p. 9: calculation and data still offset).
  • Annealing: Frenkel pairs <1 ms; stable complexes ~seconds; short-term anneal minutes to ~1 h; long-term slow, to ~1 yr. Si anneals at room temperature; unbiased GaAs needs ≥200 °C; forward bias anneals Si, GaAs, GaN — GaAs LEDs at 5 mA recover over 10³–10⁵ s (Deck 15 p. 11, which captions them photodiodes).
