Module 12

Displacement Damage and NIEL

How the kinematics of a single collision set the energy of the atomic recoils a particle produces, how the recoil energy decides whether the damage is an isolated defect, a cluster or a branching set of subcascades, what the surviving defects do to carrier lifetime, generation and charge transfer, where the NIEL correlation holds and where it fails, how the damage anneals, and what it does to imagers, solar cells and nanoscale devices.

Prerequisites M04, M05, M06 Read time ~45 min Sources Deck 03 pp. 34–45; Deck 02 pp. 23–25; Deck 04 p. 35; course textbook; NSREC short courses 1999, 2021, 2024

M04 §6 introduced the non-ionizing channel. A small share of the energy a particle deposits moves nuclei rather than electrons; an atom given more than 21 eV in silicon leaves its lattice site as a primary knock-on atom; about 90% of the vacancy–interstitial pairs created recombine, and the surviving 10% form stable defects whose accumulated measure is the displacement damage dose, fluence times NIEL. M05 §3 placed the effect in the classification: cumulative, non-ionizing, and located in the semiconductor bulk rather than in any insulator. This module opens the quantities those pages took as given. It asks what sets the energy of a recoil, what arrangement of damage a recoil of given energy leaves behind, and what the surviving defects do to a device.

Displacement damage is decided in two steps. The incident particle decides the recoil spectrum through the kinematics of the collision, and the recoil spectrum decides the spatial structure of the damage — isolated point defects, a single cluster, or a tree of subcascades (§§1–2). The surviving defects then act through their energy levels in the bandgap, and five electrical processes — generation, recombination, trapping, compensation and tunnelling — account for every device consequence (§3). NIEL compresses the first step into one number per particle, energy and target, and it succeeds to the extent that a device responds to the number of displacements rather than to their arrangement; §4 is about where that assumption fails. §5 follows the damage in time, §6 applies the whole chain to imagers and solar cells, the devices most sensitive to it, and §7 takes the limit in which one cascade is as large as the device.

One contrast runs through the module. Total ionizing dose acts in the oxides and at the oxide–silicon interface (M07, M08). Displacement damage acts in the crystal itself — the base of a bipolar transistor, the buried channel of a charge-coupled device, the absorber of a solar cell — and nothing done to an oxide changes it. The devices it limits are therefore the ones whose operation depends on the silicon bulk: on minority carriers surviving a diffusion path, or on a depleted volume staying free of generation centres.

1 · Collision kinematics and the recoil spectrum

The first event in displacement damage is a two-body collision between the incident particle and a lattice nucleus. Its outcome is the kinetic energy T handed to the struck atom, and the distribution of T over many collisions — the recoil, or primary knock-on atom (PKA), spectrum — is set by the particle's mass, its charge and its energy. That spectrum is the input to NIEL and to everything that follows in this module. Deck 03 pp. 35–36 make the point in one line: the energy of the recoils is determined by the collision kinematics.

M04 §6 wrote the accumulated quantity as a fluence multiplied by the non-ionizing energy loss, the non-ionizing counterpart of dose written as fluence times LET.

DDD = Φ · NIEL
(12.1)
DDD displacement damage dose, the non-ionizing energy deposited per unit mass (MeV/g) · Φ particle fluence (cm⁻²) · NIEL non-ionizing energy loss of that particle, at that energy, in that target (MeV·cm²/g). For a spectrum of energies, integrate NIEL(E) against the differential fluence dΦ/dE, and sum over particle species when there is more than one. The number (12.1) is the one M04 §6 uses. On the formula sheet as (12.1).

NIEL itself is an integral over the recoil spectrum, and it has three ingredients that this section supplies: the largest recoil energy a collision can produce, the cross-section for producing each recoil energy below that limit, and the Lindhard partition L(T), the fraction of a recoil's own energy that goes into moving further atoms rather than into the ionization the recoil causes as it travels.

NIEL(E) = NAA ∫TdTmax T · L(T) · dσ(E, T)dT dT
(12.2)
NIEL non-ionizing energy loss (MeV·cm²/g) · E kinetic energy of the incident particle (MeV) · NA Avogadro's number, 6.022×10²³ mol⁻¹ · A atomic weight of the target (g/mol; 28.09 for silicon), so NA/A is atoms per gram · T recoil energy of the primary knock-on atom (MeV) · L(T) Lindhard partition, dimensionless, well below 1 for recoils of tens of keV · dσ/dT per-atom differential cross-section for producing a recoil of energy T (cm²/MeV) · Td displacement threshold, 21 eV in silicon · Tmax maximum transferable energy from (12.3). The units check as (g⁻¹)(MeV)(cm²/MeV)(MeV) = MeV·cm²/g. Where several interaction channels contribute — Coulomb scattering, nuclear elastic scattering, nuclear reactions — the integral is the sum of the channels; for a compound it is the sum over the constituent atoms weighted by mole fraction. Tabulated per particle, energy and target, and used from tables. On the formula sheet as (12.2).

The prefactor depends on which cross-section is meant. Deck 03 p. 40 writes the definition with a prefactor 1/ρ, the inverse target density, and labels dσ/dT the differential scattering cross-section. Read as the usual per-atom cross-section in cm²/MeV, that combination has units of MeV·cm⁵/g and is not an energy loss per unit areal density; converting a per-atom cross-section into a per-gram quantity takes NA/A, as in (12.2). The slide's 1/ρ is correct if dσ/dT is read instead as a macroscopic cross-section — the per-atom value multiplied by the atomic density n = ρNA/A, in cm⁻¹·MeV⁻¹ — because (1/ρ)·n = NA/A. The slide also omits L(T); M04 §6 explains why, without it, the integral returns the total energy given to recoils rather than the non-ionizing part. Use (12.2) with a per-atom cross-section.

The upper limit follows from conservation of energy and momentum. A particle of mass m and kinetic energy E transfers the most energy to a stationary nucleus of mass M in a head-on collision, in which the nucleus recoils along the particle's original direction. For an electron, whose kinetic energy in this subject is comparable with or larger than its rest energy, the relativistic form is required.

Tmax = 4mM(m + M)2 E  (proton, neutron),    Tmax = 2E(E + 2mec2)Mc2  (electron)
(12.3)
Tmax maximum energy transferred to the target atom (MeV) · E kinetic energy of the incident particle (MeV) · m, M masses of the incident particle and the target atom, in any common unit, since only their ratio enters · mec2 = 0.511 MeV · Mc2 rest energy of the target atom (MeV), 26,160 MeV for silicon (28.09 u × 931.5 MeV/u). For a proton or a neutron on silicon the mass factor is 0.133. The first form is non-relativistic and holds for a proton to within about 5% up to 100 MeV; the second assumes M ≫ me and holds at any electron energy. Elastic collisions only; a nuclear reaction releases fragments whose energies these expressions do not bound. On the formula sheet as (12.3).

The mass factor is why an electron produces so little damage per particle. A 1 MeV neutron can give a silicon atom up to 133 keV and a 10 MeV proton up to 1.33 MeV. A 1 MeV electron, about 51,000 times lighter than the atom, can give it at most 155 eV — seven times the threshold, enough for one or a few displacements but not for a cascade — and below about 0.22 MeV it cannot displace a silicon atom at all. Electrons therefore produce only isolated point defects, and so do cobalt-60 gamma rays, which cause displacements only through the Compton electrons they liberate, of at most about 1 MeV. The same kinematics are worked in the sister course's EEL5385 M11 §§1 and 3.

The upper limit bounds the spectrum; the cross-section fills it in, and here charged and uncharged particles differ in kind. A proton interacts with a nucleus through two forces. The Coulomb force between the two nuclear charges has long range, so a proton passing a nucleus at any distance is deflected slightly, and distant passes, which transfer little energy, far outnumber close ones. The cross-section for Coulomb (Rutherford) scattering to transfer energy T falls as 1/T², and the recoil spectrum is crowded toward the threshold. A neutron carries no charge and interacts only through the short-range nuclear force, in a close encounter or not at all. At low neutron energies these encounters approximate hard-sphere collisions, which spread the recoil energies uniformly from zero to Tmax: a flat spectrum. Deck 03 p. 35 draws the two shapes, a falling spectrum for low-energy protons and a flat one for neutrons, and assigns the first to isolated point defects and the second to cascades and multiple cascades.

The falling spectrum has a characteristic recoil energy that barely depends on the proton energy. For a 1/T² spectrum between Td and Tmax ≫ Td, the mean recoil energy is Td ln(Tmax/Td): about 230 eV for a 10 MeV proton in silicon and still under 300 eV at 100 MeV, since the upper limit enters only through a logarithm. Hopkinson and Mohammadzadeh, in the course textbook, quote an average proton-induced recoil of about 200 eV over a wide range of proton energies, and conclude that proton elastic collisions tend to produce isolated defects.

Counting recoils and counting energy give different pictures, and NIEL counts energy. Weighted by T, the 1/T² spectrum puts equal energy into every decade of recoil energy between threshold and Tmax, so the rare energetic Coulomb recoils carry as much transferred energy per decade as the numerous near-threshold ones. The flat neutron spectrum weighted the same way puts nearly all of its energy into the top decade below Tmax.

Two further channels open as the proton energy rises. Nuclear elastic scattering — the proton deflected by the nuclear force rather than by the Coulomb field — gives recoils whose spectrum resembles a neutron's, and nuclear reactions (inelastic collisions, including spallation, in which the struck nucleus is excited or broken up) release recoiling fragments with energies far above the elastic ones. Deck 03 p. 35 places the division for silicon: below about 10 MeV Coulomb scattering dominates and the recoils are of low energy; above about 20 MeV inelastic collisions tend to dominate. The slide marks the Coulomb regime as extending to higher proton energy in GaAs. A heavier nucleus is consistent with that on two counts. The Rutherford cross-section per target atom, at a given proton energy, scales as Z²/M, the square of the nuclear charge over the nuclear mass; for gallium (Z = 31) and arsenic (Z = 33) that is about twice silicon's (Z = 14). And the Coulomb barrier — the electrostatic repulsion a proton must overcome to come within range of the nuclear force — rises with the nuclear charge, which holds off the nuclear channels to higher proton energy.

Nuclear events are rare, and each one is large. For 63 MeV protons in silicon the textbook chapter gives elastic and inelastic cross-sections of 318 and 0.52 barns. In a pixel volume of 900 µm³ exposed to 2×10¹⁰ protons/cm² that amounts to about 300 elastic collisions and 0.5 inelastic ones — yet each inelastic collision deposits roughly three orders of magnitude more damage energy than an elastic one. The count of events and the damage they carry are dominated by different collisions, which is why Deck 03 p. 36 concludes that the nature of displacement damage depends strongly on particle type and energy.

2 · From recoil to defect: point defects, clusters and subcascades

A recoil's energy decides how the damage it leaves is arranged in space, and the arrangement decides how much of it survives and what it does. Three regimes follow one another as the recoil energy rises. Their boundaries are recoil energies, and Deck 03 p. 36 maps them onto the proton energies at which the collision channels of §1 take over.

A recoiling silicon atom is itself an energetic ion inside the crystal, and it loses energy the way any ion does (M04 §1). The faster it moves, the larger the share of its energy that goes to electrons; as it slows, the share that goes into nuclear collisions grows, and its elastic scattering cross-section is largest over the last few keV of its path. The displacements a recoil causes are therefore concentrated toward the end of its path, which the sister course develops as the end-of-range effect (EEL5385 M11 §5). How far the damage spreads, and whether it breaks into separate pieces, depends on how much energy the recoil started with.

  • Free defects, below about 1–2 keV. The recoil displaces its own atom and has too little energy to displace others in a connected cascade. The vacancy and the interstitial it becomes form an isolated Frenkel pair, a few lattice spacings apart. This is the regime of Coulomb recoils, and so of electrons and low-energy protons.
  • One cascade, from about 1–2 keV to about 12–20 keV. The recoil displaces further atoms, which displace others, in a single compact cascade; the dense region it leaves is called a cluster. Nuclear elastic scattering produces recoils in this range.
  • Subcascades, above about 12–20 keV. The recoil travels far enough between its energetic collisions that the damage breaks into separate subcascades strung along its path, branching into a tree. Nuclear reactions produce recoils in this range.

Deck 03 p. 37 shows the scale. A 50 keV silicon recoil — typical, on that slide, of what a 1 MeV neutron or a high-energy proton produces — leaves a branching track about 800 Å (80 nm) long, with dense clusters at the ends of its branches. Marshall and Marshall's 1999 NSREC Short Course summarizes the finer structure from Monte Carlo cascade calculations: a single cascade typically carries two or three terminal clusters, each about 5 nm across, formed where a recoil spends the last 5–10 keV of its energy, joined by strings of dilute displacements; transmission electron microscopy of neutron-irradiated silicon finds damaged regions averaging about 4 nm. Above the upper boundary the damage is more of the same: Wood et al. showed that cascade damage looks alike however many subcascades it contains. The distinction that matters electrically is therefore between isolated point defects and clustered damage.

The number of displacements a recoil produces follows from the energy it spends on atomic collisions — its damage energy, the recoil energy multiplied by the Lindhard partition of (12.2) — divided by the energy each displacement costs.

Nd = κ · Ed2Eth
(12.4)
Nd number of atoms displaced by one recoil, dimensionless · κ displacement efficiency, 0.8 — a constant of the Norgett–Robinson–Torrens (NRT) model applied to every material, not a silicon-specific value, although Deck 03 p. 40 quotes it for silicon · Ed damage energy of the recoil (eV) · Eth displacement threshold (eV; 21 for silicon). The expression is the high-energy branch of a piecewise model: no displacement below Eth, exactly one up to 2Eth/κ. The standard literature writes Ed for the threshold and Tdam for the damage energy — the reverse of the course's symbols, which are kept here. It counts displacements created, before the roughly 90% that recombine. On the formula sheet as (12.4).

The Lindhard partition also caps the damage. However energetic a silicon recoil is, no more than about 300 keV of its energy goes into displacements, because the rest is lost to ionization; Marshall and Marshall give that ceiling, and about 2 MeV for GaAs. By (12.4) a 300 keV damage energy in silicon corresponds to about 5,700 displacements, so even the largest cascade a single silicon recoil can form contains a few thousand vacancy–interstitial pairs before recombination.

Of those pairs about 90% recombine promptly and about 10% survive (Deck 03 p. 34). The survivors do not stay as they were created. The vacancy in silicon is mobile even at liquid-nitrogen temperature, so surviving vacancies migrate until they meet another vacancy or an impurity atom and form a stable complex: the divacancy (V–V), the E centre (a vacancy bound to a phosphorus donor, V–P), and the A centre (a vacancy bound to an interstitial oxygen atom, V–O). These complexes, not the vacancies and interstitials themselves, carry most of the electrical activity of irradiated silicon. Which complexes form depends on the dopant and impurity content of the silicon, which is why the doping type returns in §4.

The recoil spectrum, not the identity of the incident particle, sets the defect structure. Marshall and Marshall, summarizing Dale et al., point out that the recoil spectrum of a 60 MeV electron resembles that of a 10 MeV proton more closely than a 10 MeV proton's resembles a 60 MeV proton's. A particle species is a poor label for displacement damage; the fraction of the non-ionizing energy delivered below and above the cascade threshold is a good one, and §4 uses it.

3 · Defects, carrier lifetime and bipolar gain

A stable defect matters electrically because it places allowed electron states inside the bandgap, where the perfect crystal has none. Those states exchange carriers with the conduction and valence bands, and the exchange takes one of five forms. Which form dominates depends on where in the gap the level lies and on the carrier populations around it — a depleted region, a neutral region under injection, a charge packet in transit — and each form maps onto a device parameter. This section names the five, derives the lifetime law that governs the most consequential of them, and carries it into the bipolar transistor, where it becomes the Messenger–Spratt relation.

The complexes of §2 each sit at a characteristic depth. The E centre lies about 0.44 eV below the conduction band, as does a level of the divacancy, and the A centre about 0.17 eV below it (Hopkinson and Mohammadzadeh). A level near midgap, about 0.55 eV from either band in silicon, is the most effective at generating and recombining carriers, because the two steps through it — valence band to level, level to conduction band — then cost equal energies, and neither is the bottleneck. Marshall and Marshall, after Gover and Srour, list the five processes by which such a level acts.

  1. Generation. An electron is thermally excited from the valence band to the level and then emitted to the conduction band, creating an electron–hole pair in two steps of about half the gap each. In a depletion region the field separates the pair before it can recombine, so every generated pair is current: leakage in a reverse-biased junction, dark current in an imaging pixel.
  2. Recombination. A carrier of one sign is captured at the level and, before it is re-emitted, a carrier of the other sign is captured as well, annihilating the pair. The minority-carrier lifetime is set by this rate, so it degrades every device that relies on minority carriers surviving a transit: bipolar gain, solar-cell current, photodetector responsivity.
  3. Trapping. A carrier is captured and later re-emitted to the band it came from. Nothing is lost permanently, but the carrier is withheld from its signal for the level's emission time — the mechanism of charge transfer inefficiency in a CCD (§6).
  4. Compensation. Deep levels capture majority carriers supplied by the shallow dopants and remove them from conduction without any recombination. The net carrier concentration falls and the resistivity rises. This is carrier removal, and it acts first on lightly doped regions, such as the collector of a bipolar transistor.
  5. Tunnelling. A level in the gap of a high-field junction lets carriers cross the barrier in two shorter tunnelling steps, raising the reverse current. It matters most in small-bandgap materials and at high fields.

The five do not act at the same fluence. Semiconductor material is normally doped at least an order of magnitude more heavily than its density of recombination and generation centres, and irradiation adds to the second population, not the first. Minority-carrier lifetime and generation lifetime therefore degrade long before the majority-carrier concentration does, and mobility suffers only at very high damage levels (Marshall and Marshall). That ordering is why displacement damage degrades bipolar transistors, solar cells, photodetectors, imagers and some optocouplers, and leaves most MOS circuits — which conduct by majority carriers across a short channel — largely unaffected. Charge-coupled and charge-injection devices are the MOS exception, because they depend on generation lifetime and on trapping. In orbit, displacement damage usually sets the useful life of imagers and solar cells (§6). A discrete bipolar transistor in orbit is usually degraded more by total dose than by displacement damage; its role in displacement-damage studies is that of a test vehicle, and among bipolar parts displacement damage remains a particular concern in analog integrated circuits (Marshall and Marshall). Deck 03 p. 34 names the affected applications as solar cells, CCDs and bipolar devices, and Deck 02 p. 23 adds other minority-carrier devices; the sister course's EEL5385 M11 §§6–7 tabulates the same mapping device by device.

Recombination through a single defect level proceeds at a rate proportional to the defect density, so the recombination lifetime is τ = 1/(σvthNt), with σ the capture cross-section for the minority carrier, vth its thermal velocity and Nt the defect density (IEEE NSREC 2024 Short Course, Part I). Irradiation adds defects at a rate proportional to fluence, and independent recombination paths add their rates, so the reciprocal lifetime grows linearly with fluence.

1τ = 1τ0 + ΦKτ
(12.5)
τ minority-carrier (recombination) lifetime after irradiation (s) · τ0 lifetime before irradiation (s) · Φ particle fluence (cm⁻²) · Kτ lifetime damage constant (cm⁻²·s, particles·s per cm²), specific to the particle and its energy and dependent on the material, its doping, the injection level, the temperature and the time since irradiation (§5); a larger Kτ is a harder material. Since the diffusion length is L = (Dτ)1/2, with D the minority-carrier diffusion coefficient (cm²/s), the same law reads 1/L² = 1/L0² + Φ/(DKτ), the form used for solar cells and photodetectors. Linear in fluence while the defect introduction rate and the injection level stay fixed; very low and very high fluences can depart from it. On the formula sheet as (12.5).

In a bipolar transistor the law acts on the base. M06 §7 divided the base current into back-injection into the emitter, recombination in the emitter–base depletion region, and recombination in the neutral base. Take the last. The minority carriers injected into the base form a stored charge QB that crosses the base in the transit time τt, so the collector current is QB/τt; the same charge recombines at the rate QB/τ, which is the neutral-base recombination current. Their ratio, τt/τ, is this component's contribution to 1/β. Substituting (12.5), and writing the transit time through the cutoff frequency, τt ≈ 1/(2πft) when base transit dominates the transistor's delay, gives the reciprocal-gain law in the form Deck 04 p. 35 and Deck 05 p. 6 state it, after Messenger and Ash.

1β = 1β0 + K1Φn2πft
(12.6)
β common-emitter current gain after irradiation, dimensionless · β0 gain before irradiation · K1 damage constant (cm²/(n·s)), which depends on the base resistivity; in the derivation above it is 1/Kτ of the base material · Φn fast-neutron fluence (n/cm²) · ft cutoff frequency (Hz). Derived here for recombination in the neutral base; the same reciprocal form holds when the lifetime that matters is that of the emitter–base depletion region, with a different constant (Schrimpf, course textbook). Applies over the fluence range where 1/β is linear, at fixed bias and temperature. On the formula sheet as (12.6).

Three consequences follow. The reciprocal gain, not the gain, is linear in fluence, so degradation data are plotted as Δ(1/β) against Φ and the damage constant is read from the slope, while the gain itself falls hyperbolically. The cutoff frequency sits in the denominator, so a transistor with a narrow base — short transit time, high ft — degrades less at the same fluence, which is Deck 04 p. 35's statement that high-speed bipolar transistors degrade less because the base is narrower. And nothing in the derivation is specific to neutrons: the same law describes displacement damage by protons, heavy ions and electrons, each with its own constant, which is what allows a damage factor to be expressed as a 1 MeV equivalent neutron fluence and converted between particles through NIEL (§4).

A transistor with β₀ = 100 falls to β = 50 after a fast-neutron fluence. A second transistor of the same base material and the same β₀, with ten times the cutoff frequency, receives the same fluence. What is its gain, and why is it not 95?

Work in reciprocal gain, which (12.6) makes linear in fluence. The first transistor moved from 1/100 = 0.010 to 1/50 = 0.020, so Δ(1/β) = 0.010. With K1 and Φn unchanged and ft ten times larger, Δ(1/β) is ten times smaller, 0.001, so 1/β = 0.011 and β ≈ 91. Scaling the gain loss itself — 50 lost, so 5 lost — gives 95, but the gain is not linear in fluence; its reciprocal is.

Check where the constant sits before substituting a tabulated value. The course, and Schrimpf's chapter in the course textbook, put the damage constant in the numerator, as in (12.6). The published Messenger–Spratt relation, and the damage constants tabulated from it, put it in the denominator: Δ(1/β) = Φn/(ωTK), with ωT = 2πft and K in n·s/cm², so K1 = 1/K. Marshall and Marshall absorb everything into one factor, Δ(1/β) = K(E)Φ. Substituting a tabulated K for K1 inverts the answer. The relation also has a regime: the 2024 NSREC Short Course lists heavy damage, a change of bias, operation at a temperature other than the irradiation temperature, short base widths, high doping and recombination in the emitter–base region as conditions under which it can mispredict; the last three are from Barnaby et al. (2017).

Displacement damage and total dose both raise the base current of a bipolar transistor while leaving the collector current alone, so both lower the gain, but they act in different places. Total dose acts at the surface: interface traps where the emitter–base depletion region meets the passivating oxide raise the surface recombination velocity (M08 §9), and the excess base current carries an ideality factor near 2. Displacement damage acts in the bulk: it shortens the lifetime throughout the neutral base, raising the ideality-1 component, and in the bulk of the emitter–base depletion region. Studies that isolate displacement damage use the separation deliberately. They pre-irradiate transistors with cobalt-60 to multi-megarad levels, to saturate the ionization damage before the particle exposure, and avoid low-current bias, where surface currents dominate (Marshall and Marshall).

Protons deliver both kinds of damage at once, and the two are not simply additive. Pease's chapter in the course textbook notes that a laboratory simulation with cobalt-60 and neutrons must irradiate the same samples consecutively for that reason, and Schrimpf's chapter records that proton irradiation can make linear bipolar circuits fail at much lower total doses than gamma irradiation, sometimes by a different failure mechanism (Rax, Johnston and Miyahira, 1999). Johnston gives an example: an LM137 negative voltage regulator irradiated with 50 MeV protons failed catastrophically at equivalent total doses of 18–35 krad(Si), through degradation of its start-up circuit, a failure mode that did not occur in the equivalent cobalt-60 tests.

4 · NIEL scaling and where it fails

NIEL is worth tabulating only if device degradation scales with it, whatever particle delivered it. M04 §6 stated this NIEL scaling hypothesis. This section states what it assumes physically, where the course's sources find it holding, and the three places they find it failing.

ΔP ∝ Φ · NIEL,    ΔP = kdamage · DDD
(12.7)
ΔP change in a device parameter within its linear range — Δ(1/β), 1/τ − 1/τ0, a dark-current increase, a change in CTI, a loss of solar-cell current · Φ fluence (cm⁻²) · NIEL (MeV·cm²/g) · kdamage damage constant for that device and parameter, in the parameter's units per MeV/g · DDD from (12.1), MeV/g. A damage factor measured with one particle at one energy converts to another by the ratio of their NIEL values. Equivalently, an environment is quoted as the fluence of a reference particle that delivers the same DDD — 1 MeV equivalent neutrons, or 10 MeV equivalent protons. NIEL is also quoted as a displacement cross-section in MeV·mb: 100 MeV·mb = 2.144 keV·cm²/g in silicon. The proportionality is Deck 03 p. 41's; the equality is the form Hopkinson and Mohammadzadeh give in the course textbook. On the formula sheet as (12.7).

NIEL measures the energy that goes into creating vacancy–interstitial pairs, and about 90% of those pairs recombine. For degradation to scale with it, two things must hold: the fraction of pairs that survives must not depend on the recoil energy, and a surviving defect must affect the device in the same way whether it came from an isolated pair or from inside a cluster (Marshall and Marshall, after Dale et al., 1988). Hopkinson and Mohammadzadeh put the condition parameter by parameter: scaling holds when the concentration of the defect that dominates the parameter is the same for point damage and for cluster damage. Huhtinen showed that it is for the divacancy, while higher vacancy complexes may be more common in clusters. The correlation is therefore an empirical result, checked for each material, device and parameter.

It holds remarkably often. Summers et al. (1993) found proton damage coefficients linear in NIEL for silicon, GaAs and InP devices. Bipolar transistor damage factors measured with protons, deuterons and helium ions, normalized to fission neutrons, match the calculated NIEL ratios over a wide energy range with no fitted parameter (Summers et al., 1987, as reproduced by Marshall and Marshall). And in GaAs solar cells the damage coefficients of the short-circuit current for electrons and protons lie close to one line across five decades of NIEL on the log-log plot of Deck 02 p. 24. The 2024 NSREC Short Course states the consequence as equal loss of output power for equal displacement damage dose from either particle (after Messenger et al.), and that correlation is what allows a solar array to be specified in displacement damage dose rather than particle by particle. For electrons the correlation holds only after a correction to NIEL, which the box after Figure 7 sets out.

One line on a log-log plot is not equal damage per unit NIEL. Deck 02 p. 24 draws the GaAs electron and proton data on one line, and the 2024 Short Course treats electron and proton displacement damage dose in GaAs cells as interchangeable; that is the version the course uses. Marshall and Marshall qualify it on two counts. A plot spanning five decades on both axes hides departures of a factor of 2–3, which matter in a prediction; on this page's digitization, the slope near 1.1 in Figure 7 already amounts to a factor of about 3 between the two ends of the line. And Walters et al. (1999), re-analysing the same GaAs data set, recommend separate fits for the two particles. The maximum power after proton irradiation correlates with NIEL from 0.2 to 9.5 MeV, but the electron data agree best over the widest energy range with NIEL raised to the power 1.7, so the equivalent 1 MeV electron displacement damage dose is defined as fluence times NIEL1.7, not by (12.1). The 2024 Short Course's own figure for this result, its Fig. 10, plots the electron data against a separate axis of 1 MeV electron equivalent dose, and its electron and proton curves have the same shape without coinciding on one dose axis. An electron dose and a proton dose computed with plain NIEL are therefore equivalent only after that correction.

The course's sources record three departures, and all three involve the arrangement of the damage rather than its amount.

Electrons in silicon depend on the doping type (Deck 02 p. 24). Electron damage coefficients in n-type silicon lie on the extension of the proton line; in p-type silicon they lie below it and rise roughly as the square of NIEL. The exception arises under exactly the condition that tests the scaling assumption hardest. An electron's damage consists entirely of isolated point defects (§1), and what an isolated vacancy becomes depends on the impurities it meets as it migrates (§2), which differ between phosphorus-doped and boron-doped silicon.

Low-energy protons in n-type silicon damage more than NIEL predicts. Deck 03 p. 39 (left panel, after Dale et al., 1989) plots proton damage factors, normalized to those of 1 MeV neutrons, against NIEL. The p-type 2N2222A transistor follows the unity-slope line. The n-type devices — the 2N2970A transistor and a charge-injection device — follow a steeper upper line toward high NIEL, which on that plot means low proton energy. Dale et al. (1988) found that the size of such departures correlates with the fraction of the NIEL carried by recoils below 1 keV: well-separated Frenkel pairs from low-energy recoils recombine less than pairs created inside a dense cascade, so a spectrum rich in low-energy recoils leaves more surviving defects per unit NIEL (Marshall and Marshall). M04 §6 draws the silicon panel.

GaAs departs at high proton energy. In the right panel of Deck 03 p. 39, measured damage factors normalized to the GaAs NIEL calculation at 10 MeV fall progressively below it above about 40 MeV. Normalized at a high energy instead, the same data would show NIEL underestimating the damage at low energy; the two descriptions are equivalent, and Barry et al. (1995) explained their GaAs light-emitting-diode data the same way as the silicon case, by a recombination efficiency of Frenkel pairs that varies with recoil energy. Close to the displacement threshold further deviations are expected, but protons of those energies contribute little to the damage behind typical spacecraft shielding.

The GaAs line and the GaAs departure do not conflict. Deck 02 p. 24 shows GaAs solar-cell damage on one line; Deck 03 p. 39 shows GaAs damage falling below NIEL. Marshall and Marshall point out that the solar-cell data behind the first figure come from protons below 20 MeV — the energies relevant to lightly shielded solar arrays — while the departure begins above 40 MeV, and that a later analysis of the same solar-cell data set shows the damage coefficients falling below the GaAs NIEL at higher energies. The choice of damage function has practical weight: depending on orbit and shielding, the predicted displacement damage dose for a GaAs device can differ by a factor of two to three between calculated NIEL and measured damage factors, and one CCD study found a factor of two in predicted on-orbit degradation. Their recommendation follows: a device highly sensitive to displacement damage should be tested with protons.

5 · Annealing of displacement damage

Displacement damage is not frozen at the moment of irradiation. The defect population keeps evolving after the exposure ends, and a device parameter measured after a burst of particles recovers in stages, quickly at first and then ever more slowly. Silicon anneals at room temperature; unbiased GaAs essentially does not. The time between exposure and measurement is therefore part of any displacement-damage result, and it decides how a test relates to the application.

The earliest stages are over before any electrical measurement can be made. Most close Frenkel pairs recombine in less than a millisecond, and the surviving vacancies, mobile even at liquid-nitrogen temperature, settle into stable complexes on a timescale of seconds (Marshall and Marshall). Deck 03 p. 38, after Srour, Marshall and Marshall (2003), follows what comes next in a device held at room temperature. After a neutron burst the carrier lifetime, or the gain of a transistor, drops abruptly and then recovers. The recovery period, short-term annealing, begins shortly after the damage is created and is essentially complete within several minutes to one hour. A long-term anneal then recovers part of the remaining damage slowly, over a period the slide carries out to a year. The slide puts the damage left after short-term annealing in quotation marks as "permanent" because it goes on recovering, only slowly, and it names the mechanism: recombination, or annihilation, and rearrangement of defects.

Two clocks for "short-term". Marshall and Marshall use the phrase for the evolution of Frenkel pairs into stable room-temperature defects and put it within about a second, while Deck 03 p. 38 shows device parameters still recovering for minutes to an hour after a neutron burst. Recovery at the device level depends on temperature and on carrier injection, which accelerates annealing (below), so the time over which it is observed depends on the device and its operating state. This page follows Deck 03 p. 38 for device-level recovery.

The stable complexes differ in thermal stability — Marshall and Marshall give the divacancy as stable to about 300 °C, the E centre to about 150 °C and the A centre to about 350 °C — so heating removes them selectively, and the defect a parameter depends on sets the temperature needed to recover it. Annealing out the traps that degrade charge transfer in a CCD requires more than 100 °C (Hopkinson and Mohammadzadeh). The same defect can also anneal differently according to how it was made: E centres produced by 1 MeV electrons anneal at a significantly lower temperature than those produced by protons (Marshall and Marshall), a difference between point and cluster damage that NIEL, which counts only energy, cannot see. The material matters as well. Silicon anneals at room temperature, while GaAs does not anneal appreciably unless heated to 200 °C or more (IEEE NSREC 2024 Short Course, after Swift et al., 2003).

Current through a forward-biased junction accelerates annealing in silicon, GaAs and GaN (IEEE NSREC 2024 Short Course). In GaAs light-emitting diodes and laser diodes the effect is decisive: little or no annealing takes place until current is applied, and recovery begins when it is (Johnston). Marshall and Marshall attribute this to recombination-enhanced defect reactions — the energy released when a carrier pair recombines non-radiatively through the defect moves the defect itself — rather than to heating of the junction. A laser diode operated under forward bias during irradiation therefore anneals while it is being damaged, and degrades less at a low particle flux than at a laboratory one.

Three consequences follow for testing. First, a damage constant is incomplete without the time and temperature of its measurement: a reading taken within the short-term window shows more damage than persists, and the universal dark-current damage factor of §6 is defined at 300 K one week after irradiation, after most of the annealing has run. Second, the test and the application can run on different clocks. A pulsed exposure such as a neutron burst is followed at once by the circuit's operation, so the early, unannealed damage is what the circuit experiences; a spacecraft accumulates its fluence over months, and each increment completes its short-term recovery long before the mission ends. Third, because long-term room-temperature annealing is usually small, a device can often be exposed passively and characterized in the laboratory later, and its degradation is generally independent of bias during irradiation. Compound-semiconductor photonic devices, whose degradation and annealing depend on bias, are the stated exception (Marshall and Marshall), and an unbiased GaAs device keeps its damage until it is powered.

6 · Imagers and solar cells

Imaging arrays and solar cells are the devices in which displacement damage most often sets the useful life. An imager reads out charge packets from millions of nominally identical pixels, so a single generation centre in one pixel, or a single trap in a transfer channel, is visible in the image. A solar cell converts light into current only through minority carriers that survive a diffusion path to its junction. Both depend on the silicon or GaAs bulk. Solar cells sit behind only a thin cover glass. Imagers in space are often heavily shielded, which shifts the protons that reach them to energies of typically 40–100 MeV (Marshall and Marshall), but they are sensitive enough that the protons that do get through still set their useful life. Each process of §3 appears here in a specific device symptom.

Charge transfer in a CCD

M06 §9 introduced the charge-coupled device: signal charge held in potential wells under clocked gates and moved packet by packet along a buried channel to one output amplifier, with the charge transfer inefficiency CTI = 1 − CTE the fraction left behind at each transfer, compounded along the chain by (6.7). Displacement damage degrades a CCD in four ways: it lowers the CTE, raises the average dark current, creates individual pixels of very high dark current, and increases the noise of the output amplifier (Marshall and Marshall). Of these, CTI is the one most directly tied to displacement damage. Total dose acts on a CCD through its gate dielectrics: it shifts flat-band voltages and raises surface dark current through interface traps (M08 §9; Hopkinson and Mohammadzadeh §2). The traps that capture signal charge in the buried channel are bulk defects, which only displacement damage creates. The dominant such trap at moderately high operating temperatures has a level about 0.44 eV below the conduction band and is attributed to the E centre, on its energy and its annealing behaviour; it forms because the usual buried channel is n-type, doped with phosphorus (Hopkinson and Mohammadzadeh). The two dark-current effects, which displacement damage also produces directly, follow in the next subsection.

A trap degrades transfer through a race between two time constants. Its capture time, τc = 1/(σvthns), with ns the local density of signal electrons, is shortest at the dense centre of a packet and depends only weakly on temperature. Its emission time grows as exp(Et/kT), with Et the depth of the level below the conduction band, and so depends strongly on temperature. Charge is lost from a packet when a trap captures within the packet's dwell time in the pixel and re-emits only after the packet has moved on; the released electrons join a later packet. The image effect is trailing: charge smeared behind bright features along the transfer direction, seen as streaks. Because capture needs the packet's own electrons, a small signal loses a larger fraction than a large one; because emission is thermally activated, the temperature and the clock rate decide which traps matter. A linear CCD clocked at 1 MHz or faster is relatively immune, since the E centre's capture is too slow for the short dwell time (Marshall and Marshall).

The tolerances are severe. Keeping signal loss below 10% over 1000 transfers requires a CTE of at least 0.9999 per transfer, which for a pixel of about 50 µm³ means fewer than one radiation-induced defect in every ten pixels (Marshall and Marshall), and CTI becomes a major effect at equivalent 10 MeV proton fluences of about 10¹⁰ cm⁻² (Hopkinson and Mohammadzadeh). Deck 03 p. 44 shows the dependence on signal size and on background for an EEV CCD02 after 7.2×10⁹ protons/cm² at 10 MeV, a fluence corresponding to about 4 krad(Si). With a dark-charge background of 150 electrons the CTI approaches 5×10⁻⁴ for the smallest signals and falls as the signal grows; a background of 16,000 electrons holds it near 7×10⁻⁵ at every signal size, because the background keeps the traps filled and they cannot interact with the signal packet. The improvement usually comes at the price of added noise, the background's own shot noise. Marshall and Marshall compare the two cumulative effects in scientific CCDs: flat-band shifts and dark-current increases at total doses below 10–20 krad(Si) are often manageable by adjusting voltages and temperature, while significant CTE loss appears at proton exposures of less than 1 krad(Si).

The countermeasures follow from the trap physics. Cooling, often to about −80 °C, lengthens the E centre's emission time until filled traps stay filled; a deliberately raised background charge in every pixel, called a fat zero, or a charge packet injected ahead of the signal, fills them electrically; a narrow implanted notch confines small packets to a smaller volume and so to fewer traps; a p-channel CCD, whose boron-doped channel contains no E centres, is harder; and an active pixel sensor, which reads each pixel in place instead of transferring charge, has no CTI at all (Marshall and Marshall; Hopkinson and Mohammadzadeh). Where none of these suffices the remaining option is shielding. Behind a thick tantalum shield, the secondary neutrons produced in the shield itself come to dominate the displacement damage dose beyond about 2 cm (Marshall and Marshall).

Dark current, hot pixels and random telegraph signals

The second symptom is generation, process 1 of §3. A defect in a pixel's depletion region generates electron–hole pairs thermally, and the pixel collects them as dark signal. Averaged over an array, the increase is proportional to the displacement damage dose and to the depleted volume, with a constant that holds across devices, particles and doping.

The mean hides the feature that matters in an image. Displacement damage arrives as discrete events, a few of them large, so the dark current varies strongly from pixel to pixel. Pixels that received a nuclear reaction, or whose defect sits in a region of high electric field — about 10⁵ V/cm, where the field lowers the barrier for emission and multiplies the generation rate — become hot pixels, dark-current spikes an order of magnitude or more above the mean. Field-enhanced pixels show a lower activation energy, as low as about 0.4 eV against 0.63–0.65 eV for ordinary generation, so they fade less on cooling and dominate cold images (Hopkinson and Mohammadzadeh). The displacement damage dose sets the mean; the shape of the distribution depends on the recoil spectrum and on the field inside each pixel.

The shape follows from counting statistics. Goiffon models the number of damaging interactions in a pixel as Poisson-distributed, with a mean proportional to DDD × Vdep, and the dark current each contributes as exponentially distributed; a pixel's dark current is the sum. At low fluence most pixels receive no interaction or one, and the damaged pixels form an exponential tail above the undamaged population. As the number of interactions per pixel grows, the sum of many independent contributions approaches a Gaussian by the central limit theorem, and its width relative to its mean falls as the inverse square root of the number of events. Deck 03 p. 45 shows the approach, from Marshall and Marshall (after Marshall et al. and Dale et al.): dark-current histograms of a 256 × 256 charge-injection device after 12 MeV protons at 4×10¹⁰, 1×10¹¹ and 2×10¹¹ cm⁻², with N = 1967, 4918 and 9835 primary interactions per pixel and Ni = 1.8, 4.5 and 9.0 inelastic interactions per pixel. The thousands of elastic interactions add a nearly fixed amount to every pixel; the skew comes from the few inelastic reactions, each of which deposits a large damage energy in one pixel. The high-energy tail is conspicuous at the lowest fluence, and by the highest the distribution is nearly symmetric — the tail is the low-fluence signature of single-particle damage, not a feature that grows with exposure. Marshall and Marshall add that this nonuniformity is inherent in the statistics of the collisions and cannot be hardened against.

Between the lowest and highest fluence of Deck 03 p. 45 the fluence rises five-fold. By roughly what factor should the width of the dark-current distribution, relative to its peak position, fall, and does the slide bear it out?

The mean damage energy per pixel is proportional to the number of interactions, and so to fluence. For a sum of independent events whose number is Poisson-distributed, the variance is also proportional to that number, so the width grows only as its square root, and the width relative to the mean falls as one over the square root of the fluence: by √5 ≈ 2.2. Read from the slide, the full width at half maximum divided by the peak position is about 0.47 at 4×10¹⁰ cm⁻² and about 0.21 at 2×10¹¹ cm⁻², a factor of about 2.2.

The shape of the distribution also separates the two cumulative effects in an imager. Total dose raises the dark current of every pixel through interface traps at much the same rate, shifting and broadening the whole distribution as a pedestal; displacement damage produces the high-side tail of hot pixels (M08 §9).

Some damaged pixels do not hold a steady dark current. Their generation rate switches between two or more discrete levels at random times, a random telegraph signal, so a dark frame taken for calibration no longer predicts the next exposure. Hopkinson and Mohammadzadeh report it in all proton-irradiated CCDs and active pixel sensors examined, over proton energies from at least 1.5 to 60 MeV, with switching times near room temperature from seconds to hours, mostly of order minutes, lengthening on cooling. In a CCD the probability of such a defect is about 1.6×10⁻⁵ per incident 10 MeV proton, so after 10¹⁰ protons/cm² about 40% of 20 µm × 20 µm pixels show it. Goiffon adds that displacement damage is more effective than ionization at producing high-amplitude, multilevel switching. The defects responsible are bistable or metastable, and Hopkinson and Mohammadzadeh note that they had not been identified.

The proton hits in the SOHO images are ionization, not displacement damage. Deck 03 p. 43 appears among the displacement-damage slides, under the statement that protons can damage sensitive CCDs, and shows coronagraph images from the LASCO instrument on the SOHO spacecraft taken during the coronal mass ejection of 6 November 1997; protons from the event reach the CCD and pepper the lower two images. Those specks are single-event transients. Each proton generates electron–hole pairs along its track, the pixels it crosses collect that charge as though it were signal, and the event appears in the frame being integrated as a bright pixel or, for an oblique track, a short streak; when the charge exceeds a pixel's full-well capacity it spills along the column, which is blooming. The next readout clears it. Hopkinson and Mohammadzadeh describe such events as short-lived and confined to the exposure in which they arrive (M06 §9, M08 §9, and M11 §§1–2 for charge deposition and collection). The same protons do deposit displacement damage, but it appears as hot pixels that persist from frame to frame and as a gradual loss of transfer efficiency, not as the scattered specks of a single frame.

Solar cells

A solar cell collects the minority carriers that light generates within about a diffusion length L = (Dτ)1/2 of its junction; carriers generated deeper recombine before they reach it. Displacement damage shortens τ, the diffusion length falls as 1/L² = 1/L0² + Φ/(DKτ) by (12.5), and the short-circuit current, and with it the output power, falls with fluence (IEEE NSREC 2024 Short Course; Deck 02 p. 24 for Jsc in GaAs cells). Solar arrays are the least shielded electronics on a spacecraft, behind only a thin cover glass, so they see low-energy protons that heavier shielding would stop, and energetic electrons as well: Johnston notes that the cover glass is ineffective against energetic electrons, which therefore damage solar cells along with protons.

The NIEL correlation for GaAs cells (§4) is what makes displacement damage dose a workable basis for predicting their degradation. The proton spectrum reduces to one dose by (12.1), the electron spectrum to one equivalent 1 MeV electron dose computed with NIEL raised to the power 1.7 (the correction in the box after Figure 7), and measured characteristic curves of power against dose convert each dose into a power loss (Marshall and Marshall, after Walters et al., 1999). The 2024 NSREC Short Course cites Messenger et al. (2001) for the dose method, a study that compares it with the alternative of converting the environment into an equivalent fluence of 1 MeV electrons. At high electron fluences a second mechanism appears: carrier removal, process 4 of §3, raises the base resistivity and widens the depletion layer, and Johnston, after Yamaguchi et al. (1999), shows that in silicon cells it can cause a catastrophic failure that models based on lifetime alone do not predict.

Other minority-carrier optoelectronics follow the same logic, with one extreme case. Most light-emitting diodes and laser diodes are relatively hard, because the carrier lifetime in their active regions is already very short (Marshall and Marshall). Amphoterically doped AlGaAs light-emitting diodes, used in optocouplers, are the exception: Johnston reports about half the light output lost after about 2×10¹⁰ protons/cm² at 50 MeV, a fluence equivalent to about 3 krad(Si), with the loss due entirely to displacement damage.

7 · Single-particle displacement damage in nanoscale devices

Every result so far has treated displacement damage as a population: many defects in a volume large enough that their number, not their placement, sets the parameter. That description fails when the device is no larger than one cascade. A single recoil then either forms its cluster inside the sensitive region or it does not, and the damage becomes a single-particle event produced by a cumulative-effect mechanism — the edge case of the classification in M05 §6.

The scales come from §2. A 50 keV silicon recoil spreads its displacements over about 80 nm and concentrates them in terminal clusters about 5 nm across. An imaging pixel of 13–20 µm is 160 to 250 times the length of the whole cascade, so one cascade changes one pixel's dark current by a discrete step within a statistical distribution — which is how single-particle displacement damage was first seen, in the dark current of charge-injection-device pixels each exposed, statistically, to a single particle (IEEE NSREC 2024 Short Course, after P. W. Marshall et al., 1989). Reverse-biased diodes have since shown step changes in leakage current that coincide with the charge transients of single heavy ions (Auden et al., 2012). In a transistor large compared with a cascade, one cluster is too small to move any terminal quantity. Deck 02 p. 25 draws what scaling changed: a nanoscale transistor in an integrated circuit is comparable in size to a subcascade.

At that size one cluster changes one device. The 2024 NSREC Short Course collects the evidence. Heavy-ion displacement damage reduces the drain current of 14 nm FinFETs; weak bits in heavy-ion-irradiated SRAMs built with 5 nm FinFETs correlate with displacement damage rather than with total dose; and simulations of FinFETs from the 22 nm down to the 6 nm node show that a damage cluster at the top of the fin can severely degrade the drain current. In memories the symptom is a bit that no longer behaves. Displacement damage increases the number of stuck bits in SDRAM, and in DRAM both total dose and single-particle displacement damage produce stuck bits and intermittently stuck bits (Goiffon et al., 2020). A DRAM cell holds its bit as charge on a capacitor between refreshes, so a defect cluster that raises the generation current of the storage junction drains the cell early — the generation process of §3, which makes a hot pixel in an imager, acting on a storage node. Deck 02 p. 25 draws the practical conclusion: bad or stuck bits are likely, and error detection and correction (EDAC) is required to avoid or recover from them.

EDAC stores check bits with each data word and recomputes them on every read, so that an error in one bit of the word is detected and corrected. A stuck bit is corrected on every read, but it occupies the code's correction capacity for that word for as long as it persists, and a second error in the same word — a single-event upset, for example — then exceeds what a single-error-correcting code can repair. Error-correcting codes and the other circuit-level defences against single particles belong to the single-event hardening module on the course roadmap.

Deck 02 p. 25 also warns that single-particle displacement damage is often difficult to distinguish from micro-dose and from single-event effects. All three leave one cell permanently or persistently changed after one particle, so the symptom at the memory's terminals is the same. Micro-dose is the ionizing counterpart: an ion crossing the gate oxide of one small transistor deposits enough dose in that oxide alone to shift that transistor, a total-dose effect confined to one device. The stuck bits heavy ions produce in memories have long been attributed to it (E. Normand's chapter in the course textbook; IEEE NSREC 2024 Short Course, Part III). A destructive single event, such as the gate rupture of M11 §10, leaves a third kind of trace, a damaged structure. The three differ in where the trace sits, and the evidence that separates them is the evidence that identifies each kind of trace.

Where this leaves us

Displacement damage has now been followed from a single collision to a failed device. Kinematics fix the largest recoil a particle can make, and the cross-section fixes how often it makes each smaller one: charged particles make mostly low-energy recoils by Coulomb scattering, neutrons and energetic protons make energetic recoils by nuclear scattering and nuclear reactions, and electrons make only point defects. The recoil energy decides whether the damage is an isolated Frenkel pair, a cluster or a tree of subcascades, and about a tenth of the pairs survive to form stable complexes whose levels in the gap generate, recombine, trap, compensate and assist tunnelling.

Those processes map onto device parameters: recombination onto lifetime, and through it onto bipolar gain by the reciprocal-gain law and onto solar-cell current by the diffusion length; generation onto leakage and imager dark current; trapping onto CCD transfer; compensation onto carrier removal. NIEL scaling compresses particle and energy into one number, and it works because, to first order, the surviving fraction and the defect inventory do not depend on the recoil spectrum. It fails where they do: electrons in p-type silicon, low-energy protons in n-type silicon, and GaAs at high proton energy. Electron damage in GaAs solar cells tracks NIEL only after a fitted exponent. The damage anneals in silicon and hardly at all in unbiased GaAs, so every damage constant carries a time and a temperature. When a device shrinks to the size of one cascade, the population picture ends and a single recoil becomes a stuck bit that error correction must absorb.

The next built module, M15, turns to total-dose hardness assurance: how parts are sampled and tested, and why the MOS test method fails for linear bipolar parts. The circuit-hardening modules on the course roadmap take up error correction and the other defences against single particles, and A1 §5 places the displacement-damage literature cited here in the history of the field.
