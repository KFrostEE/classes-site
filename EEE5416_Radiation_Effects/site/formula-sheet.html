Two conventions run through everything below and are the largest single source of wrong answers in this subject. First, dose is always named with its material — rad(Si) and rad(SiO2) are different quantities, because the same fluence deposits different energy per gram in different materials. Second, this subject mixes CGS and SI freely: lengths appear in cm, µm and nm within one problem, capacitance per unit area in F/cm², density in g/cm³ or kg/m³ depending on the equation. Each gloss below states which unit its constant assumes.

Dose and energy deposition
Absorbed dose from an ion fluence and an LET Multiply directly: D = 1.60×10⁻⁵ · LET · Φ. The constant already carries the MeV→J and mg→kg conversions, so LET must be in MeV·cm²/mg and Φ in cm⁻². Answer is in rad of whatever material the LET was quoted for. 10⁷ ions/cm² at an LET of 40 MeV·cm²/mg deposits 6.4 krad — the dose a heavy-ion test delivers along with its upsets. (3.2)
Annual dose from a dose rate, or the reverse Multiply or divide by 3.156×10⁷ s/yr. 1 krad/yr = 3.2×10⁻⁵ rad/s; 1 Mrad/yr = 3.2×10⁻² rad/s. Check the shielding depth is the same on both sides before comparing two dose figures — depth changes the answer as much as orbit does. §15
Dose equivalent in rem or Sv, or a human exposure limit H = wRD. Pick wR by species: 1 photons and electrons, 2 protons, ~20 alphas and heavy ions, 5–20 neutrons. Tissue only. The "1 rem ≈ 1 rad" shortcut belongs to the mixed atmospheric field and to nothing else. (3.3)
Shield thickness to stop electrons of a given energy Katz–Penfold for the range in g/cm², then divide by the density for a length. Use the 0.530E − 0.106 branch above 2.5 MeV. Then check the answer against the dose-depth argument: beyond the electron range the remaining dose is bremsstrahlung and high-energy protons, and more mass buys very little. (3.5), (3.4)
Choice of shield material, or why lead is wrong Compare at equal areal density, not equal thickness. Then apply ZE/700: bremsstrahlung yield scales with the absorber's atomic number, so high Z is worse against electrons. High Z is right only for spot shielding a small part, or as the front layer of a graded stack. (3.6)
LET or stopping power of a particle at a given energy From tables in practice. To reason about it, read equation (4.2): energy loss scales as z²/β², so scale a known value by the charge squared and inversely by the velocity squared. Then divide by density for LET, and check the mass unit — mg/cm³ for MeV·cm²/mg. (4.2), (4.3)
Where an ion stops, or which species a test needs Range from tables in g/cm², divided by density for a depth. Compare against the depth of the active region: the Bragg peak must reach it, and charge collection is greatest when the peak coincides with it. A degrader foil of known areal density translates the peak by a known amount. (4.4), (3.4)
Dose from a photon field Equation (3.2) does not apply — a photon has no LET. Use the mass energy-absorption coefficient of the material at that photon energy, times the energy fluence. Then apply the enhancement factor if a high-Z layer is nearby. (4.7), (4.8)
Dose rate from a measured detector current A reverse-biased p–i–n diode collects every pair it generates, so Iph/q is the pair rate and (Iph/q)Ep the absorbed power. Divide by the mass of the active region, ρAt. Lengths in cm, Ep in joules, then ÷10−5 for rad/s. (4.9)
Converting a dose between materials — rad(Si) to rad(SiO2) Take the ratio of mass energy-absorption coefficients at that photon energy. 0.558 at 10 keV (so 1 rad(SiO2) ≈ 1.8 rad(Si)) and 1.003 at 1.25 MeV, where the two labels are interchangeable. Check whether the question is at X-ray or Co-60 energy before doing anything else. Bulk values — a few-nm gate oxide inside silicon takes the silicon's dose, not this ratio. (4.10)
Electron–hole pairs per cm³ from a dose Either Neh = ρD/Ep in strict SI, or the one-step shortcut Neh = g0D with D in rad. The two agree; the second avoids the unit conversion entirely. (4.5), (4.6)
Dose enhancement near a high-Z layer, or the dose a dielectric beside a metal gate absorbs Ratio of the dose actually deposited in the sensitive region to the equilibrium dose; a measured pair of doses gives it directly, and the dielectric's dose is DEF × the equilibrium dose. For HfO₂ beside TiN under X-rays, M04 §5 gives 2–4× calculated for gate stacks and Deck 07 quotes 2–8× observed in capacitors — stack- and spectrum-specific. DEF ≈ 1 for Co-60. (4.8)
Device response to total dose
Threshold shift from a dose — the standard total-dose calculation Work entirely in cm. Get Cox = εox/tox, then ΔNot = fotfyfDEkgD tox, then ΔVot = −qΔNot/Cox. Read fy off the yield curve for the stated particle and field; take fDE = 1 for Co-60 and >1 for a low-energy X-ray beside high-Z layers. Check the dose is in rad(SiO₂), not rad(Si). The tox² result is first order above about 20 nm; tunnelling neutralization pulls a thinner oxide below it, and below about 5 nm almost no net hole charge remains. (7.2)
Hole trapping efficiency, or the trapped density a source produces Rearrange (7.1). A measurement of ΔVot gives ΔNot through Cox|ΔVot|/q, and (7.1) then returns fot. (7.1)
Trapped hole areal density Not from a dose Pairs generated, times the hole yield, times the thickness over which they are collected. Use tox for the upper bound in which every surviving hole is trapped, or teff when the problem gives an effective transport thickness. (7.1a), (7.1)
Threshold or flat-band shift from trapped charge Qox = qNot, then Cox = εox/tox, then ΔV = −Qox/Cox. Keep everything per cm²: εox in F/cm and tox in cm. Charge away from the silicon interface counts in proportion to its distance from the gate. (7.2a), (6.2)
Trap density Not or Nit from a measured shift Run the chain backwards: N = Cox|ΔV|/q, with the oxide-trap or the interface-trap component of the shift. The interface-trap result counts only the states charged over the part of the gap the shift spans. (7.5)
Oxide-trap and interface-trap shifts separated The midgap technique. The midgap shift is the oxide-trap shift; ΔVit = ΔVth − ΔVmg for a transistor. For a capacitor the second point is inversion or flat band, and the two intervals count opposite halves of the gap — say which one a quoted value spans. (7.4), (7.5)
Interface-trap density from a charge-pumping measurement Dit = ICP/(qAGfΔΨs). Gate area in cm². Use it rather than the midgap method when the subthreshold region is not straight. (7.6)
Which Vth terms radiation can move Only the oxide-charge and interface-trap term. φMS, 2φF and Qdep are fixed by materials and doping. For the net sign: p-channel shifts add, n-channel shifts oppose and can rebound positive with time. (8.3), (8.4)
Which bias during irradiation is worst Not the highest field. The measured shift is a rising yield times a capture probability falling as Eox−1/2, so it peaks near 1–2 MV/cm. For a SiO₂ gate oxide the worst case is a positive gate at the operating field: irradiate at the worst-case operating bias, since grounded pins under-test. (7.3)
Drain current, or which of the three signatures a post-rad curve shows Translation is ΔVT; change of slope is interface traps; lower saturated value is mobility. State the VT extraction convention. (6.3)
Subthreshold swing after interface traps build up (6.4) with Cit = qDit: Dit in cm⁻²eV⁻¹ and a single factor of q. The course's worked solutions drop Cd: S = S0(1 + qDit/Cox), with S0 = 59.5 mV/decade at 300 K (quoted as 60). The increase the traps add is the same in both forms. Then feed S into the leakage ratio. (6.4)
Mobility after irradiation Empirical: μ = μ0/(1 + αitΔNit + αotΔNot), with αit the larger. The α values are fitted per technology, so the equation predicts a trend, not an absolute value. (6.5)
Leakage increase from a threshold shift Subthreshold current is exponential in gate overdrive. Use exp(ΔV/(nVT)) if given the ideality factor, or 10ΔV/S if given the swing. The ratio is an increase only for a shift toward turn-on; a shift the other way divides the current by the same factor. (8.1)
Whether field-oxide charge inverts the surface Sheet charge → field → voltage across the field oxide: σs = qNox, E = σs/εox, V = EtFOX, with tFOX in cm. Compare against the parasitic transistor's own threshold, 2φF + |Qdep|/CFOX, not against 2φF alone — the depletion term dominates. Under 500 nm the surface inverts at about 1.1×10¹¹ cm⁻² of trapped charge for NA = 10¹⁵ cm⁻³, and 1.1×10¹² cm⁻² for a 10¹⁷ cm⁻³ channel stop. (8.2)

Oxide charge in time, temperature and field
Recovery after annealing — oxide-trap charge or shift remaining after a time, given a time constant The single exponential is the course's calculation form: ΔNot(t) = ΔNot,0e−t/τ. Convert hours to seconds first — τ is quoted in seconds and belongs to one temperature. Checkpoints: 37% remains at 1τ, 10% at 2.3τ, 1% at 4.6τ. It describes one trap level; a real oxide anneals as ln t over many decades (M09 §5), so a fitted τ is not extrapolated far outside its range. (9.1)
How far tunnelling neutralization has reached, or how long it takes to reach a depth The front advances ln10/(2β) per decade of time, about 0.2 nm per decade: one more nanometre costs five decades. The course's rule of thumb fixes the position as well: 2.5 nm at about 1 s, 0.25 nm per decade (Deck 07 p. 19). It advances from both electrodes and is nearly independent of temperature, so it still acts at 77 K. (9.2), (10.6)
Which trapped holes are emptied thermally at a given temperature and time, or the effect of heating on oxide-trap annealing T in kelvin. The front advances (kT/q)ln10 per decade of time — 59.5 mV at 300 K, 74 mV at 100 °C, 15 mV at 77 K — and lies deeper in proportion to T at fixed time. (9.3)
Whether an anneal at a given temperature removes interface traps Interface traps anneal significantly only above about 125 °C. Below that, heating completes their buildup: a 100 °C bake carries them to their plateau while the trapped holes anneal, the worst case for a positive n-channel shift. At 77 K interface traps barely form and do not anneal, thermal annealing of trapped holes stops, and only tunnelling near the electrodes goes on. (9.1), M09 §9
Scaling a radiation-induced interface-trap density between oxide thicknesses Multiply by (t2/t1)n, with n = 1.65 for irradiation at +2 MV/cm and 1.01 at −2 MV/cm. Above about 12 nm only; thinner oxides fall far below the law. (9.4)
Compensating electron charge, or fe/fh, from TSC and C–V on one capacitor Q(+) = ∫ΔITSCdt, after the leakage baseline is subtracted, counts all the trapped holes only when the ramp bias sweeps released holes across the whole oxide (gate negative during the ramp after a positive-bias irradiation); Q(net) = −CoxΔVmg with Cox the device capacitance in F; Q(−) = Q(+) − Q(net); fe/fh = Q(−)/Q(+). Divide by the gate area and by q for cm⁻². The net shift reports Nh(1 − fe/fh). (10.9), (7.4)
Gate-oxide growth and conduction
Oxide thickness after thermal oxidation (time, temperature, ambient) Work in µm and hours, with τ = 0 for bare silicon. With only B/A given, the linear law x = (B/A)(t + τ) is the only route, and it is an upper bound. Single-event effects
Charge deposited by an ion, or collected from an LET and a depth (11.1) returns charge per micrometre of track: 10.4 fC/µm per unit LET in silicon. Multiply by the collection depth in µm — depletion width, film thickness or the given sensitive depth. LET in MeV·cm²/mg, ρ in g/cm³, Ep in eV. (11.1)
Threshold LET from a critical charge and a collection depth Set (11.1) × d equal to Qcrit: in silicon Lth ≈ Qcrit[fC]/(10.4·d[µm]), in MeV·cm²/mg. (11.1), (11.4)
Critical charge, or whether a strike upsets a node Qcrit = CnVcrit from the node capacitance and the voltage excursion that flips it; 1 fF × 1 V = 1 fC. Upset when Qcoll > Qcrit. Qcrit is a property of the technology and the circuit, never of the ion, and is a constant only when the node integrates the whole pulse. (11.3), (11.4)

Parameter degradation scales with the same product, which is what converts a neutron result to a proton result: the ratio of the two NIEL values is the scaling factor. Check that the particle and target are not among the known failures of the scaling. (12.1), (12.7)

2 · How solutions work in this subject

What is true of a solution regardless of which equation it uses.

The chain every calculation walks along

Energy deposited → charge generated → charge that survives recombination → charge that is trapped or collected → a device parameter that moves → a circuit that fails. Almost every problem in the course is one or two consecutive links of that chain. Identify which two links the question spans, and the equation follows.

Three quantities carry cm² and are distinct. LET is MeV·cm²/mg — an energy loss normalised by areal density. Cross-section is cm²/device or cm²/bit — an effective target area. NIEL is MeV·cm²/g — the non-ionizing counterpart of LET. Dose carries no cm² at all: it is energy per unit mass, rad or Gy.

Ionizing and non-ionizing energy loss are separate accounts. The same particle deposits into both. Ionizing loss feeds total dose and single-event charge; non-ionizing loss feeds displacement damage. Only a very small fraction of the deposited energy goes into displacements, and of the atoms displaced roughly 90% recombine, leaving about 10% as stable defects.

Charge yield is never assumed to be one unless the problem says so. Between generation and trapping, a field-dependent fraction of the electron–hole pairs recombines. A calculation that omits the yield factor f(Eox) or ηh gives an upper bound, and that is the right answer only when the problem asks for one.

Signs. Trapped oxide charge is positive, so ΔV = −Qox/Cox is negative. Under total dose the threshold voltage moves negative for both n- and p-channel devices: on an NMOS that means a device that will not turn off, on a PMOS it means |Vth| grows and the device turns on later. Interface traps are amphoteric — acceptor-like above midgap, donor-like below — so their contribution is negative for a PMOS and positive for an NMOS, and can partly cancel the oxide-trap shift in an NMOS.

The collection depth is a separate quantity

Equation (11.1) is dimensionally a charge per unit length, and Deck 03 p. 26 presents it as Qcol without stating the unit. The depth over which charge is actually collected — the junction depletion width, extended by funnelling and by diffusion from below — has to come from the problem or from the device geometry. Where a problem gives an LET and asks for a charge, it must also give a collection depth or a sensitive-volume thickness; where it gives neither, the quantity being asked for is the charge per micrometre.

The published Messenger–Spratt relation puts the damage constant in the denominator, Δ(1/β) = Φn/(ωTK) with ωT = 2πft and K in n·s/cm², so the course's K1 is the reciprocal of the tabulated K. 11 · Circuit-level consequences

Modules M13 and M14, pending. From the Lecture 9 practice problems — the standard circuit relations, applied with a degraded parameter substituted in.

ACL = AOL1 + AOLβf
(13.1)
ACL closed-loop gain · AOL open-loop gain after irradiation · βf feedback factor, dimensionless — the fraction of the output fed back, 1/10 for a nominal gain of 10 and 1 for a unity-gain buffer. The fractional gain error is 1/(1 + AOLβf), so an open-loop gain that falls from 10⁶ to 3,000 still leaves a nominal-gain-of-10 stage accurate to about 0.3%. Feedback conceals gain degradation until the loop gain approaches unity, at which point accuracy collapses quickly. A radiation-induced input offset is not concealed at all — it appears at the output multiplied by the closed-loop gain, and in precision stages it dominates the total error budget.
Iout ≈ Iref · ββ + 1
(13.2)
Iout mirror output current (A) · Iref reference branch current (A) · β current gain after irradiation, dimensionless. A bipolar mirror's reference branch must supply the base currents of both transistors as well as the reference collector current, so the output falls short by a fraction that grows as β degrades. The course's form is above; the standard two-transistor mirror result is Iout = Iref·β/(β + 2), since two base currents are drawn from the reference node. At β = 20 the two give 0.95Iref and 0.91Iref; they converge at high gain and diverge exactly where radiation puts the circuit.
Vout,min = VDD − (|Vth| + |Vov|)
(13.3)
Vout,min minimum output voltage at which the PMOS mirror transistor stays saturated (V) · VDD supply (V) · Vth threshold voltage, magnitude taken (V) · Vov overdrive VGS − Vth, magnitude taken (V). Total dose moves the threshold negative, which changes |Vth| and therefore the headroom the stage needs. The current the mirror actually delivers changes as well, through both the threshold shift and the mobility degradation that accompanies interface-trap buildup, so a headroom check alone does not establish that the stage still works.

RDM = RmfRspec
(15.1)
RDM radiation design margin, dimensionless · Rmf mean radiation failure level of the part type for the application's failure definition, in krad(Si); for the lognormal distribution normally assumed, the geometric mean exp⟨ln Rfail⟩ of the individual failure doses · Rspec specified mission dose at the part's location, in krad(Si). Numerator and denominator in the same dose unit and the same material. RDM = 1 means half the population fails at the specification. In Pease's design-margin-breakpoint method, RDM below 1–2 makes a part unacceptable, 1–2 to 10 places it in hardness-critical category 1 (a lot acceptance test on every lot), 10 to 100 in category 2 (periodic sample testing), and above 100 in the hardness non-critical category. M15 §2.
Psn = 1 − C   ⟹   Ps = (1 − C)1/n
(15.2)
Ps probability of survival demonstrated for the lot, dimensionless · n number of parts tested at the specification dose, all of which pass · C confidence level, dimensionless; 0.90 is the value usually specified. Solved for the sample size, n = ln(1 − C)/ln Ps. Zero-failure binomial sampling; the survival probability is that of the lot at the tested dose only. n = 11 gives Ps = 0.81 at C = 0.90, which Pease states as a lot tolerance percent defective (LTPD) of 20, Ps = 0.80. Demonstrating Ps = 0.999 at the same confidence needs about 2300 parts (Pease quotes 2303) — or an overtest: 11 parts at about 3.02 times the specification dose give the same assurance, provided the standard deviation of ln(failure dose) is at most 0.5 (§14). M15 §2.
exp(⟨ln Rfail⟩ − KTLs) ≥ Rspec   ⟺   RDM ≥ PCC = exp(KTLs)
(15.3)
⟨ln Rfail⟩ mean of the natural logarithms of the sample failure doses (dose in krad(Si), logarithm dimensionless) · s standard deviation of those logarithms, dimensionless · KTL one-sided tolerance-limit factor, a function of sample size, survival probability and confidence, read from tables · Rspec specification dose (krad(Si)) · PCC part categorization criterion. Lognormal failure doses assumed. Pease gives KTL ≈ 4.2 for 15 samples at Ps = 0.999, C = 0.90, and 3.1 for his 18-sample example at Ps = 0.99, C = 0.90. The two forms are the same inequality: the lot passes when its design margin exceeds the margin its own scatter demands. M15 §2.
t = DḊ
(15.4)
t duration of the exposure (s) · D total dose (rad) · Ḋ dose rate (rad/s), in the same material as D. Test standards write both in rad(Si); device data are often in rad(SiO₂). For Co-60 gammas the two labels differ by less than one per cent, but for 10 keV X-rays 1 rad(SiO₂) corresponds to about 1.8 rad(Si) (M04 §8), so a dose in one material and a rate in the other gives a duration wrong by that factor. Check the prefixes too: rad against mrad and krad is a factor of 10³. Divide by 86 400 s/day for days. 100 krad(Si) at 50 rad(Si)/s takes 2000 s; Condition D of Test Method 1019, 1.5 times a 100 krad(Si) specification at 10 mrad(Si)/s, takes 1.5×10⁷ s, about 174 days. Atmospheric depthSea level 1,033 g/cm²; 12 km 197 g/cm²; 18 km 77 g/cm² Depth is the pressure at that altitude divided by g. The Pfotzer–Regener maximum sits at 15–20 km, which is 56–123 g/cm².
Energy1 eV = 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ J; 1 MeV = 1.602×10⁻¹³ J 1 erg = 10⁻⁷ J.
LET1 MeV·cm²/mg = 1,000 MeV·cm²/g The course uses per mg; NIEL tables use per g. A factor of 1,000.

Distance in the belts1 RE = 6,380 km L is a geocentric distance in RE; altitudes are (L − 1)RE. Sources sometimes quote one and label it the other.
Magnetic field1 T = 10⁴ G The surface equatorial field is 0.31 G = 3.1×10⁻⁵ T.
