Two conventions run through everything below and are the largest single source of wrong answers in this subject. First, dose is always named with its material — rad(Si) and rad(SiO2) are different quantities, because the same fluence deposits different energy per gram in different materials. Second, this subject mixes CGS and SI freely: lengths appear in cm, µm and nm within one problem, capacitance per unit area in F/cm², density in g/cm³ or kg/m³ depending on the equation. Each gloss below states which unit its constant assumes.

2 · How solutions work in this subject

What is true of a solution regardless of which equation it uses.

The chain every calculation walks along

Energy deposited → charge generated → charge that survives recombination → charge that is trapped or collected → a device parameter that moves → a circuit that fails. Almost every problem in the course is one or two consecutive links of that chain. Identify which two links the question spans, and the equation follows.

Three quantities carry cm² and are distinct. LET is MeV·cm²/mg — an energy loss normalised by areal density. Cross-section is cm²/device or cm²/bit — an effective target area. NIEL is MeV·cm²/g — the non-ionizing counterpart of LET. Dose carries no cm² at all: it is energy per unit mass, rad or Gy.

Ionizing and non-ionizing energy loss are separate accounts. The same particle deposits into both. Ionizing loss feeds total dose and single-event charge; non-ionizing loss feeds displacement damage. Only a very small fraction of the deposited energy goes into displacements, and of the atoms displaced roughly 90% recombine, leaving about 10% as stable defects.

Charge yield is never assumed to be one unless the problem says so. Between generation and trapping, a field-dependent fraction of the electron–hole pairs recombines. A calculation that omits the yield factor f(Eox) or ηh gives an upper bound, and that is the right answer only when the problem asks for one.

Signs. Trapped oxide charge is positive, so ΔV = −Qox/Cox is negative. Under total dose the threshold voltage moves negative for both n- and p-channel devices: on an NMOS that means a device that will not turn off, on a PMOS it means |Vth| grows and the device turns on later. Interface traps are amphoteric — acceptor-like above midgap, donor-like below — so their contribution is negative for a PMOS and positive for an NMOS, and can partly cancel the oxide-trap shift in an NMOS.

D = ΔEDΔm    ΔED = ΔEE − ΔEL
(3.1)
D absorbed dose (rad or Gy) · ΔED mean energy deposited in the volume — energy entering minus energy leaving, not energy entering · Δm mass of the volume. 1 rad = 100 erg/g = 0.01 J/kg = 6.24×10¹³ eV/g; 1 Gy = 1 J/kg = 100 rad = 6.24×10¹⁵ eV/g. Always name the material: rad(Si) and rad(SiO2) differ because the same fluence deposits different energy per gram in each.
D(rad) ≈ 1.60×10⁻⁵ · LET · Φ
(3.2)
D absorbed dose in the material the LET was quoted for (rad) · LET linear energy transfer, MeV·cm²/mg · Φ fluence, cm⁻². The constant 1.60×10⁻⁵ carries the whole conversion: MeV→J multiplies by 1.602×10⁻¹³, energy per mg → energy per kg multiplies by 10⁶, giving 1.602×10⁻⁷ J/kg, and dividing by 0.01 converts Gy to rad. Both units must be exactly as stated or the constant is wrong. Monoenergetic beam at normal incidence.
H = wR · D
(3.3)
H equivalent dose (Sv, or rem) · D absorbed dose in tissue (Gy, or rad) · wR radiation weighting factor, dimensionless: 1 for photons, electrons and muons; 2 for protons and charged pions; 20 for alpha particles, fission fragments and heavy ions; for neutrons a continuous function of energy running from 2.5 to a maximum of 20.7 near 1 MeV (ICRP 103 — the older ICRP 60 gave 5 for protons and a 5–20 step function for neutrons, still widely quoted). 1 Sv = 100 rem, 10 mSv = 1 rem. Never enters a device calculation; parts are specified in rad(Si). Rem and rad coincide only where wR = 1.
xρ = ρ · t
(3.4)
xρ areal density, or mass thickness (g/cm²) · ρ density (g/cm³) · t physical thickness (cm). The correct variable for shield depth and for every range table, because energy loss is set by the number of electrons along the path rather than by length. Aluminium: ρ = 2.699 g/cm³, 1 mm = 0.270 g/cm², 200 mils = 5.08 mm = 1.37 g/cm². Equal areal density equates materials only to first order — high Z adds bremsstrahlung and nuclear secondaries, equation (3.6).
R = 0.412 En, n = 1.265 − 0.0954 ln E  ·  R = 0.530 E − 0.106
(3.5)
Katz–Penfold empirical electron range. R extrapolated (practical) range — the thickness beyond which essentially no electrons emerge, g/cm²; divide by ρ for a length · E electron kinetic energy, MeV. The paper states the first form for 0.01 < E < 2.5 MeV and the second for 2.5 < E < 20 MeV; extrapolating the first to 3 MeV gives 1.474 against the second form's 1.484 g/cm², so they join smoothly. The constants are fitted to aluminium absorbers. This is not the CSDA range, which is longer because it counts the wandering path rather than the depth reached. Katz & Penfold, Rev. Mod. Phys. 24, 28 (1952).
(dE/dx)rad(dE/dx)col ≈ ZE700
(3.6)
Ratio of radiative to collisional mass stopping power for electrons, both in MeV·cm²/g · Z atomic number of the absorber · E electron kinetic energy, MeV. An order-of-magnitude relation: Attix puts 800 in the denominator where Knoll puts 700. At 1 MeV: 1.9% in aluminium (Z = 13), 11.7% in lead (Z = 82). A ratio of stopping powers at one instantaneous energy, not the fraction of the electron energy ultimately radiated, which is smaller because the ratio collapses as the electron slows. Use it for the proportionality to Z, which is exact here and is the argument for a low-Z shield against an electron environment.
S = dEdx
(4.1)
S stopping power, energy lost per unit path length (MeV/cm). The two channels are electronic stopping, collisions with electrons, which produces ionization and therefore dose and single-event charge; and nuclear stopping, collisions with nuclei, which produces displacements. LET as the course uses it refers to the electronic part.

LET = 1ρ · dEdx
(4.3)
LET mass stopping power (MeV·cm²/mg in this course; MeV·cm²/g elsewhere — a factor of 1000, and the commonest unit error in the subject) · ρ material density (mg/cm³ to match, so 2,328 mg/cm³ for Si) · dE/dx (MeV/cm). Normalising by density is what makes LET comparable across materials. LET rises along the track to a maximum near its end — the Bragg peak — and the integral of dE/dx over the track length is the total deposited energy. Integrating LET itself would leave the mass normalisation behind, so multiply by ρ first. Charge collection is greatest when the Bragg peak coincides with the sensitive volume, which is what a heavy-ion test must arrange.
R(E0) = ∫0E0 dES(E)
(4.4)
R continuous-slowing-down range, as a path length (cm, µm) or divided by density as an areal density (g/cm², the form range tables use) · E0 initial kinetic energy (MeV) · S(E) stopping power along the slowing-down history (MeV/cm). Assumes a straight path, which holds for heavy charged particles and fails for electrons — use the Katz–Penfold extrapolated range (3.5) for those. Evaluated from tables in practice: NIST PSTAR (protons), ASTAR (alphas), SRIM (heavy ions through arbitrary layer stacks). Reference points in silicon: a 5.5 MeV alpha has a CSDA range of 6.49 mg/cm², or 27.9 µm, against a projected range of 6.40 mg/cm²; a 100 MeV proton travels far enough to cross any die. Statistical spread in the endpoint — range straggling — is of order 1% of the range.
Neh = ρDEp
(4.5)
Neh electron–hole pairs generated per unit volume (m⁻³ as written) · ρ density in kg/m³ (2,200 for SiO2, 2,328 for Si) · D dose in J/kg, so 10 krad = 100 J/kg · Ep pair creation energy in joules (17 eV = 2.72×10⁻¹⁸ J for SiO2; 3.6 eV = 5.77×10⁻¹⁹ J for Si). Strict SI throughout; divide the result by 10⁶ to reach cm⁻³. This counts pairs generated, before any recombination.
Neh = g0 · D
(4.6)
Neh pairs per cm³ · g0 initial pair density generated per rad (pairs/cm³ per rad): 8.1×10¹² for SiO2, 4×10¹³ for Si, ~7×10¹³ for GaAs · D dose in rad of that material. Algebraically identical to (4.5) — g0 = ρ/Ep with the rad and unit conversions folded in — but it avoids the SI conversion entirely and is the faster route when the dose is already in rad. Each value reproduces from the density and pair energy of its own material: 1 rad = 6.24×10¹³ eV/g, so SiO2 gives 2.2 × 6.24×10¹³ ÷ 17 = 8.1×10¹².
D = (µenρ)mat · Ψ
(4.7)
D absorbed dose in the named material (MeV/g; convert with 1 rad = 6.24×10¹³ eV/g) · µen/ρ mass energy-absorption coefficient of that material at that photon energy (cm²/g, tabulated by NIST) · Ψ energy fluence, total photon energy crossing unit area (MeV/cm²). The route to dose for a photon field, where equation (3.2) does not apply because a photon has no LET. Written as rates on both sides it gives dose rate from energy fluence rate, the form in the professor's Charge Generation and Dose Deposition handout. The coefficient is strongly material- and energy-dependent, which is what (4.8) exists to correct for at an interface.
DEF = Dsensitive regionDequilibrium
(4.8)
DEF dose enhancement factor, dimensionless · Dsensitive region average dose actually deposited in the device's sensitive region · Dequilibrium dose that would be deposited under charged-particle equilibrium, i.e. with no nearby high-Z layer. Arises because a high-Z layer next to a low-Z one emits more photoelectrons and backscattered electrons into it than it receives. The effect grows as photon energy falls and as the neighbouring material's Z rises, because the photoelectric cross-section scales steeply with both. Separately, the largest measured enhancements — factors of 8–10 in thin oxides — are reported at higher X-ray energies near 100 keV with very high-Z gold layers. Either way, 10 keV X-ray testing and Co-60 testing do not give the same result for the same nominal dose, which is what the factor exists to correct.
Ḋdet = Iph Epq ρ A t    Ψ̇ = Iph Epq A (μenμ) [1 − e−(μ/ρ)ρt]    ḊBḊA = (μen/ρ)B(μen/ρ)A
(4.9), (4.10)
Photodiode dosimetry — the route from a measured current to a calibrated dose rate. Ḋdet dose rate in the detector's own material (J/(g·s); ÷ 10−5 to reach rad/s, since 1 rad = 10−5 J/g) · Iph photocurrent with dark current subtracted (A) · Ep pair creation energy in joules (3.6 eV = 5.77×10−19 J for Si; 17 eV = 2.72×10−18 J for SiO2) · q = 1.602×10−19 C · ρ density (g/cm³) · A active area (cm²) · t depleted thickness (cm) · Ψ̇ incident energy fluence rate (J/(cm²·s)) · μ/ρ mass attenuation coefficient (cm²/g) — every interaction that removes a photon · μen/ρ mass energy-absorption coefficient (cm²/g) — only energy deposited locally. They are not interchangeable, and both appear in the middle form: μ in the exponent, because every interaction removes a photon from the beam, and the ratio μen/μ in front, because only that share of the interacted energy stays behind (0.97 in Si at 10 keV, 0.47 at 1.25 MeV). The first form assumes complete pair collection; A and t set the mass the dose refers to, so the geometry is part of the measurement. The ratio form transfers a dose between materials in the same beam. It is a bulk relation: it needs each material thick enough to establish charged-particle equilibrium in its own right, so it does not apply across the interfaces of (4.8), and it does not give the dose in a nanometre-scale gate oxide buried in silicon — a layer thin against the secondary-electron range takes the dose of its surroundings. (6.1)

S = S0 · (1 + qDitCox)
(6.4)

Interface traps must be charged and discharged as the surface potential moves, which adds capacitance in parallel with the semiconductor and degrades the gate's control. This is the course's form; the full expression also carries the depletion capacitance, S = S0(1 + (Cd + Cit)/Cox), so a real pre-irradiation swing exceeds 60 mV/decade even with no traps.
μeff = μ01 + α ΔNit
(6.5)

Empirical, not derived. It assumes the effect of oxide charge on mobility is projected onto the interface and falls off strongly with the charge's distance into the oxide, so one interface-referred density stands in for charge at several depths. The mechanism is Coulomb scattering of channel carriers by charged centres within a few nm of the interface. M06 §6.
β = ICIB
(6.6)
β common-emitter current gain, dimensionless · IC collector current (A) · IB base current (A). Under total dose the collector current is essentially unchanged while the base current rises, so gain falls as the reciprocal of the base current: if IB doubles, β halves. The rise in IB comes from interface traps formed where the emitter–base depletion region meets the oxide; these act as recombination centres and raise the surface recombination velocity. Trapped positive oxide charge acts through a separate, electrostatic route — it moves the surface potential, spreading the emitter–base depletion region at the surface in an NPN and so adding to the degradation, but accumulating the n-type base surface in a lateral PNP and so opposing it. The two mechanisms are therefore not interchangeable, and their relative weight is what makes a given structure sensitive. Degradation is worst at low bias, where recombination in the emitter–base depletion region dominates the base current, and lateral and substrate PNP structures degrade more than vertical NPN ones.
retained = (1 − CTI)n ≈ 1 − n·CTI      CTI = 1 − CTE
(6.7)
CTI charge transfer inefficiency per transfer, dimensionless · CTE charge transfer efficiency · n number of transfers · retained fraction of the original signal packet. The linear approximation is the course's form and is accurate while n·CTI ≪ 1: at CTI = 10⁻⁴ over 2,000 transfers it gives a 20% loss against the exact 18.1%, and the gap widens as the product approaches 1. Radiation raises CTI by creating bulk traps that capture and delay charge, which is why CCDs are among the devices most sensitive to displacement damage.

(8.3)

Only the middle term moves under irradiation. Work function, Fermi potential and depletion charge are fixed by materials and doping, which neither photons nor electrons change at course-relevant fluences — so ΔVth = −(ΔQot+ΔQit)/Cox and the whole device problem reduces to two measured densities. M08 §1.
ΔVth = ΔVot + ΔVit    ΔVot < 0 always    ΔVit > 0 (n-ch), < 0 (p-ch)
(8.4)

Qualification therefore adds 50% dose plus a 168 h biased anneal at 100 °C. M08 §3.
ID ≈ I0 exp [VG − VthnVT]  ⟹  ratio = exp (ΔVnVT) = 10ΔV/S
(8.1)

The two ratio forms are equivalent, since S = nVTln10 — use whichever the problem supplies. A 0.15 V shift at n = 1.5 multiplies the off-state current by about 47; this exponential sensitivity is why a modest threshold shift becomes a standby-power failure.
σs = qNox    E = σsεox    V = E · tFOX
(8.2)
σs areal sheet charge (C/cm²) · Nox trapped charge areal density (cm⁻²) · E field in the oxide (V/cm) · εox 3.45×10⁻¹³ F/cm · tFOX field-oxide thickness in cm (0.50 µm = 0.50×10⁻⁴ cm) · V voltage dropped across it (V). The surface inverts when V exceeds the parasitic device's own threshold, 2φF + |Qdep|/CFOX, and the depletion term dominates because CFOX is small: under 500 nm of oxide it is 0.60 + 2.04 V at NA = 1015 cm−3, and 0.83 + 24.1 V at a channel-stop doping of 1017 cm−3. Quoting 2φF alone understates the threshold several-fold, and it is the depletion term that makes a channel stop work. Because the field oxide is one to two orders of magnitude thicker than the gate oxide, it develops volts rather than millivolts from the same areal charge — which is why parasitic field-oxide and shallow-trench leakage paths fail long before the gate oxide does, and why enclosed layout transistors and guard rings exist.
ΔVot(t) = ΔVot,0 · e−t/τ
(9.1)
ΔVot(t) oxide-trap shift remaining after time t (V) · ΔVot,0 shift immediately after irradiation (V) · t elapsed time in seconds · τ annealing time constant in seconds, strongly temperature-dependent. A single-exponential description of the oxide-trap component only. Interface-trap charge does not anneal on the same timescale — it continues to build for thousands of seconds after the exposure ends and then persists — which is why the two components must be separated before either is extrapolated.

(7.3)

Deck 05 p. 34 states the exponent as −1/2 and marks a fitted −0.55; p. 54 gives the interface-trap buildup the same dependence, fitted −0.6, which is the evidence linking interface-trap formation to hole trapping. The midgap technique, and the reason it works: interface traps are amphoteric and are electrically neutral when the Fermi level is at midgap, so the entire midgap shift is attributable to oxide charge. The difference between the midgap point and a second point is then the interface-trap contribution alone. Requires a measured curve, not a single point, and assumes the trap distribution does not shift the midgap reference itself.

Qcol = 1.6×10⁻² · LET · ρEp
(11.1)
Qcol charge deposited per micrometre of track, in pC/µm · LET linear energy transfer (MeV·cm²/mg) · ρ material density (g/cm³, 2.328 for Si) · Ep electron–hole pair creation energy (eV, 3.6 for Si). Multiply by the charge-collection depth in µm to obtain the collected charge that (11.3) compares against Qcrit. In silicon this evaluates to 10.4 fC/µm per unit LET, so an LET of 97 MeV·cm²/mg deposits almost exactly 1 pC/µm — the standard anchor for checking any single-event charge calculation.
The collection depth is a separate quantity

Equation (11.1) is dimensionally a charge per unit length, and Deck 03 p. 26 presents it as Qcol without stating the unit. The depth over which charge is actually collected — the junction depletion width, extended by funnelling and by diffusion from below — has to come from the problem or from the device geometry. Where a problem gives an LET and asks for a charge, it must also give a collection depth or a sensitive-volume thickness; where it gives neither, the quantity being asked for is the charge per micrometre.

Qcrit = Cn · Vcrit
(11.2)
Qcrit critical charge (C; usually quoted in fC) · Cn effective node capacitance (F; usually fF) · Vcrit node voltage excursion that flips the stored state (V). Qcrit is a property of the technology and the circuit, not of the ion. It falls as nodes shrink and supply voltages drop — below 1 fC in the smallest nodes, against tens of fC in older technologies — which is the mechanism by which scaling increases single-event sensitivity even as the target area per node falls.
upset ⟺ Qcol > Qcrit
(11.3)
Qcol charge collected at the sensitive node (C) · Qcrit critical charge (C). The whole of single-event upset in one comparison. Where the problem treats Qcol as a distribution rather than a number — because strike location, angle and track structure vary — the upset probability is the fraction of that distribution lying above Qcrit.

(11.4)
σ single-event cross-section, cm²/device or cm²/bit — state which, since the two differ by the bit count · Nevents events counted · Φ fluence (cm⁻²). An effective target area: events per unit fluence, and nothing else. It is not a current density (A/cm²) and not a dose. Measured against LET, the curve rises from a threshold LET and saturates at a value that cannot exceed the physical area of the sensitive region; both the threshold and the saturated value are needed to predict a rate.

(11.6)
FIT failures in time — one FIT is one failure per 10⁹ device-hours. Convert the rate to per-hour first: a rate quoted per day is divided by 24. Mission analyses often quote rates per day and reliability budgets quote FIT, and the factor of 24 between them is easy to drop.

DDD = Φ · NIEL
(12.1)
DDD displacement damage dose — non-ionizing energy deposited per unit mass (MeV/g) · Φ particle fluence (cm⁻²) · NIEL non-ionizing energy loss (MeV·cm²/g). The non-ionizing counterpart of total ionizing dose, and the quantity that plays the same role for bulk defects that dose plays for trapped charge.
NIEL(E) = 1ρ ∫TdTmax T · L(T) · dσ(E,T)dT dT
(12.2)
NIEL non-ionizing energy loss (MeV·cm²/g) · E incident particle energy (MeV) · ρ target density (g/cm³) · T recoil energy of the primary knock-on atom (eV or MeV) · dσ/dT differential scattering cross-section (cm²/MeV) · Td displacement threshold energy, ≈21 eV for Si · L(T) Lindhard partition function, the fraction of a recoil’s energy that goes into further atomic motion rather than into ionization the recoil itself causes, dimensionless and well below 1 for recoils of tens of keV · Tmax maximum transferable energy (MeV). Two factors carry the definition. The lower limit excludes recoils too soft to displace an atom, which contribute nothing. L(T) is what makes the result a non-ionizing energy loss: drop it and the integral returns the total energy given to recoils, a larger and different quantity.
Nd = κ · Ed2Eth
(12.3)
Nd number of displaced atoms from one recoil, dimensionless · κ displacement efficiency, 0.8 — a universal constant of the NRT model from the Lindhard energy partition, applied to every material in the international dpa standard, not a silicon-specific value · Ed damage energy imparted to the primary knock-on atom (eV) · Eth displacement threshold energy (eV, ≈21 for Si). The Norgett–Robinson–Torrens model, and strictly it is piecewise: no displacement for Ed < Eth, exactly one for Eth ≤ Ed ≤ (2Eth)/κ, and the expression above only above that. Note also that the standard literature uses Ed for the threshold and Tdam for the damage energy — the reverse of the symbols here, which follow the course. It counts Frenkel pairs created; roughly 90% of them recombine promptly, leaving about 10% as the stable defects that actually degrade the device.
ΔP ∝ Φ · NIEL
(12.4)
ΔP degradation of a device parameter — gain loss, dark current increase, minority-carrier lifetime reduction, solar-cell output loss · Φ fluence (cm⁻²) · NIEL (MeV·cm²/g). Damage scales with the product, which is what allows a result measured with one particle and energy to be converted to another: the ratio of the two NIEL values is the scaling factor. This is why NIEL is tabulated at all. The proportionality holds well for protons and neutrons over a wide energy range and breaks down where the recoil spectrum changes character, which is the reason damage-factor-versus-NIEL plots are checked rather than assumed.
1β = 1β0 + K1Φn2πft
(12.5)
β current gain after irradiation, dimensionless · β0 pre-irradiation gain · K1 damage constant, which depends on base resistivity, in cm²/(n·s) · Φn fast neutron fluence (n/cm²) · ft transistor cutoff frequency (Hz). Check the convention before substituting a tabulated constant. The published Messenger–Spratt relation places the damage constant in the denominator, Δ(1/β) = Φn/(ωTK) with ωT = 2πft and K in n·s/cm², so the course's K1 is the reciprocal of the tabulated K. The reciprocal gain is linear in fluence, not the gain itself, so a plot of 1/β against fluence is the one that gives a straight line. Displacement damage reduces minority-carrier lifetime in the base, and the ft in the denominator is why high-speed devices with narrow bases degrade less: base transit time and base width fall together.

9 · Circuit-level consequences

Modules M13 and M14, pending. From the Lecture 9 practice problems — the standard circuit relations, applied with a degraded parameter substituted in.

ACL = AOL1 + AOLβf
(13.1)
ACL closed-loop gain · AOL open-loop gain after irradiation · βf feedback factor, dimensionless — the fraction of the output fed back, 1/10 for a nominal gain of 10 and 1 for a unity-gain buffer. The fractional gain error is 1/(1 + AOLβf), so an open-loop gain that falls from 10⁶ to 3,000 still leaves a nominal-gain-of-10 stage accurate to about 0.3%. Feedback conceals gain degradation until the loop gain approaches unity, at which point accuracy collapses quickly. A radiation-induced input offset is not concealed at all — it appears at the output multiplied by the closed-loop gain, and in precision stages it dominates the total error budget.
Iout ≈ Iref · ββ + 1
(13.2)
Iout mirror output current (A) · Iref reference branch current (A) · β current gain after irradiation, dimensionless. A bipolar mirror's reference branch must supply the base currents of both transistors as well as the reference collector current, so the output falls short by a fraction that grows as β degrades. The course's form is above; the standard two-transistor mirror result is Iout = Iref·β/(β + 2), since two base currents are drawn from the reference node. At β = 20 the two give 0.95Iref and 0.91Iref; they converge at high gain and diverge exactly where radiation puts the circuit.

Dose1 rad = 0.01 Gy = 100 erg/g = 6.24×10¹³ eV/g Always carries a material: rad(Si) ≠ rad(SiO2).
Dose1 Gy = 1 J/kg = 100 rad = 6.24×10¹⁵ eV/g 1 krad = 10 Gy; 1 Mrad = 10 kGy.
Dose in equations1 krad = 10 J/kg; 10 krad = 100 J/kg Equation (4.5) needs J/kg, not rad.
Dose equivalent1 rem = 0.01 Sv; 10 mSv = 1 rem; H = wRD The radiation weighting factor wR is 1 for photons and electrons, 2 for protons, 20 for alpha particles and heavier ions, and for neutrons a continuous function of energy from 2.5 to 20.7 (ICRP 103; ICRP 60's 5–20 step is still widely quoted). Rem and rad coincide only for photons and electrons. Dose equivalent is a biological quantity and never enters a device calculation.
