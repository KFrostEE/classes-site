Device structures and regions

TermWhat it isKey factsWhy it matters
MOS capacitor A conducting gate over an insulating SiO₂ film grown on silicon: metal, oxide, semiconductor. One plate is a semiconductor, so the balancing charge is gathered from a layer hundreds of nanometres deep. Cox = εoxε0/tox, always per unit area. No steady current flows; the gate acts only through its field. Every property of the transistor the course cares about follows from this capacitor. M01 §1
Accumulation, depletion, inversion The three responses of a p-type surface to gate bias: holes drawn to the surface (negative gate); holes repelled, leaving ionised acceptors (small positive gate); a layer of mobile electrons formed at the surface (large positive gate). Strong inversion is the convention φs = 2φF. Above it the depletion width stops at Wm and extra gate voltage drops almost entirely across the oxide. Only inversion creates a channel. The depletion charge under it is one of the four threshold terms. M01 §2
Channel
inversion layer		 The thin layer of minority carriers at the Si/SiO₂ interface under strong inversion, joining source to drain. Within a few nanometres of the interface. Effective mobility is roughly half the bulk value, degraded chiefly by the transverse field across the oxide. Carries the drain current; its charge per unit area is Cox(VG − VT). M01 §§2, 4
Source and drain The two heavily doped regions at the ends of the channel, of the type opposite to the body, that supply and collect the channel carriers. n⁺ at typically 10¹⁹–10²⁰ cm⁻³, three to five orders above the substrate; the notes use 10²⁰ cm⁻³. Each forms a pn junction with the body. Their junction depth and lateral spread under the gate are what drain engineering controls. M01 §3, M02 §§5.2, 6
Enhancement-mode and depletion-mode devices An enhancement-mode device has no channel at zero gate voltage and needs one induced; a depletion-mode device conducts at zero gate voltage and must be driven to turn off. The worked n-channel example, VT = −2.24 V, is depletion-mode. Logic needs enhancement-mode devices, which is why the interface charge had to be cut. M01 §3, M03 §7
Pinch-off The point at which the gate-to-channel voltage at the drain end falls to threshold and the inversion charge there goes to zero. At VDS = VGS − VT. Beyond it the extra drain voltage drops across a short depleted region and the current saturates. Separates the linear equation (1.4) from the saturation equation (1.5). M01 §4
p-channel MOSFET
pMOS, PMOSFET		 The mirror device: p⁺ source and drain in an n-type body, inverted to a hole channel by a negative gate voltage. Bulk hole mobility about a third of electron mobility: 500 against 1450 cm²/V·s (Sze). With an aluminium or n⁺ polysilicon gate, all four threshold terms are negative. Delivers about a third of the current at equal size, so it is drawn wider; its larger threshold set the supply voltage. M01 §5, M03 §6
CMOS
complementary MOS; the inverter		 An n-channel and a p-channel transistor on one die, wired so that one conducts whenever the other does not. The inverter ties both gates to the input and both drains to the output. In either stable state one device is off, so there is no path from supply to ground and current flows only while switching. That property is what off-state leakage erodes, multiplied over hundreds of millions of pairs. M01 §6
Well A region of the doping type opposite to the substrate, in which transistors of one polarity are built. Conventionally a shallow implant followed by a drive-in anneal, giving a profile nearly flat near the surface and rolling off to the well–substrate junction. The body doping of every CMOS device of one type. Retrograde wells are M08. M02 §5.1
Self-aligned gate The gate is patterned first and then used as the mask for the source–drain implant, so the source and drain edges fall at the gate edges with no alignment step. Needs a gate that survives the activation anneal: aluminium melts at 660 °C, polysilicon does not. Proposed 1965–66, demonstrated at Bell Labs in 1967; first commercial silicon-gate process at Fairchild in 1968. The same implant dopes the polysilicon. Removes the deliberate gate overlap of the aluminium-gate flow, and with it the overlap capacitance. M02 §3.1, M03 §8
Overlap capacitance Gate-to-source and gate-to-drain capacitance where the gate lies over the heavily doped source or drain through the gate oxide. Produced by laterally diffused source and drain plus a separately aligned metal gate drawn wider than the channel. Charged and discharged on every transition, so it costs speed. M01 §7.3, M02 §3.1
Polysilicon gate A gate of polycrystalline silicon — silicon deposited as a film of small, randomly oriented grains — doped heavily. Notes: replaced aluminium in the mid-1970s (silicon-gate production began in 1968). Work function set by doping, between 4.05 eV (n⁺) and 5.17 eV (p⁺). n⁺ poly served both device types down to 0.35 µm. Doped above about 10²⁰ cm⁻³. Turns φMS into a process variable and makes the self-aligned gate possible. On a high-k film it reacts with an unstable oxide (Ta₂O₅ needs a TiN or WN barrier), depletes, and on HfO₂ must be deposited below 600 °C (notes); metal gates replaced it. M02 §3.1, M03 §8, M07 §9
Silicide A compound of a metal with silicon that conducts far better than doped silicon and tolerates the same process temperatures. WSix in the polycide; TiSi₂ and CoSi₂, of lower resistivity, in the salicide. Formed by reaction, consuming silicon: 1 nm of Ti consumes 2.27 nm of Si to give 2.51 nm of TiSi₂. Lowers gate-line and source–drain contact resistance. M02 §§3.2–3.3
Polycide A two-layer gate: tungsten silicide over polysilicon, the polysilicon kept against the oxide for its work function. Introduced below 1.25 µm gate length. Notes: resistance reduced to one fourth. Literature: doped poly bottoms out near 20–30 Ω per square, the capped stack reaches 2–5 Ω per square, a factor of five to ten. Polysilicon lines had become too resistive to serve as local interconnect. M02 §3.2
Salicide
self-aligned silicide		 Silicide formed at once on the gate and on the source and drain, with no lithography: sputter metal everywhere, anneal so it reacts only with silicon, strip the unreacted metal. TiSi₂ or CoSi₂. Lecture 3 described one anneal; production uses two, about 600–700 °C then about 800 °C for Ti, with the strip between. The sidewall spacers keep gate and source–drain silicide apart. Lower poly-line resistance and lower contact resistance, because the silicide–silicon interface forms below the native oxide. M02 §3.3
Dual-doped polysilicon
notes: “asymmetric CMOS”; literature: symmetric CMOS		 n⁺ polysilicon over the n-channel devices and p⁺ polysilicon over the p-channel devices. The two gate work functions, near 4.05 and 5.17 eV, move φMS by the full 1.12 V band gap. Costs, from the notes: extra masking, boron penetration through the gate oxide, and boron counter-doping sideways along poly lines. Makes both devices surface-channel. M02 §8, M03 §8, M05 §4
Surface channel and buried channel A surface channel forms at the Si/SiO₂ interface. A buried channel conducts in a counter-doped layer beneath it, which the gate reaches only through a depletion layer. n⁺ poly on a p-channel device leaves VT too negative (−1.05 V against +0.07 V for p⁺, 10¹⁶ cm⁻³ well, 10 nm oxide); the boron correction, about 1.2×10¹² cm⁻², turns the top of the n-well p-type. Buried-channel PMOSFETs leak unacceptably at 0.25 µm gate lengths (notes), which forced p⁺ gates. Counter-doping to recover the threshold under a midgap metal gate also gives buried-channel MOSFETs, with degraded short-channel behaviour (notes p. 60). M02 §8, M03 §8, M07 §§8.1, 9
Blanket anti-punch-through implant A maskless boron implant under the whole channel, after the threshold-adjust implant, peaking deeper than it. Added as the NMOSFET gate length approached 1 µm. Same doping everywhere along the channel. Closes the sub-surface punch-through path; heavy enough for the drain edge, it raises VT and lowers mobility everywhere. M02 §§5.1, 5.4
Super-steep retrograde profile
SSR		 A channel profile whose doping rises steeply with depth: light where the carriers travel, heavy where the depletion regions must be held apart. Notes: inversion layer kept below 5×10¹⁷ cm⁻³; indium replaces boron (n-channel), arsenic or antimony replaces phosphorus (p-channel). Wolf: 100–200 keV In or As gives about 5×10¹⁶ cm⁻³ at the interface and a peak near 10¹⁸ cm⁻³ some 80 nm down. Correct VT without mobility loss; needed below about 0.13 µm. M02 §5.3
Halo implant A self-aligned implant of the well's own species around the inner edge of the drain extension. Boron for an n-channel device. Energy somewhat above the lightly doped drain implant; originally at normal incidence. Puts anti-punch-through doping only where punch-through begins, leaving the channel lightly doped. M02 §5.4
Pocket implant
LATIPS; quad implant		 The halo delivered at a large tilt, so the ions come to rest beneath the gate edge at a chosen depth and lateral distance. Wolf: 20–45° from the normal; after the extension implant, before the spacer; dose split into four equal segments with 90° rotations between them (a quad implant). Every gate edge, in both orientations on the chip, receives its own pocket. M02 §5.4
Lightly doped drain
LDD		 A lightly doped region between the channel and the heavily doped drain contact, reaching under the gate edge. Notes: about 10¹⁷ cm⁻³ beside a 10²⁰ cm⁻³ contact, implant dose 1–5×10¹³ cm⁻². Flagged: that dose over a 0.15–0.2 µm junction gives roughly 10¹⁸ cm⁻³, inconsistent with the notes' 10¹⁷. Phosphorus, medium-current implanter. Introduced at 2 µm. Spreads the drain voltage over a wider depletion layer, cutting the peak field that drives hot-carrier injection. M02 §6.1
Sidewall spacer A wedge of oxide left standing against each vertical face of the gate. Conformal LPCVD SiO₂, then an anisotropic fluorine-based RIE etch-back with no mask; the width is set by the deposited thickness. The rounded outer corner is the shape of the conformal film at the gate's top corner; Lecture 4's reflow at 800–900 °C is flagged on the page. Holds the heavy implant a fixed distance from the gate, and separates gate from source–drain silicide. M02 §§2, 6.1
Source/drain extension
SDE		 The shallow near region of a two-region drain, as heavily doped as the second region, the deeper contact region beyond the spacer that carries the metal contact, but shallower. At 0.18 µm. Notes: supply reduced from 5 V to 2 V; literature: nominal 1.8 V, reached in steps (5 → 3.3 → 2.5 → 1.8 V). Dose raised from 1–5×10¹³ to 5–10×10¹⁴ cm⁻², about twenty times. Scaling limits: junction depth 30 nm, under-diffusion 15 nm. Replaced the LDD once series resistance mattered more than hot carriers; the shallow junction limits drain-field reach. M02 §§6.2, 11
Epitaxial substrate A heavily doped wafer carrying a lightly doped single-crystal epitaxial layer of the same type, grown with the orientation of the silicon beneath. Notes: p⁺ with a p epi layer, or n⁺ with an n epi layer. Transistors sit wholly in the epi layer. The heavy substrate removes the resistance latch-up needs. M02 §7
Field oxide Thick oxide over the silicon wherever no transistor is built, so a metal line crossing it cannot invert the silicon. Notes: typically about 1 µm; the notes' own table gives 200–400 nm for LOCOS field oxide. The thick masking oxide of the classic flow is not field oxide — it is stripped, leaving no isolation. Device isolation. M01 §7.2, M02 §9, M05 §1
LOCOS
local oxidation of silicon; pad oxide		 Field oxide grown thermally wherever a patterned silicon nitride film is absent; the nitride sits on a thin pad oxide. Pad oxide 10–50 nm, there to keep the nitride's stress out of the silicon. Semi-recessed: part below, part above the original surface. Served until the 0.35 µm generation, then failed on the bird's beak and on non-planar topography. The isolation that STI replaced. M02 §9
Bird's beak The tapered oxide wedge that grows under the nitride edge in LOCOS. Oxidant diffuses sideways through the pad oxide; the ≈2.2× volume expansion lifts the nitride edge and shapes the taper. Shortened by a thinner pad oxide or thicker nitride, not by the mask. Encroaches on active area by an amount the lithography cannot control. M02 §9
Shallow trench isolation
STI		 A trench etched into the silicon and filled with deposited oxide, then polished flat. Nothing is consumed, so no bird's beak; the surface is planar. Oxidised sidewall liners 15–40 nm (notes' thickness table). Replaced LOCOS below 0.35 µm. The full flow is M15. M02 §9, M05 §1
Silicon on insulator
SOI		 Transistors built in a thin single-crystal silicon film on a buried oxide layer. No conduction path beneath the devices; junction capacitances terminate on an insulator. The notes give it a short overview only. Removes the bulk paths for latch-up and transient radiation currents and the source- and drain-to-substrate capacitance. M02 §10

Gate oxide
gate dielectric		 The insulator between the gate electrode and the channel. 1.5–10.0 nm. By generation (notes): 3–4 nm at 180 nm (1999), 2–3 nm at 150 and 130 nm, 1.5–2.0 nm at 100 nm, < 1.5 nm at 70 nm, < 1.0 nm at 50 nm (2012). The thinnest film in the process and the one every electrical parameter depends on. Grown dry, for quality. M05 §§1–2
Tunnel oxide A very thin oxide in non-volatile memory, through which charge deliberately tunnels to program and erase a cell. 6.0–10.0 nm in EEPROMs and flash memories. The one application where tunnelling is intended. Stress-induced leakage limits it to about 8 nm for ten-year retention. M05 §1, M06 §4
Screen oxide A thin oxide left on the surface during ion implantation. 10.0–20.0 nm. The implanted profile in silicon ends up shallower by about the screen thickness. Lecture 3: thick enough (≈ Rp) to put the peak at the Si/SiO₂ interface, so an opposite-type implant forms one junction, not two. Textbooks: suppresses channelling and protects the surface. M03 §9.2, M05 §1
Diffusion mask
masking film		 A patterned oxide that confines a diffusion or an implant to the windows opened in it. 100–200 nm against implantation and diffusion; ≈1 µm, grown wet, in the classic three-mask flow. Boron diffuses about 10⁻⁶ times as fast in SiO₂ as in Si at 1100 °C. The masking works only because that ratio is so large. M01 §7.3, M05 §1
Trench liner
STI sidewall liner		 A thin oxide grown on the walls of an etched isolation trench before it is filled with deposited oxide. 15–40 nm. Grown rather than deposited for a clean sidewall interface. M05 §1
Pad oxide As the class uses it: the oxide through which a contact window is opened to a diffused region (usually called the pre-metal dielectric). In the wider literature: the thin thermal oxide under the nitride in LOCOS or STI. 10–50 nm in the LOCOS sense (M02). In the LOCOS sense it keeps nitride stress out of the silicon and is the path for the sideways oxidant diffusion that forms the bird's beak. M05 §1, M02 §9
Surface passivation layer A film deposited over the finished chip to protect it from dust, humidity and mechanical contact. Silicon nitride also serves. Deposited; no row. Mechanical and chemical protection — distinct from electrical passivation, requirement 10. M05 §§1, 4
Interlevel dielectric The insulator between one metal wiring layer and the next, with openings where they connect. Deposited; no row. A modern part has at least six levels of metal. Part of the interconnect system whose planarization is M13. In a DRAM it buries the word and bit lines under a stacked capacitor; its CVD can oxidise a polysilicon electrode, and a borophosphosilicate-glass reflow at 800 °C raises Ta₂O₅ leakage. M05 §1, M07b §§3.1, 4.1, 5.3
Gate-stack sidewall re-oxidation Oxide grown on the plasma-damaged silicon exposed at the foot of the gate after the gate etch. 5.0–7.5 nm. Repairs damage that would otherwise sit in the path of the channel. M05 §1
Inter-polysilicon dielectric The insulator between two polysilicon layers: between the floating gate and the control gate of an EEPROM or flash cell, or between the plates of a two-poly capacitor. 4.0–20.0 nm. Holds the stored charge on the floating gate. M05 §1
Sacrificial oxide An oxide grown and then stripped, consuming the top few nanometres of silicon with their damage and contamination. 6–15 nm. The real gate oxide is then grown on a clean surface. M05 §1
DRAM capacitor dielectric The insulator of the storage capacitor in a dynamic memory cell, which holds one bit as charge. 5–10 nm. Thinner stores more charge per unit area at the same voltage, and faces the same thickness limit as the gate: SiO₂, Si₃N₄ or the ONO sandwich down to 50 Å (Wolf's minimum), then high-k films. M05 §1, M02 §4, M07b §3.2
Native oxide Oxide that forms on bare silicon exposed to air at room temperature. 0.5–1 nm almost at once, stopping at 1–2 nm after a few hours. A metal deposited on silicon lands on it, even after an HF clean; a silicide forms below it, which is why salicide contacts are low-resistance. On a polysilicon storage node it is a low-k layer under the capacitor dielectric, which rapid thermal nitridation converts to nitride. M02 §3.3, M07b §§4.1, 5.2

Materials and their atomic structure

TermWhat it isKey factsWhy it matters
Covalent bond
valence electrons		 A pair of electrons of opposite spin shared by two atoms, one from each. Valence electrons are the outermost-shell electrons that take part in bonding. Silicon has four valence electrons. One line in a bond diagram is one shared pair; the notes' double lines are the same bond with its two electrons drawn separately. Every defect in the oxide and at the interface is a departure from this bonding. M05a §1
Valence
bond count; satisfied atom		 The number of covalent bonds an atom forms in these solids. An atom bonded exactly that many times is satisfied. Si 4, O 2, N 3, H 1, F 1. Boron forms three, or four when it accepts an electron: B⁻ substituted in silicon, BO₄⁻ beside BO₃ in glass. Counting valences fixes SiO₂ and predicts which atoms can terminate a broken bond. M05a §1
Formal oxidation state The charge an atom would carry if every bond were fully ionic, both electrons assigned to the more electronegative atom. Si +4 and O −2 in SiO₂, the notes' “Si (+4) … O²⁻”. The Si–O bond is about 45% ionic, so the real displaced charge is a fraction of an electron per bond. Bookkeeping, not a measured charge; the same count labels interface silicon Si¹⁺ to Si⁴⁺. M05a §§1, 5
sp³ hybrid orbital One of four equivalent combinations of an atom's 3s and three 3p orbitals, each pointing to a corner of a regular tetrahedron. Configuration 3s¹ 3p³ in the crystal; angle between hybrids arccos(−1/3) = 109.47°. Bonding combinations form the valence band, antibonding the conduction band; the 1.12 eV between them is the gap. A dangling hybrid has no partner, so its level falls inside the gap — the interface trap. M05a §2
Crystalline silicon
diamond lattice		 Two interpenetrating face-centred cubic lattices offset by a quarter of the cube diagonal; every atom has four neighbours at the corners of a tetrahedron. Lattice constant a = 5.431 Å; 8 atoms per unit cell; 5.0×10²² atoms/cm³; Si–Si 2.35 Å = √3a/4. The notes draw it as a flat square grid, which keeps bond count and connectivity but not angles. The reference structure for the oxide and the interface. M05a §2
Miller indices
(100) and (111) planes		 The reciprocals of a plane's intercepts on the three cube axes, cleared to the smallest integers. (100): 6.8×10¹⁴ atoms/cm², two outward bonds each, 1.36×10¹⁵ bonds/cm². (111): 7.8×10¹⁴ atoms and 7.8×10¹⁴ outward bonds/cm². Every wafer size uses (100). (100) is used for MOS despite more outward bonds, because it gives the lower interface-trap density and fixed charge. M05a §2, M05b §3.6, M04 §5
Monolayer One atomic plane of silicon. 6.8×10¹⁴ atoms/cm² on (100). The unit in which the transition region's width is measured. M05a §5
SiO₄ tetrahedron The unit of silicon dioxide: a silicon at the centre bonded to an oxygen at each of four corners. Si–O 1.62 Å; O–Si–O 109.5°. Notes' table: O–O 2.27 Å, which conflicts with their own Si–O; the tetrahedral edge is 2.65 Å (measured 2.62–2.65 Å). Every silicon in the oxide keeps four bonds, each through an oxygen. M05a §3
Bridging oxygen An oxygen bonded to two silicons, shared between two tetrahedra. Notes: Si–O–Si 110°–180°, most probable 144° (Lecture 7: 145°–147°); Si···Si across the bridge 3.12 Å (3.08 Å from 1.62 Å at 144°). Four oxygens per silicon, each shared by two, gives SiO₂. The more bridging oxygens relative to non-bridging, the more cohesive the film (notes). M05a §3
Non-bridging oxygen An oxygen bonded to one silicon only. Its second bond ends in a hydroxyl (Si–O–H), in O⁻ beside a positive ion such as Na⁺ held ionically, or in an unpaired electron. Each is a place where the network stops; it lowers cohesiveness and hosts mobile ions. M05a §3
Hydroxyl group
Si–OH		 A non-bridging oxygen terminated by hydrogen, formed when water breaks a bridge: ≡Si–O–Si≡ + H₂O → 2 ≡Si–OH. Steam-grown oxide contains OH and H₂O. Releasing OH from Si–OH breaks a Si–O bond, 4.68 eV, stronger than Si–H at 3.30 eV. Why dry oxide is more cohesive than wet. As-grown wet oxides carry oxide traps because of their OH⁻ and H₂O; the notes assign an electron trap to a bond tied to H or OH alike. M05a §3, M05b §4.2
Quartz and vitreous silica
fused silica; continuous random network		 Quartz is crystalline SiO₂ with long-range order. Vitreous (fused) silica keeps each tetrahedron but varies bridge angles and ring sizes, leaving short-range order only. Wolf: thermal oxide 2.20 g/cm³ against 2.65 g/cm³ for quartz, 43% of volume occupied. Other references: 2.27 g/cm³, or 2.28×10²² SiO₂ units/cm³, the figure the site's atom counts use. Real rings run from three members to about ten. Thermal oxide is always the glass; its open network lets hydrogen, sodium and boron enter and move. M05a §3
Devitrification Crystallisation of fused silica into a crystalline form. Takes months or years at temperature, as in fused-silica furnace tubes. Crystalline silica is the stable phase, but no oxidation or anneal lasts long enough to form it. M05a §3
Network former A foreign atom that takes the place of silicon and bonds into the network through bridging oxygens. Phosphorus in a tetrahedron; boron in a triangle, BO₃, or a tetrahedron, BO₄⁻. Part of the covalent framework, so it does not drift. M05a §3
Network modifier An ion sitting in the open spaces between tetrahedra, held by ionic bonds to surrounding oxygens, chiefly non-bridging ones. The alkali ions Na⁺ and K⁺. Ionic bonds are not directional, so the ion hops between spaces and drifts under a field. This is the mobile ionic charge Qm. M05a §3, M05b §6
Transition region
suboxide; Si¹⁺, Si²⁺, Si³⁺		 The graded layer of partly oxidised silicon between the crystal and the oxide. Himpsel (pure-O₂ oxides): Si¹⁺ + Si²⁺ + Si³⁺ = (1.5 ± 0.5)×10¹⁵ cm⁻², about two monolayers. Si 2p shifts 0.95, 1.75, 2.48 and 3.9 eV for Si¹⁺ to Si⁴⁺. Plummer: perhaps one atomic distance. The notes place the fixed oxide charge here. Himpsel attributes the region's finite width — graded rather than abrupt — to the bond-density mismatch, 5.00×10²² against 2.28×10²² Si/cm³. M05a §5
Refractory metal A metal with a very high melting point, used to form silicides. Lecture 3: tungsten, titanium, molybdenum, tantalum, and nickel; the notes add cobalt. Nickel and cobalt are grouped as near-noble metals in the silicide literature. The silicide-forming metals of polycide and salicide. M02 §3.3
Dopant species Phosphorus and arsenic are group-V donors; boron is the group-III acceptor. Indium (acceptor) and antimony (donor) are low-diffusivity substitutes. Speed is set by the point defect that carries the atom, not by mass: B and P mainly interstitial (fast), Sb mainly vacancy, As both in roughly equal measure; In interstitial but with a higher activation energy. Indium is a deep acceptor, 0.16 eV above Ev, with solubility near 10¹⁸ cm⁻³ (Wolf). Diffusivity and straggle decide which species can hold a steep implanted profile. M01 §5, M02 §5.3
Polarisation
electronic; ionic (lattice); susceptibility χ		 The dipole moment per unit volume, P, induced when a field displaces the bound charges of an insulator. It places bound charge on each face of the film, so the plates carry more charge at a given voltage: k = 1 + χ, with P = ε0χE. Beyond the notes. Electronic: each electron cloud shifts relative to its nucleus; equals n², about 2.1 of SiO₂'s 3.9 (n ≈ 1.46), typically 4–5 in the wide-gap metal oxides. Ionic: the cation and anion sublattices shift against each other. Orientational polarisation does not operate in these solids. HfO₂'s ≈ 24 is roughly 4 electronic and 20 ionic; SiO₂'s is 2.1 and 1.8. Ions stop following near 10¹² Hz, electrons near 10¹⁵ Hz; transistors run at 100 MHz–10 GHz. Electronic polarisation alone cannot reach 20–25, so a large k is ionic: k = n² + klat, klat ∝ NZ*²/(μωTO²) — a large effective charge and a soft restoring force; the ionic masses cancel. M07 §2, M07b §6.1
Born effective charge
Z*; transverse or dynamical effective charge		 The polarisation produced per unit displacement of an ion, in units of the electron charge. It can exceed the ion's nominal charge. Beyond the notes. Hf in cubic HfO₂: 5.85 calculated, against a nominal +4 (Zhao & Vanderbilt), because charge flows along the partly covalent Hf–O bond as its length changes. Ti in titanate perovskites: about +7 against a formal +4 (Ti 3d–O 2p hybridisation). Ionic character is only a first estimate: Al₂O₃ is more ionic than SiO₂ yet reaches only k = 9, its bonds stiff and its effective charges modest. With a soft polar vibration it gives HfO₂ and ZrO₂ k of 20–25 (Robertson's incipient ferroelectrics) and the titanates hundreds or thousands. The same soft modes scatter channel electrons. M07 §§2, 8.4, M07b §6.1
Amorphous and polycrystalline films
grain, grain boundary; crystallisation		 An amorphous solid has no long-range order: each atom has a regular set of neighbours, but the arrangement does not repeat. A polycrystalline film is many small crystals, or grains, each ordered and differently oriented; grains meet at a grain boundary, a thin disordered region of broken and strained bonds. Notes' table (p. 49): SiO₂, Si₃N₄ and Al₂O₃ amorphous; HfO₂ and ZrO₂ monoclinic; Ta₂O₅ orthorhombic. HfO₂ crystallises in a 1000 °C anneal (notes). Robertson's criterion: stay amorphous through processing up to 1000 °C for 5 s. Wolf: a film that becomes polycrystalline becomes leaky. Grain boundaries are leakage paths (Wilk) and diffusion paths for boron or phosphorus from a polysilicon gate (Robertson), and k varies from grain to grain. The leakage penalty proved smaller than feared; the diffusion and uniformity arguments stand. A BST film, by contrast, must be crystalline to reach high k. M07 §5.4, M07b §7.3
Glass former
coordination number		 A solid whose network absorbs disorder without breaking bonds, and so stays amorphous. The coordination number is the number of neighbours each atom has. Beyond the notes. SiO₂: each Si has 4 O neighbours and each O bridges 2 Si through a freely variable angle — an excellent glass former. Monoclinic HfO₂ and ZrO₂: 7 O neighbours per metal atom (8 in the cubic and tetragonal forms, each O bonded to 4 metals). Robertson: Al₂O₃ the next best after SiO₂, Ta₂O₅ moderately good. Ionic bonds and high coordination make HfO₂ and ZrO₂ poor glass formers that crystallise at low temperature, and a network that cannot relax keeps its vacancies and interstitials as charge traps. Alloying with a glass former — a silicate or an aluminate — keeps the film amorphous at a cost in k. M07 §§5.4, 6
Silicon nitride
Si₃N₄		 A dielectric distinct from silicon dioxide and from the oxynitride of a nitrided gate oxide. Nearly impermeable to oxygen; impervious to mobile ions. Its deposition is one source of hydrogen in the films over the gate. Notes' table (p. 49): k = 7, Eg 5.1 eV, amorphous (Robertson: 5.3 eV, conduction-band offset 2.4 eV). The LOCOS oxidation mask, a surface passivation layer and a sodium barrier. Its k sets the notes' high-k threshold, and it is the nitride of the ONO capacitor dielectric. M02 §9, M05b §§4.3, 6, M06 §9.1, M07 §1.1, M07b §3.2

Czochralski growth
boule, ingot		 A single crystal pulled slowly from a melt on a rotated seed; the finished crystal is the boule or ingot. 300 mm wafer The wafer diameter whose area economics drove the last transition. 2.25 times the area of 200 mm. Notes' cost per in²: $2 at 150 and 200 mm, $3 at 300 mm. 775 ± 25 µm thick, 50 µm (about 7%) thicker than 200 mm; back side polished; (100) at every size. Per-wafer process costs are shared among 2.25 times as many dies; most 200 mm tools could not be reused. M04 §§4–5
ULSI wafer specification The notes' deep-submicron requirements on the starting wafer, set against the VLSI-era values. Heavy metals < 0.001 ppb (5×10¹⁰ cm⁻³); dislocations ≤ 1 cm⁻²; flatness < 1 µm; bow < 5 µm; carbon < 0.1 ppm; resistivity gradient < 1%; minority carrier lifetime 300–1000 µs. Lifetime is a purity requirement stated electrically: Fe, Cu and Ni are recombination centres. M04 §5
Flats and notch A primary flat ground along the boule is a reference direction; a shorter secondary flat encodes orientation and type. A small notch replaces both at larger diameters. Secondary flat from primary: (100) p-type 90°, (100) n-type 180°, (111) p-type none, (111) n-type 45°. Flats to about 150 mm; notch at 200 mm and above. About six dies lost per flat on 200 mm (professor's estimate). Orientation and type are otherwise invisible once the wafer is sliced. M04 §6.1
Kerf loss Silicon converted to powder by the diamond saw that slices the boule. Roughly a third of the material. Recovered swarf yields solar-grade, not electronic-grade, silicon. M04 §6.2
Lapping
subsurface damage		 Mechanical grinding of both faces against an abrasive disc with water. It leaves crushed and strained crystal below the surface: subsurface damage. 200 mm cascade (notes): sawn 914 µm at 76 µm roughness; lapped to 814 µm at 12.5 µm, 50 µm per face. Flattens the wafer but adds damage the etch must remove. M04 §6.2
Wafer chemical etch An acid etch that removes the lapping damage without adding mechanical damage. 814 → 750 µm, 32 µm per face; roughness about 2.5 µm after. Class: nitric acid with hydrochloric acid. Literature: HNO₃ oxidises, HF dissolves the oxide, acetic acid moderates; HCl does not dissolve SiO₂. The oxidise-then-remove mechanism recurs in CMP. M04 §6.2
Edge rounding
contouring		 Rounding the square sawn edge, and the notch, on a diamond-coated lathe. Notes: straight after sawing, before lapping (Lecture 5: after the etch). Prevents chips that nucleate slip in later furnace steps, and the resist edge bead that holds a mask off the wafer. M04 §6.3
Wafer polishing by CMP Chemical mechanical polishing of bare silicon: wafer pressed face down on a rotating pad fed with a slurry of colloidal silica in NaOH. Abrasive about 10 nm. Frictional heat makes the NaOH oxidise the silicon; the products are sodium silicates, Na₂SiO₃ and Na₄SiO₄; the softer oxidised layer is abraded (Knoop about 600 against 1150 for Si). 750 → 725 µm; roughness 2.5 µm → under 100 Å. Double-side polished at 300 mm. The same process later planarizes interconnect (M13). M04 §6.4

With carrier and platen turning in the same sense at the same rate the relative velocity is ωd everywhere, so removal is uniform. Lecture 5 described opposite senses. Sets how the rotation rates are chosen; taken up in M13. M04 §6.4
PACE
plasma-assisted chemical etching		 A spatially confined plasma scanned over the wafer, removing silicon chemically with fluorine (from SF₆) where it sits. Notes: ions far below 1 eV and no RIE-type subsurface damage. An unbiased surface in a plasma receives ions of order 10 eV; the damage-free removal comes from its chemical nature and the absence of bias. Corrects local flatness that global CMP cannot. M04 §6.4

Defects and charges

TermWhat it isKey factsWhy it matters
The four oxide charges
Qi = Qf + Qit + Qot + Qm		 The single interface-charge term of the threshold equation resolved into fixed, interface trapped, oxide trapped and mobile ionic charge, classified by position. Each is quoted as Q in C/cm² or N in cm⁻², with Q = qN. Notes: Qm and Qf are “not well controlled”; their source, Wolf, says “not only well controlled”. Qit and Qot are the ongoing reliability problem. Two are fixed in amount at manufacture; the other two grow in service. M05b §§1, 7, M03 §3
Fixed oxide charge
Qf, Nf		 A sheet of positive, immobile charge in the transition region, attributed to incompletely oxidised silicon. Two atomic models are proposed, neither shown conclusively correct: an excess (trivalent) Si⁺, or an excess-oxygen centre, a non-bridging oxygen that has lost an electron. Within about 2 nm of the interface. Set by the last high-temperature step and its ambient; minimised by a high-temperature inert anneal, argon preferred to nitrogen. About a third as large on (100) as on (111). Unchanged by the hydrogen anneal. Shifts the C–V curve without stretching it. M05b §2
Interface trapped charge
Qit, Nit, Dit; interface trap, surface state		 Charge in states in the silicon band gap localised at the silicon surface, which exchange carriers with the silicon and so change charge as the gate moves the surface Fermi level. Clean surfaces in vacuum: about 10¹⁵ cm⁻²; one in 1000 of the (100) atoms gives 6.8×10¹¹ cm⁻² and 0.63 V through 20 nm; after a hydrogen anneal as low as 10¹⁰ cm⁻². Notes' four effects: surface potential, generation–recombination leakage, a capacitance Cit, weakening of the oxide (a correlation in Wolf). Stretches the C–V curve; regenerated in service by hot carriers. M05b §3
Dangling bond
trivalent silicon		 An sp³ orbital on a silicon with three bonds instead of four, overlapping nothing and holding one unpaired electron when neutral. Neither bonding nor antibonding, so its level lies in the gap. Can hold zero, one or two electrons: positive, neutral or negative. On oxidised (111) silicon about 10¹³ cm⁻², roughly constant for oxidation at 300–800 °C (Stesmans). The origin of interface traps. Lecture 7: left by incomplete, abrupt oxidation; literature: set by lattice mismatch and interfacial stress. M05a §1, M05b §§3.1–3.2
Amphoteric trap A trap that acts as a donor (0 → +) with the Fermi level low in the gap and as an acceptor (0 → −) with it high, neutral in between. The 0/− transition lies above the +/0 transition by the correlation energy, the extra repulsion a second electron meets in an occupied orbital. The dangling bond is the example; in an n-channel device at inversion its interface traps are negative, so VT rises. M05b §§3.1, 4.3
Pb centre The interfacial dangling bond identified by electron spin resonance: an interface silicon back-bonded to three silicons with an unpaired electron in the fourth orbital, •Si≡Si3. Pb on (111); Pb0 and Pb1 on (100). Notes: “a deep trap level near the Si midgap”. Measured on dry-oxidised (111): two levels, Ev + 0.26 eV and Ev + 0.84 eV (Poindexter 1984), about 0.3 eV either side of midgap. The interface trap, named. Fewer survive oxidation on (100). The same centre appears in EEE 5416 M07. M05b §§3.2, 3.6
Si–Si stretched bond
oxygen vacancy		 The bridging oxygen between two silicons is missing and the two silicons bond to each other directly. About 2.5 Å (2.3–2.7 Å by calculation), longer than 2.35 Å in the crystal and shorter than the 3.12 Å across a bridge: the silicons move together. Notes: a continuum of deep levels at the interface, shallow levels in the bulk. A hole trap — in Wolf's word, the precursor of one. M05b §3.4
Si–O stretched bond An oxygen present and bonded but displaced from its equilibrium position on a longer, weaker bond. Nothing is missing. Notes: continuum of deep levels at the interface, shallow levels in the bulk. Theory places the weak Si–O states below midgap, the weak Si–Si states above it. Interfacial nitrogen relieves it (notes p. 37). M05b §§3.4–3.5
Impurity-tied bond A foreign atom bonded to a surface or oxide silicon in place of the bridging oxygen — mostly hydrogen or a hydroxyl group. Notes (after Wolf, as a model): an electron trap, capturing electrons travelling in the oxide. A hydrogen on an interfacial silicon removes that silicon's level from the silicon gap. Explains how the hydrogen anneal passivates the interface while excess hydrogen in the oxide still traps. M05b §3.4
Deep and shallow levels A deep level lies far from both band edges and holds a captured carrier; a shallow level lies close to one edge and releases the carrier to that band quickly. An interface defect with a level in the 1.12 eV silicon gap is an interface trap; a bulk defect has its level in the ≈9 eV oxide gap and charges only when carriers enter the oxide. Decides whether a defect is an interface trap or an oxide trap. M05b §3.5
Generation–recombination centre A level in the gap offering a two-step path for an electron and a hole to recombine, or to be generated. Midgap is most efficient because thermal re-emission is slowest there; capture does not depend on the level's position. Interface traps generate junction leakage: Atalla's 1958 oxide cut it 10–100×. M05b §§3.5, 3.7
Oxide trapped charge
Qot, Not		 Holes or electrons trapped at defects in the bulk of the oxide, so either sign. Sources: growth defects; implantation, sputtering and plasma etching (notes), PECVD and RIE (lecture); hot carriers and F–N current; Si–O bonds broken by field. Anneal above 550 °C (notes; lecture: about 500 °C, Ar or N₂), before metallization; some neutral traps remain. Grows in service; with Qit the ongoing problem. M05b §5
Strained and broken bonds A strained bond has its length or angle forced from equilibrium; a broken bond has come apart, leaving an unbonded orbital on each side. Usually neutral when empty; charged when it captures an electron or a hole that has entered the oxide. The trap centres of Qot (notes: reactions that “bend or break Si–O bonds”). M05b §5.1
E′ centre An oxygen vacancy that has captured a hole. In the α-quartz model the Si–Si bond breaks: one silicon keeps an unpaired electron, the other is left positive. In amorphous SiO₂ some charged vacancies keep a weakened Si–Si bond. In α-quartz the positive silicon puckers through the plane of its three oxygens; in amorphous SiO₂ several forms occur. Positive and paramagnetic, so seen both as charge and in ESR. The dominant deep hole trap in thermal oxide. The atoms rearrange, so the charge outlasts the stress and is lost only slowly — by electrons tunnelling in from the silicon, or on heating — hence the deliberate anneal. M05b §5.2
Mobile ionic charge
Qm, Nm		 Alkali ions, Na⁺ and K⁺, held ionically beside non-bridging O⁻ as network modifiers, hopping between open sites. Drift toward the silicon under positive bias, faster when hot; C–V hysteresis in severe cases. Sources: water, chemicals, solvents, people. Barriers: silicon nitride, Al₂O₃, phosphosilicate glass. Largely gone by the 1970s (Plummer). Its threshold shift reverses with bias polarity — the signature that separates it from trap charge. M05b §6
Electron trap and hole trap Defects in the oxide that capture an electron, or a hole, arriving through the film — by tunnelling, hot-carrier injection or, for holes, anode injection. Trapped electrons lower the cathode field and broaden the tunnelling barrier; trapped holes raise it and narrow the barrier. Hole transport itself creates bulk traps. Opposite feedback on the tunnelling current; both are stage-one breakdown damage. M06 §§3.1–3.2
Neutral oxide trap A trap generated in the oxide by high-field stress current, uncharged while empty. Its creation alone shifts no threshold; it does so only while it holds a captured carrier. Serves as an intermediate state for two-step tunnelling. The origin of stress-induced leakage current; some survive the oxide-trap anneal. M06 §4, M05b §5.4

TermWhat it isKey factsWhy it matters
Three-mask aluminium-gate process The classic n-channel flow: grow a masking oxide, open source–drain windows (mask 1), diffuse phosphorus, strip and grow the gate oxide, open contacts (mask 2), evaporate aluminium, pattern it (mask 3). p-type wafer at 10¹⁵–10¹⁶ cm⁻³; ≈1 µm wet masking oxide at 1000–1100 °C; phosphorus at 1000–1150 °C; ≈0.1 µm dry gate oxide. The gate is drawn wider than the channel to cover misalignment. Obsolete, but the baseline every later replacement is measured against; it leaves no isolation oxide. M01 §7
Photolithography
photoresist, mask		 Patterning by a light-sensitive polymer: resist is spun on and baked, exposed to ultraviolet through an aligned mask, developed to a stencil, the layer beneath is etched, and the resist is stripped. Each mask is aligned to marks left by the one before. A resist bead at a square wafer edge holds a contact or proximity mask off the surface. Every pattern is made by covering the whole wafer and removing material. M01 §7.2, M04 §6.3
Buffered oxide etch Hydrofluoric acid buffered with ammonium fluoride. HF attacks SiO₂ but not silicon; NH₄F replenishes fluoride so the rate stays constant. Isotropic: it undercuts the resist by the depth etched, so 1 µm of oxide etched through a 2 µm resist window gives a 4 µm oxide window. Tolerable at tens of microns, fatal at one micron; replaced by RIE for fine features. M01 §7.3
Thermal diffusion
drive-in		 Introducing dopant through windows in a masking oxide at high temperature; a drive-in anneal lets an implanted dose spread until its profile is nearly flat near the surface. Diffusion proceeds in three dimensions, so a diffused junction spreads sideways under the mask edge. The lateral spread is what forced the gate overlap that the self-aligned gate removed. M01 §7.3, M02 §§3.1, 5.1
Ion implantation Firing dopant ions at the wafer with enough energy to embed them at a controlled depth, around the projected range Rp with straggle ΔRp. Above a threshold dose, about 10¹⁵ cm⁻² (Plummer), the implanted layer is left amorphous; threshold-adjust (near 10¹² cm⁻²) and LDD (1–5×10¹³ cm⁻²) doses lie below it and leave the layer damaged but crystalline. An anneal restores the crystal and moves the dopant onto substitutional sites, where it is active. Medium-current implanters for moderate doses (LDD), high-current for heavy doses (contact region). Self-aligns to the gate and places dopant where no diffusion could. M03 §9, M02 §6.1
Channelling Ions travelling far down open corridors between rows of atoms in crystalline silicon. Adds a deep, poorly controlled tail to the implanted profile. An amorphous screen oxide deflects the ions so they enter at an effectively random angle. The textbooks' reason for implanting through a screen oxide. M03 §9.2
Threshold-adjust implant A low-dose implant into the channel region that changes the doping in the first few tens of nanometres without disturbing the bulk. Notes: boron for both n- and p-channel devices; drawn as a peak near 10¹⁷ cm⁻³ just below the surface over a 10¹⁶ cm⁻³ background. Energies 25–100 keV. Boron raises the n-channel VT and makes the p-channel VT less negative; a heavier dose also thins the channel depletion region against DIBL. M03 §9.1, M02 §5.1, M05 §3.1
Counter-doping
compensation		 Implanting dopant of the opposite type into doped silicon; the net doping is the difference of the concentrations, and the type flips if the implant outnumbers the background. 10¹⁶ − 10¹⁵ = 9×10¹⁵ cm⁻³, not 10¹: bring both to one power of ten before subtracting. Boron into an n-well under an n⁺ gate makes the buried channel; donors into the p-type channel under a midgap metal gate do the same. M03 §9.1, M02 §8, M07 §9
Transient enhanced diffusion A short burst of accelerated diffusion of interstitial-mediated dopants after implant damage floods the lattice with silicon self-interstitials. Affects boron, phosphorus and indium. Griffin et al. measured indium's enhancement as comparable to boron's; Wolf states indium is less susceptible. Limits how steep an implanted channel profile stays; treated in M09. M02 §5.3
Chemical vapour deposition
CVD		 Deposition by chemical reaction of precursor gases at the heated wafer, which lets the film reach surfaces that do not face a source. The reactants are present together. Notes (p. 65): two or more gases mixed in the chamber react in the gas phase and deposit on the wafer. Wolf gives both paths, in the gas phase or on the wafer surface; Plummer defines CVD by the surface reaction. Leaves carbon, hydrogen or chlorine from the precursor (Robertson). Conformal and uniform over large areas. ALD is CVD with its two reactions separated in time. M07 §7.1, M07b §5.1
Reaction-limited and transport-limited deposition
reaction-rate-limited; mass-flow-limited		 A CVD film grows by two steps in series, transport of precursor to the surface and reaction on it, and the slower sets the rate. Reaction-limited: R = R0 exp(−Ea/kT), rising exponentially with temperature. Transport-limited: nearly independent of it. TAETO for Ta₂O₅ (Wolf): reaction-rate limited from 450 to 485 °C, Ea = 1.3 eV; mass-flow limited above 485 °C. The rate doubles every ΔT = kT² ln 2/Ea, 25 °C at 467 °C. BST from non-adducted precursors: crossover near 480 °C (Wolf's figure labels about 475 °C). Reaction-limited growth is conformal, because precursor reaches every surface at the same concentration; DRAM Ta₂O₅ and conformal BST are run there, with the temperature held tightly. Here k is Boltzmann's constant. M07b §§5.1, 8.3
Step coverage
conformality		 The ratio of the film thickness on a sidewall or at the bottom of a feature to its thickness on the flat top surface, usually in per cent; 100% is a perfectly conformal film. ALD: 100% (notes). BST: MOCVD 80%, sputtering and sol-gel 60% (notes; Wolf gives bounds — MOCVD can exceed 80%, the others typically below 60%). Non-adducted MOCVD BST: 100% at 480 °C. Sputtered films are thinner on vertical walls. A capacitor folded into a trench or around a tall pedestal needs it; it decided the regime and the method. M07 §§7.1–7.2, M07b §§5.1, 8.1, 8.3
LPCVD
low-pressure chemical vapour deposition		 Deposition by gases reacting at a heated surface under reduced pressure. Gives conformal films. Spacer oxide: Plummer gives silane with oxygen near 400 °C or dichlorosilane with nitrous oxide near 900 °C (the lecture named silane and nitrous oxide). LPCVD-W and WSix use WF₆ and SiH₄ and bring fluorine into the gate oxide. DRAM Ta₂O₅: the most common method (Wolf), from TAETO or TATDMAE with O₂ at 1–4 Torr and 450–485 °C (notes). Makes the sidewall spacer and the Ta₂O₅ capacitor dielectric. M02 §6.1, M06 §9.4, M07b §5.1
MOCVD
metal-organic chemical vapour deposition		 CVD in which the metal arrives in a metal-organic precursor molecule. Listed for Ta₂O₅ (notes pp. 54, 58). BST is most commonly deposited by it: 80% conformal (notes), with its solid precursors dissolved and flash vaporised. Robertson rates its film purity equal to ALD's. The method that won for BST, for conformality and composition control. M07 §7.1, M07b §§5.1, 8
RTCVD
rapid thermal chemical vapour deposition		 CVD in a rapid-thermal, single-wafer chamber. The notes deposit HfO₂ by in-situ RTCVD or by PVD (reactive sputtering) (p. 66). One of the notes' two HfO₂ routes. M07 §§7.1, 8.4
PECVD
plasma-enhanced chemical vapour deposition		 Chemical vapour deposition of oxide and oxynitride films assisted by a plasma. Added by Lecture 8 to the notes' list of processes that introduce oxide traps. Exposes the gate oxide to energetic ions, electrons and ultraviolet photons. M05b §5.3
Atomic layer deposition
ALD		 Notes (p. 65): one reactant gas is introduced at a time; a monolayer of the first is adsorbed by chemisorption; the precursors are pulsed in sequence without mixing in the gas phase, separated by inert-gas purges, and each surface reaction goes to completion. Notes' advantages: very thin films; low temperature, below 400 °C (typical, not a limit — the notes' own Al₂O₃ process runs at 450 °C); 100% step coverage; a wide range of precursors; low impurity (relative to CVD — Robertson rates ALD purity equal to MOCVD, below sputtered or oxidised-metal films); atomic-level composition control. Switching under a second gives 50–200 Å/min. Thickness is set by counting cycles. It nucleates poorly on hydrogen-terminated silicon, so production starts on a thin chemical oxide that joins the interfacial layer and the EOT floor. M07 §§6, 7.2
Chemisorption and self-limiting reaction
physisorption		 Chemisorption is adsorption by forming a chemical bond between a gas molecule and a reactive surface site — for oxides usually a hydroxyl, –OH. Physisorption holds a molecule only by weak van der Waals forces. A reaction is self-limiting when it stops once the surface sites are used up. The TMA pulse ends when every hydroxyl has reacted; the methyl-covered surface does not react with TMA, and the excess is purged. The temperature must stay below that at which the precursor decomposes on its own, which would turn the process back into CVD. What separates ALD from CVD: each pulse deposits a fixed amount however long the pulse and however large the dose. M07 §7.2
Growth per cycle
t = Ncyc × g		 The thickness one ALD cycle adds; the film thickness is the cycle count times it, once the film has closed. Notes (after Wolf): a few ångströms at most, “as determined by lattice constant” — flagged: the bound holds, the cause is sub-monolayer adsorption, the bulky ligands of an adsorbed precursor blocking neighbouring sites (steric hindrance, Robertson). TMA with water: 1.1–1.2 Å per cycle (George). The notes' 200 Å/min at Wolf's up to 4 cycles/s: 0.83 Å/cycle. Set by the density of reactive sites and the size of the precursor, not the lattice. Excludes the slow first cycles of nucleation on a bare surface. M07 §7.2
TMA and ZrCl₄
trimethylaluminium, Al(CH₃)₃; zirconium tetrachloride		 The metal precursors of the notes' two ALD films, each alternated with an oxidant: TMA for Al₂O₃, ZrCl₄ for ZrO₂. Notes (p. 66): Al₂O₃ from TMA and ozone at 450 °C; the notes do not name the method, Wolf gives ALD. With water: ‖–OH + Al(CH₃)₃ → ‖–O–Al(CH₃)₂ + CH₄, then ‖–Al–CH₃ + H₂O → ‖–Al–OH + CH₄; ozone oxidises the methyls instead. ZrCl₄ with H₂O releases HCl, on a hydroxylated surface at about 300 °C (Robertson). The textbook self-limiting half-reactions. M07 §§7.2, 8.2–8.3
Reactive ion etching
RIE		 An anisotropic plasma etch that removes material vertically and hardly at all sideways; fluorine-based for SiO₂. Ions accelerated into the surface at hundreds of electronvolts leave subsurface damage. The lecture: the only etch used at small dimensions. Forms the spacer; a named source of oxide traps. M02 §6.1, M04 §6.4, M05b §5.3
Ion milling Removal of material by sputtering it with energetic inert-gas ions, with no chemistry to help. Needed where the etch products are not volatile: BST, the other metal oxides and perovskites, and the noble metals (notes p. 64). Film and mask are removed at similar rates, and sputtered material lands again on the sidewalls; small features hinder it (notes). Wolf: dual-frequency plasmas and etching above 350 °C under development. Limits how small and how vertical a capacitor can be patterned. Ru and RuO₂ are the exceptions: in O₂/Cl₂ plasmas they form volatile RuO₄ and etch anisotropically. M07b §9.2
Hard mask A patterned SiO₂ layer that carries the pattern through an etch in place of photoresist. Ru/BST/Ru flow: the storage-node pattern is etched into the SiO₂, the ruthenium and TiN/Ti are etched through between the mask blocks, and the SiO₂ is stripped. An organic resist would itself be removed by the oxygen-containing plasma that etches ruthenium. M07b §9.3
Sputtering
reactive sputtering; RF sputtering; PVD		 Physical vapour deposition in which ions from a low-pressure argon discharge strike a target and eject atoms that condense on the wafer. In reactive sputtering a reactive gas, here oxygen, is added so that the film forms as a compound. Used for Ti, Mo, Co, Ni and W (Lecture 8), and Ru electrodes (notes). It is itself a plasma process. Reactive, in O₂ + Ar: ZrO₂ and HfO₂ (notes). RF drive for an insulating target, on which a DC discharge would charge up (BST). A composite BST target has too short a useful life, because its elements sputter at different rates (notes, Wolf). Deposits the silicide metals and the capacitor electrodes; a named source of oxide traps. Molecular beam epitaxy

Thermal oxidation
dry and wet		 Growing SiO₂ from the silicon itself: dry, Si + O₂ → SiO₂; wet, Si + 2H₂O → SiO₂ + 2H₂. The oxidant crosses the existing oxide and reacts at the Si/SiO₂ interface. Furnace at 1000–1100 °C. Wet is ten to twenty times faster because SiO₂ dissolves about 3×10¹⁹ cm⁻³ of water against 5×10¹⁶ cm⁻³ of O₂ at 1100 °C; the two diffuse at comparable rates. Consumes 0.46 of the oxide thickness in silicon. Dry for gate oxides, where quality matters; wet for thick masking and field oxides. Dry oxide is also more cohesive. M01 §7.1, M05a §§3–4
Wet O₂ and pyrogenic steam Wet O₂ is oxygen bubbled through deionised water held near 95 °C; pyrogenic steam is made by burning hydrogen and oxygen at the back of the tube. Lecture 1 described a nitrogen carrier, which gives nominally pure steam. Deionised water reaches about 18 MΩ·cm against 1–5 kΩ·cm for tap water. Wet O₂ is the ambient the standard wet rate constants are measured in; sodium in impure water drifts in the oxide. M01 §7.1
Deal–Grove model The linear–parabolic growth law, tox² + Atox = B(t + τ): oxidant transport through the oxide in series with reaction at the interface. Thin films grow linearly at B/A (reaction-limited, orientation-dependent); thick films parabolically at B (diffusion-limited). Notes: predicts films thicker than 350 Å well (Lecture 8: 250 Å). Units µm and hours. The thick-oxide model the thin-oxide regime breaks. M06 §8.1
Anomalous region Dry oxidation below 200 Å, where growth runs faster than Deal–Grove predicts. Five proposed models (notes): space charge (O₂⁻ diffusing with holes; its O₂⁻ premise largely discounted, Plummer), micropores about 10 Å across, parallel O₂ and O reactions, a blocking layer, and Massoud's extra reaction sites. None accepted over the others. Exactly the thickness range gate oxides moved into. M06 §8.1
Reisman–Nicollian model An empirical power law for dry O₂, tox = a(tg + τ)b, fitting all published data to zero thickness. b from 0.25 to 1.0. Wolf at 800 °C: a = 0.302 nm, b = 0.704, τ = 13.1 min, so 60 min gives 6.20 nm from a 1.85 nm starting layer. Units nm and minutes. No anomalous regime on this description; not the form simulators use. M06 §8.1

Dry oxidation, lightly doped substrates, 800–1000 °C. The thin-oxide model implemented in SUPREM III and IV, because it keeps the Deal–Grove constants. M06 §8.1
Rapid thermal processing
RTP; RTO, RTA, rapid thermal nitridation		 Single-wafer processing in a lamp-heated chamber: heated at tens to hundreds of °C per second, held for seconds, cooled as fast. Three advantages: leakage falls with growth temperature up to 1050 °C, better boron resistance, higher mobility from a smoother interface. Lecture 9 called all of them RTA. Grows a 2 nm film at a temperature where quality is good, in seconds instead of minutes. Rapid thermal nitridation also prepares the polysilicon storage node of a Ta₂O₅ capacitor. M06 §8.2, M07b §5.2
Cluster tool Single-wafer process chambers arranged around a central vacuum transfer chamber, with a robot moving the wafer between them. Lecture 9's gate-stack sequence: oxidation, nitridation, polysilicon deposition. Impractical with batch furnaces. The pre-oxidation clean can be done in situ, so the oxide never meets air. M06 §8.2
In-situ steam generation
ISSG		 Applied Materials' wet RTO: premixed H₂ and O₂ ignited by the hot wafer, generating H₂O, OH and atomic oxygen in the chamber. Below 20 Torr, in a cold-wall RTP reactor. The rate rises as pressure falls and tracks atomic oxygen. Oxides in the 2.0 nm range (notes); dry oxides stop at 1.6–2.5 nm. Better reliability than furnace or dry oxides of the same thickness. M06 §8.3
Electrical passivation Bonding the surface silicon so completely that few electrically active states remain: requirement 10 of a gate dielectric. A terminated dangling bond is passivated; one left open is unpassivated. Atalla's group at Bell Laboratories, 1958: a thin oxide over an emerging junction cut its leakage 10–100×. ≡Si• + H → ≡Si–H leaves no level in the gap. Distinct from the protective overcoat. M05 §4, M05b §4.1
Hydrogen anneal
forming gas; post-metallization anneal, sinter, alloy anneal		 An anneal in hydrogen that ties up the dangling bonds oxidation left: H₂ diffuses to the interface, dissociates, and H bonds to the trivalent silicon. Notes: 100% H₂, or 4% H₂ in N₂ (forming gas). 400–450 °C (lecture; Plummer, about 30 min); ≈450 °C (Wolf). Lecture 7's 3% H₂ in 97% argon is flagged: forming gas is H₂ in nitrogen. Pure hydrogen burns in air between about 4% and 75%, but flammability belongs to the mixture: H₂ in N₂ stays non-flammable up to about 5.5%, in Ar about 3.1%, so the notes' 4% and the lecture's 3% are both non-flammable. The last thermal step, after metallization, because aluminium caps it at 450 °C. Leaves fixed charge unchanged. Platinum catalyses the reduction of BST by its hydrogen, so platinum is a poor top electrode (Wolf); Ru/Ta₂O₅/Ru capacitors leaked much less after it at 400 °C. M05b §4.1, M07b §§5.4, 9.1
Densification anneal An anneal in oxygen that brings as-deposited Ta₂O₅ — oxygen deficient and amorphous, and so leaky (notes) — toward stoichiometry, and crystallises it. Notes (p. 58): O₂ anneal or RTA in oxygen at 600–700 °C. Wolf: conventional, a 500 °C furnace step in ultraviolet-generated ozone; rapid thermal, 600 °C in an oxygen plasma then above 700 °C, reported to cut the leakage at 1 V by up to six orders. Keep the peak below about 800 °C, above which silicon diffuses through the nitride barrier and forms SiO₂. Oxygen is thought to fill the vacancies (Wolf's accepted interpretation, not an established result); hydrocarbons and water leave, shrinking the film and its EOT. Later furnace steps at 800 °C for an hour or more make the leakage rise steeply. M07b §5.3
Post-deposition anneal
PDA		 An anneal after a high-k film is deposited, chiefly to reduce its leakage. ZrO₂ (notes): O₂ or inert gas, 400–600 °C — flagged: Wolf's range for significant SiO₂ growth at the ZrO₂/Si interface; his leakage anneal is 400–500 °C. BST (notes): after the top electrode, 500–700 °C in N₂O, O₂ or N₂; about 550 °C for films grown at 550 °C, nearer 700 °C for low-temperature films. Pr₂O₃ (notes): furnace, 600 °C in dry N₂ (5 min, Wolf), or RTA at 1000 °C for 15 s in N₂. Its oxygen can cross ZrO₂ or HfO₂ and grow SiO₂ beneath — a kinetic route to an interfacial layer. Deuterium anneal The same anneal with deuterium in place of hydrogen. Reduces hot-electron degradation by more than an order of magnitude (Lyding, Hess & Kizilyalli 1996). Shows that Si–H bond breaking drives hot-carrier damage. M05b §4.3
Oxide-trap anneal A heat treatment that repairs the broken and strained bonds holding Qot. Notes: above 550 °C (Lecture 8: about 500 °C, flagged); ambient Ar or N₂ (lecture). Some neutral traps remain. Must precede metallization; traps made afterwards by back-end plasma steps stay. M05b §5.4

The gate half of Chapter 4 (M07): the class, the criteria a replacement for SiO₂ must meet, the interfacial layer, the candidates in the notes' order, and the gate electrode. High-k dielectric Notes (p. 49): a dielectric whose constant exceeds that of silicon nitride, k > 7. Other conventions: above SiO₂'s 3.9; Robertson asks for above 10, preferably 25–30. Notes' three uses: gate dielectric, DRAM storage capacitor, FeRAM capacitor. Notes' table (p. 49), k / Eg in eV: Ta₂O₅ 26 / 4.5, ZrO₂ 25 / 7.8, HfO₂ 24 / 5.7, Al₂O₃ 9 / 8.7, Si₃N₄ 7 / 5.1, SiO₂ 3.9 / 8.9; BST 300, PLZT 1000. interlayer; interfacial SiO₂		 The thin layer of SiO₂ — or oxynitride, or silicate — between a high-k film and the silicon, grown on purpose or formed during deposition or annealing. Band offset
conduction-band offset ΔEc; valence-band offset ΔEv		 The energy by which a dielectric's conduction-band edge lies above silicon's (the barrier for electrons), and its valence-band edge below silicon's (for holes): ΔEc + Eg,Si + ΔEv = Eg. Memory cells and capacitor dielectrics

The memory half of Chapter 4 (M07b): the DRAM cell and why its capacitance is held constant, the capacitor structures, the electrodes, Ta₂O₅ capacitors, the perovskites and BST, and the ferroelectric memory. The perovskite physics is beyond the notes, which assume it.

TermWhat it isKey factsWhy it matters
One-transistor, one-capacitor cell
1T1C; DRAM cell; pass, access or transfer transistor		 The DRAM cell (notes p. 56): a MOS capacitor connected to the bit line through an n-channel MOSFET switch, the pass transistor, whose gate is controlled by the word line. A dynamic RAM stores each bit as charge on the capacitor; a static RAM cell is a latch of cross-coupled transistors. Write: raise the word line, drive the bit line to Vdd for a “1” or 0 V for a “0”, lower the word line. The word line is boosted above Vdd + Vt, because an n-channel switch passing Vdd turns itself off a threshold below its gate. Far smaller than a six-transistor static cell, which is why DRAM is the dense, cheap main memory. With a ferroelectric capacitor the same cell is FeRAM. M07b §§1, 10.2
Word line and bit line The word line runs along a row of the array, shared by every cell in the row, and drives the pass-transistor gates. The bit line runs along a column, shared by every cell in it, and connects to one source/drain of each pass transistor. A bit line typically carries 256 to 1024 cells; with the wire and each cell's drain-junction and gate-fringe capacitance, Cbitline is of order 100–300 fF. In a stacked cell both lines are buried in the ILD below the capacitor. The bit line's capacitance, which does not shrink with the cell, sets how small the read signal is. M07b §§1, 2.2, 3.1
Storage node and plate electrode
bottom and top electrode; cell plate		 The storage node is the capacitor electrode connected to the pass transistor — in a stacked cell, the bottom electrode. The plate electrode, or cell plate, is the other, common to every cell in the array — the top electrode (notes p. 55). The notes draw the plate tied to ground. The storage node was doped polysilicon, the plate polysilicon or a metal; high-k dielectrics brought TiN plates and Pt, Ru or RuO₂ storage nodes. The storage node holds the bit; where it is polysilicon, an interfacial oxide can form. M07b §§1, 4.1
Charge-sharing read and sense amplifier The bit line is precharged to Vdd/2; raising the word line connects Ccell to the far larger Cbitline, and the charge redistributes, moving the bit line slightly above or below Vdd/2. A sense amplifier, a cross-coupled latch, compares it with an identically precharged reference line and drives the two to Vdd and 0. Vs = (Vnode − Vdd/2) Ccell/(Ccell + Cbitline) (Taur & Ning; beyond the notes). A cell charged to 1 V on a 1 V supply gives at most about 0.1 V at a transfer ratio of 0.2. The read destroys the stored charge and the latch writes it back. Notes (p. 53): sense-amplifier detection capability has stayed about the same, so each cell needs about the same charge. M07b §§1, 2.2
Transfer ratio
Ccell/(Ccell + Cbitline)		 The factor by which charge sharing scales the stored voltage, measured from Vdd/2, before it reaches the sense amplifier. Beyond the notes. 30 fF on a bit line of 300 fF: 0.09; on 100 fF: 0.23. Taur & Ning take about 0.2. The signal scales directly with Ccell, so the cell capacitance cannot fall with the cell area. M07b §§1, 2.2
Refresh and retention time Refresh: periodically reading and rewriting every cell, because its charge leaks away — what “dynamic” refers to. Retention time: the longest interval over which a cell still reads correctly; the refresh interval is set shorter. Typical worst-case specification about 100 ms. Leakage paths: the pass transistor's subthreshold current and gate, the storage junction, and a capacitor dielectric thin enough to tunnel. 30 fC with under 10% loss in 100 ms allows about 30 fA in total (Taur & Ning). Notes (p. 53): the retention time has stayed constant, requiring about the same storage capacity per cell. The budget forces a pass-transistor VT of at least about 0.7 V and gate oxide above about 2 nm. M07b §§1, 2.2
Storage capacitance per cell
30 fF; N = CcellV/q		 The capacitance each DRAM cell has kept through the generations, about 30 fF (notes p. 52), while the operating voltage fell from 5 V to 3 V and may drop to 1 V. Notes: 30 fF ≈ 1–2×10⁶ electrons per cell — flagged, inherited from Wolf: 30 fF holds 1.9×10⁵ electrons at 1 V, 5.6×10⁵ at 3 V, 9.4×10⁵ at 5 V; 1–2×10⁶ would need 5.3–10.7 V. 1 fF × 1 V = 1 fC = 6.24×10³ electrons. Held for three reasons (notes pp. 52–53): soft errors, unchanged sense-amplifier detection, unchanged retention time. At fixed capacitance the charge still falls with the voltage. M07b §2
Soft error
soft-error rate, SER; α-particles; cosmic rays		 A change in a stored bit caused by a transient disturbance rather than damage: the cell is rewritten correctly on the next write and works normally afterwards. Notes' two causes: α-particles — helium nuclei from the decay of trace uranium and thorium, at parts per million, in the packaging (May and Woods, 1979) — and cosmic rays, which at ground level upset memories through secondary neutrons (beyond the notes). At 3.6 eV per pair, a 5 MeV α stopping in silicon makes about 1.4×10⁶ pairs; electrons collected at the n⁺ storage junction discharge a “1”. Notes: holding about 30 fF per cell keeps the SER low. Critical charge and the neutron environment: EEE 5416 M03 §8, M11 §4. M07b §2.1
Trench capacitor A capacitor etched down into the silicon beside the pass transistor: its area comes from the trench depth, its footprint only from the opening. In the notes' drawing (p. 57) the wall is lined with dielectric, the charge sits in the silicon outside it, and a polysilicon plate fills the trench. A planar 30 fF capacitor on 10 nm of SiO₂ needs about 8.7 µm²; since the 4 Mb generation (1.2 µm) capacitors have been trenches or stacks (Taur & Ning). Formed before the source/drain activation, so its dielectric must survive about 1000 °C: ONO stayed common, Al₂O₃ entered later (Saino). One of the notes' three-dimensional structures (p. 53). Its thermal budget kept most high-k dielectrics out. M07b §§3, 3.1
Stacked capacitor
pedestal; cylinder		 A capacitor built above the finished transistor: a conducting plug rises through the ILD, the storage node stands on it, the dielectric covers its top and sides, and the common plate covers the array (notes p. 61, after Wolf Fig. 4-11). Area = top plus sidewalls. Wolf's 0.15 µm cell: a pedestal 0.15 × 0.45 µm, 0.3 µm high, 0.43 µm²; about 80 fF/µm², so EOT ≈ 0.43 nm — 2.8 nm of Ta₂O₅ (k 25) or 9.9 nm of BST (k 90). Wolf's roadmap row the notes drop: a cylinder in 1999, a pedestal thereafter. Built last, with a wider thermal margin, so most high-k capacitor dielectrics were developed in it. Ta₂O₅, with a minimum practical EOT of about 1.5 nm (Wolf), needed far taller nodes. M07b §§3, 3.1, 4.4
ONO
oxide–nitride–oxide		 A capacitor dielectric sandwich of SiO₂, Si₃N₄ and SiO₂, whose nitride raises the capacitance above that of oxide alone; the dielectric of the SIS capacitor. Notes: 50 Å — Wolf's minimum, below about 5 nm tunnelling gives excessive leakage. With HSG in 0.18 and 0.15 µm DRAMs (notes p. 54); not good for memories above “256 MB” — flagged: megabits, the 256 Mb generation. The thinner-dielectric approach (notes p. 53); its limit forced high-k. M07b §§3.2–3.3
Hemispherical grain polysilicon
HSG; rough surface polysilicon, RSP		 A bottom-electrode polysilicon grown with large rounded grains; the dielectric over it follows the bumps, and the capacitance scales with the true surface area. Area about ×2 (notes pp. 53–54). Beyond the notes: deposited amorphous, annealed near its crystallisation temperature, often after seeding with disilane, and surface migration grows each nucleus into a hemisphere (Watanabe 1995). The third approach; with ONO in 0.18 and 0.15 µm DRAMs, and with Ta₂O₅ for the 1 and 4 Gb generations (notes). M07b §§3.3, 5.5
SIS, MIS and MIM capacitors The notes' three electrode combinations (p. 55): polysilicon–insulator–polysilicon; polysilicon–insulator–metal (silicon storage node, metal plate); metal–insulator–metal. Polysilicon grows SiO₂ at the high-k interface — native oxide, or in later anneals or ILD CVD — which adds to the EOT: 1 nm of it under 10 nm of a k = 90 film leaves 30% of the capacitance. Notes' alternatives: Al, iridium, platinum, ruthenium, TaN, TiN and W. The notes' roadmap is MIM from 1999 to 2014. The lowest-k layer dominates the series stack, and the higher the main film's k, the more an interfacial oxide costs. Production used MIS Ta₂O₅ at an EOT of about 3.5 nm (Saino). M07b §§4.1, 4.4
Platinum-group metals
PGMs; Pt, Ir, Ru		 The six noble metals of groups 8–10 in periods 5 and 6 — ruthenium, rhodium, palladium, osmium, iridium, platinum — which resist chemical attack and corrosion. Notes (p. 55): Pt, Ir and Ru have good corrosion resistance, thermal stability and diffusion-barrier properties (Wolf adds low resistivity). Pt does not oxidise here; Ru and Ir form conducting oxides. Notes' problems (p. 56): Pt — adhesion, etch residue, cost (Wolf: about $1000 per 200 mm wafer for 50 nm); Ru — toxic RuO₄ from its etch; pinholes (Ru, in Wolf). The electrodes that survive BST processing (notes p. 63), all hard to dry-etch. Pt, the most-used BST electrode in development, forms silicides (Ti/TiN barrier, also its glue) and catalyses BST reduction in forming gas; the notes list it as a top electrode, Wolf does not (flagged). M07b §§4.2, 9.1
Conducting oxide electrodes
RuO₂, IrO₂, SrRuO₃, (La,Sr)CoO₃		 Metal oxides that conduct like metals, so an electrode made of one, or growing one at its surface, adds no low-k layer. Notes: RuO₂ bottom electrode, ρ = 35 µΩ·cm (p. 59; about 13 times aluminium's 2.7; single crystals 30–40); IrO₂ and the conducting perovskites SrRuO₃ and (La,Sr)CoO₃ as top electrodes (p. 64). Wolf's text calls 35 µΩ·cm a conductivity; its unit makes it a resistivity, as the notes write it. Already fully oxidised, so no dielectric oxide grows at the interface during later anneals. RuO₂ is easy to etch. M07b §§4.3, 5.4, 9.1
Ruthenium tetroxide
RuO₄		 The volatile tetroxide formed when ruthenium or RuO₂ is etched in oxygen-containing plasmas (O₂/Cl₂). Notes (p. 64): “MP 25.4°”, “both flamable and toxic gas”. Melting point 25.4 °C and toxicity correct. Flagged: RuO₄ does not burn; it is a powerful oxidiser (GHS), which can ignite organic material it meets. Diffusion barrier
Ti/TiN; TiN or WN; TiAlN, TiSiN		 A conducting layer between an electrode and a material it would react with. Ti/TiN under a Pt or Ru bottom electrode on the polysilicon plug (notes p. 63; also Pt's glue layer). TiN or WN between Ta₂O₅ and a polysilicon electrode, against silicide (notes). Wolf lists TiN, TiAlN and TiSiN for BST. TiN is not very stable against oxidising species above 500 °C (notes). Platinum forms silicides with silicon; the barrier must itself survive the BST anneals. TiN also serves as an electrode: a TiN plate over Ta₂O₅ makes an MIS capacitor. M07 §8.1, M07b §§4.3, 5.4, 9.1
Ta₂O₅ DRAM capacitor The first high-k dielectric to replace ONO in the DRAM capacitor, k = 25 (notes pp. 54, 58). Notes' methods: LPCVD (the most common), MOCVD, sputtered Ta then oxidation, anodic oxidation. On an RTN-treated polysilicon node; densified at 600–700 °C; TiN or WN barrier under a polysilicon plate, or a TiN plate. Bottom electrodes Pt or RuO₂ (notes; Wolf's reports: TiN or RuO₂ bottom, Pt top — flagged). Ru/Ta₂O₅/Ru reached k = 65. Etch gases include C₂F₆, CH₄, CF₃Cl, CHF₃ and the notes' “CCl₃F₂”, an impossible formula (flagged). TAETO and TATDMAE The organometallic precursors of LPCVD Ta₂O₅: tantalum ethylate (pentaethoxide), Ta(OC₂H₅)₅, and tantalum tetraethoxy dimethylamino ethoxide, Ta(OC₂H₅)₄(OCH₂CH₂N(CH₃)₂). TAETO is reaction-limited from 450 to 485 °C, Ea = 1.3 eV. The window is the reaction-limited, conformal regime. The film keeps carbon and hydrogen from them until densified. M07b §§5.1, 5.3
Rapid thermal nitridation of the storage node
RTN		 A short, lamp-heated anneal of the polysilicon storage node in a nitriding gas before Ta₂O₅ is deposited; Wolf's example is 900 °C for 60 s in NH₃. Nitrides the native oxide and forms a nitride that blocks oxygen. Notes (p. 58): “an interfacial layer of SiO₂ that is achieved by rapid thermal (polysi) nitridation” — flagged: nitridation cannot produce SiO₂; the layer is a nitride, or oxynitride, there to keep SiO₂ out. Above about 800 °C silicon diffuses through it. Even the nitride is a low-k layer in series: 10 nm of Ta₂O₅ on polysilicon has an EOT of 2.5 nm, only 1.56 nm of it the Ta₂O₅ — one reason metal and conducting-oxide bottom electrodes were pursued. M07b §5.2, M07 §4
Perovskite
ABO₃		 A compound ABO₃ with the structure of the mineral perovskite, CaTiO₃: a large A cation (Ba²⁺, Sr²⁺, Pb²⁺) at the cube corners, a small, highly charged B cation (Ti⁴⁺, Zr⁴⁺) at the centre, oxygens at the face centres. Beyond the notes. Each Ti sits in a TiO₆ octahedron; the octahedra share corners, a framework of Ti–O–Ti chains, and each A cation has twelve oxygen neighbours. Charges: +2 + 4 = +6 against three O²⁻. Its large k is ionic polarisation (Wolf): a weakly held Ti⁴⁺ and a Born effective charge of about +7. The notes' BST, PZT and “other perovskite metal oxides”, with k from a few hundred to a few thousand depending strongly on composition and crystal structure (notes p. 54). Conduction-band offsets near zero rule them out for the gate. M07b §6.1, M07 §5.2
Ferroelectric and paraelectric
domain; hysteresis loop; coercive field Ec; remanent polarisation Pr		 Below the Curie point the titanium moves off-centre spontaneously, every cell carries a dipole, and aligned cells form domains with a spontaneous polarisation: ferroelectric. Above it the titanium is centred on average and there is no spontaneous polarisation: paraelectric. Beyond the notes. A ferroelectric traces a loop: a field beyond the coercive field switches the polarisation, and a remanent polarisation ±Pr remains at zero field. A paraelectric's polarisation is single-valued and returns to zero. The notes' “near-ferroelectrics” are operated a little above the Curie point: large k, no spontaneous polarisation. DRAM needs a paraelectric, whose charge follows its voltage; FeRAM stores a bit in the two remanent states. M07b §§6.2–6.3
Curie point
Tc; Curie–Weiss law		 The temperature of the ferroelectric–paraelectric transition. Beyond the notes. BaTiO₃: about 120 °C. The permittivity peaks at Tc and falls above it as k ∝ 1/(T − T0), T0 close to Tc. Replacing Ba by Sr lowers Tc roughly in proportion; SrTiO₃ never becomes ferroelectric. Bulk Ba0.7Sr0.3TiO₃ has Tc close to room temperature. Wolf's design rule: add Sr to BaTiO₃ to bring Tc below room temperature and ensure paraelectric operation. PZT's is placed well above the operating temperature. M07b §§6.2–6.3, 10.1
Barium strontium titanate
BST; BaxSr1−xTiO₃		 The paraelectric perovskite that was, when the notes' source was written, the most promising capacitor dielectric for future high-density DRAMs. Its films are polycrystalline. Notes (p. 60): 0.5 < x < 0.7, the fraction of A sites held by barium; k = 90 for DRAM films (Wolf: 88–90 at DRAM thickness). Also 300 in the p. 49 table and 250 required by 2005 in the roadmap (700 for “Epi BST”) — three contexts, not a conflict. k falls as the film thins. High k needs purity, correct stoichiometry and crystallinity (notes p. 61); roughness lowers k and raises leakage (notes p. 63). Only a k near 100 reaches the 0.4 nm EOT of a simple stack in a film ten nanometres thick. The perovskites did not become the production mainstream. M07b §§3, 4.4, 7
Titanium content
Ti% = [Ti]/([Ba] + [Sr] + [Ti]) × 100		 Titanium as a percentage of all the cations of a BST film (notes p. 63); oxygen is not counted, and a stoichiometric perovskite gives 50%. Dielectric dispersion The fall of k as the frequency rises, as each polarisation mechanism, and any slower defect or grain-boundary process, stops following the field. Notes: small dispersion “because of the high-k value” — flagged: Wolf gives high k and small dispersion as two separate reasons. β-diketonate precursors
THD; adducted and non-adducted		 MOCVD sources of Ba and Sr: each ion held by two β-diketonate anions, for BST THD (2,2,6,6-tetramethyl-3,5-heptanedionate), Ba(THD)₂ and Sr(THD)₂. Flash vaporisation
liquid delivery		 Solid precursors dissolved in an organic solvent are metered as a liquid onto a hot surface, vaporised at once, and carried as a gas to the chamber (notes pp. 61–62). Lead zirconate titanate
PZT; notes: “lead zirconium titanate”; PLZT		 The ferroelectric perovskite Pb(Zr,Ti)O₃, a solid solution of lead zirconate and lead titanate: Zr⁴⁺ and Ti⁴⁺ share the B site, lead the A site. SBT
SrBi₂Ta₂O₉; notes: “Strontium Bismuth Titanate”		 The notes' second FeRAM ferroelectric, expanded in the notes and in Wolf as strontium bismuth titanate — flagged: SBT is strontium bismuth tantalate, SrBi₂Ta₂O₉, a layered perovskite of perovskite-like slabs between bismuth oxide layers. Ferroelectric memory
FeRAM		 Non-volatile memory storing each bit as the direction of a remanent polarisation in a ferroelectric capacitor; the notes' third high-k application, “an emerging area”. Measurement and characterisation

TermWhat it isKey factsWhy it matters
C–V measurement
capacitance–voltage		 Gate capacitance measured against gate voltage, at low frequency (traps and inversion layer follow the signal) or high frequency (they do not). Fixed, oxide-trapped and mobile charge shift the curve along the voltage axis; interface traps stretch it, and the gap between low- and high-frequency curves measures Dit. Polysilicon depletion holds the low-frequency inversion capacitance below Cox. How the four charges and gate depletion are told apart electrically. M05b §3.7, M06 §6.2
C–V hysteresis A difference between the capacitance–voltage curve swept upward and the curve swept back down. Appears when charge is trapped in the dielectric during one sweep and released during the other; mobile ions give it in severe cases. The notes' Pr₂O₃ capacitors showed none (p. 67). Not the polarisation–field loop of a ferroelectric. Its absence shows that the film has few traps able to charge and discharge on the time scale of the measurement. M07 §8.6, M05b §6
Bias–temperature stress Holding a MOS capacitor at gate bias and elevated temperature, then reversing the bias, while tracking the threshold or C–V shift. A shift that reverses with polarity and speeds up with temperature is mobile ions; a progressive shift that stays, with a stretched curve, is trap generation. The standard test for mobile ionic charge. M05b §6
Constant-voltage stress A fixed voltage held across the oxide while the gate current is monitored over time. A falling current indicates net electron trapping; a rising one net hole trapping or trap-assisted current; an abrupt jump is breakdown. On a 20 nm oxide the trace typically falls throughout and then jumps. The sign of dI/dt shows which change is winning, not which mechanism is running. M06 §§3.1–3.2
Electron spin resonance
ESR; electron paramagnetic resonance		 Microwave absorption by unpaired electrons in a magnetic field, at a field set by the atom the electron sits on and its neighbours. Sees only paramagnetic states: the neutral dangling bond and the E′ centre, not the empty or doubly occupied states. Identified the interface trap as the Pb centre and counts it. M05b §§3.2, 5.2
X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy X-rays eject core electrons; their binding energy reveals the chemical state of the emitting atom. The Si 2p binding energy rises by about 1 eV per oxygen neighbour: 0.95, 1.75, 2.48, 3.9 eV for Si¹⁺ to Si⁴⁺ (Himpsel, synchrotron X-rays). Measured the width of the transition region. M05a §5
Secondary ion mass spectrometry
SIMS		 Sputtering a film away layer by layer and mass-analysing the ejected ions, giving concentration against depth. Showed less hydrogen and an interfacial nitrogen peak in N₂O-grown films. Locates the nitrogen in an oxynitride. M06 §9.3
Ellipsometry Thickness and refractive index inferred from the change in polarisation of light reflected from the surface. Optical, non-contact and fast. Accuracy degrades below 50 Å, needing multiple-wavelength or multiple-angle variants. Several causes: the transition region, up to about 10 Å and so 20–50% of an ultra-thin film, with its own refractive index and dielectric constant; surface roughness; a contamination ad-layer counted as film; parameter correlation in stacked films; single-angle ambiguity. The production thickness measurement. M06 §10
HRTEM
high-resolution transmission electron microscopy		 Imaging a thinned cross-section of the actual stack at atomic resolution. Oxide as thin as 2.4 nm has been measured with it. Destructive and slow. The calibration measurement for thin-film thickness. M06 §10
X-ray diffraction Scattering of X-rays by the atomic planes of a solid, which identifies crystal planes and, in a glass, the distribution of bond angles. Locates the flats on a boule. In vitreous silica it gives Si–O 1.62 Å and a Si–O–Si distribution from 120° to 180°, peaking at 144°. The measured basis of the notes' oxide geometry. M04 §6.1, M05a §3

Conduction, degradation and failure

TermWhat it isKey factsWhy it matters
Tunnelling Passage of a particle through a classically forbidden barrier, because its wavefunction has non-zero amplitude beyond it. Probability falls exponentially with barrier width. A current that flows under bias and stops without it: reversible, not itself damaging. Leads to breakdown only through the trapping and trap generation it causes. M06 §1, M02 §4
Fowler–Nordheim tunnelling
F–N		 An electron tunnels through only the triangular tip of the tilted oxide barrier, a distance φox/Eox, then is accelerated to the gate in the oxide conduction band. Needs Vox > 3.1 V. Measured about 5×10⁻⁷ A/cm² at 8 MV/cm (Wolf), where the tunnelling distance is 3.9 nm. A plot of log(J/Eox²) against 1/Eox is straight. The thick-oxide, high-field stress mechanism, and how floating-gate memories move charge; six decades too small to be the leakage limit. M06 §1.2
Direct tunnelling An electron crosses the whole trapezoidal oxide barrier in one step, straight into the gate. Notes: in oxides thinner than 4 nm, very large below 3 nm, excessive below 1.4 nm. Notes' leakage at 1 V: 1×10⁻¹² A/cm² at 35 Å, 1×10⁻¹⁰ A/cm² at 15 Å — the literature's 15 Å value is about 10 A/cm². Wolf's low-leakage RTO oxide crosses 1 A/cm² at 1.4 nm. WKB: T ≈ exp(−2t√(2m*qφB)/ħ), exponential in the physical thickness and in the square roots of barrier height and effective mass. The leakage that sets the thickness floor of SiO₂ and forces high-k. A high-k film wins by physical thickness but gives some back through its lower barrier and mass: Taur and Ning rank candidates by k√(m*φB). M06 §§1.3, 2, 7, M07 §3
Schottky emission Thermal emission of electrons or holes over the barrier at the interface into the dielectric's bands. Beyond the notes. Robertson: inhibited when both band offsets exceed about 1 eV. Wilk: with a conduction-band offset much below 1 eV, thermal emission or tunnelling gives unacceptably high leakage. The reason for the 1 eV band-offset criterion that eliminated Ta₂O₅, whose offset is about 0.35 eV. M07 §5.2
Cathode and anode The electrode acting as the electron source, and the one acting as the sink. Under positive gate bias the inverted silicon is the cathode and the gate the anode; negative bias reverses them. Trapping effects are stated by electrode role, independent of which layer plays it. M06 §3
Impact ionisation in the oxide An electron in the oxide conduction band gains enough energy to promote a valence electron across the oxide gap, creating an electron–hole pair. Needs more than about 9 eV above the oxide conduction-band edge, so Vox ≳ 12 V from silicon, and fields above roughly 7–8 MV/cm. Confined to thick films; a thin film breaks down first. M06 §3.2
Anode hole injection An F–N electron reaches the anode hot, excites a deep valence electron there, and the hot hole left behind tunnels back into the oxide and is trapped near the cathode. Operates at lower voltage than impact ionisation. A model that has never been completely verified (Wolf). Supplies the trapped holes of positive feedback in thin films. M06 §3.2
Trap-assisted tunnelling An electron crosses the oxide in two short tunnelling steps via a trap level inside it. Two short steps are far more probable than one long one. The extra current generates more traps: a second positive-feedback loop. The mechanism of SILC. M06 §3.3
Stress-induced leakage current
SILC		 The increased low-field leakage of an oxide after high-field (F–N) stress, through neutral traps the stress generated. First reported in 1987. Transient in thicker oxides (traps fill); continuous in thinner ones (filling and emptying balance). Limits non-volatile memory tunnel oxides to about 8 nm for ten-year retention. M06 §4
Soft breakdown
quasi-breakdown		 A sudden but modest jump in gate current, with a power-law rather than ohmic current–voltage characteristic afterwards. Beyond the notes. Below about 5 nm; above about 10.7–11.0 MV/cm. At 2.5 nm, 4.1 V, 140 °C: about 10⁻⁴ A against about 10⁻² A for hard breakdown. Often replaces hard breakdown in thin gate oxides. M06 §4
Dielectric breakdown
TDDB; two-stage breakdown		 Permanent loss of insulation. Stage 1: slow degradation by field or current into a localised conductive path (time-dependent dielectric breakdown). Stage 2: runaway in microseconds, Joule heating a filament — the notes' poly short. Good dry oxide breaks down at 8–10 MV/cm; requirement: above 8 MV/cm. Notes: 20-year life below 8 MV/cm, logic and memory run near 5 MV/cm. Flagged: the two 8 MV/cm roles cannot both hold; 8 MV/cm is the minimum breakdown strength, about 5 MV/cm the operating field. Time to breakdown falls about a decade per 0.3–0.5 MV/cm. A spot 30% thin sees 1.4× the field. Lecture 8: “two types”. Caps the supply: 2 nm at 10 MV/cm allows 2 V, about 1.4 V with a 30% margin. M06 §5, M05 §4.2
Burn-in Finished, packaged chips operated at elevated temperature and voltage, then re-tested, and the parts that fail or change discarded. MIL-STD-883 Test Method 1015 holds the parts under bias throughout. Repairs nothing. Screens the defect-driven early failures that no field limit catches. M05 §4.2, M06 §5
Hot carriers
hot-electron injection		 Electrons or holes with kinetic energy far above the thermal value, gained from a strong field — chiefly the lateral field near the drain. Those with enough energy surmount the Si/SiO₂ barrier: 3.1 eV for electrons, 4.6 eV for holes. Severe at 2 µm gate length, which motivated the LDD. Break Si–H (mean 3.30 eV; about 2.83 eV for H on an interfacial (111) Pb site). Requirement 8 of a gate dielectric is resistance to them. M02 §6.1, M05 §4, M05b §4.3, M06 §1.1
Hot-carrier degradation The chain the notes and Lecture 7 state: a hot electron breaks Si–H → the dangling bond returns → Qit rises → VT rises (n-channel) → ID falls → the device fails. The damage collects near the drain, so the threshold shift is smaller than a uniform sheet would give and larger with source and drain exchanged. Excess hydrogen in the oxide worsens it. Long-term reliability loss: a part that met specification drifts out of it in service. M05b §4.3, M02 §6.1
Negative-bias temperature instability Breaking of interfacial Si–H bonds in p-channel devices held at negative gate bias and elevated temperature. No hot carriers involved. A second route to regenerated interface traps. M05b §4.3
Process-induced damage
plasma damage; antenna charging		 Charge collected from a plasma by the metal and polysilicon connected to a gate — the antenna — driven through the gate oxide. Notes: damage increased as the oxide thinned from 11.5 to 5.5 nm; main source the RF back-sputter via clean; correlates with long-term reliability. A 1996 study found the opposite trend; unresolved. One of the six factors setting the minimum oxide thickness. M06 §10
Polysilicon depletion effect
PDE; virtual electrodes		 A depletion layer of ionised donors at the poly/oxide interface of an n⁺ gate, present in depletion and inversion, acting as a capacitor Cpolydep in series with Cox. At 10²⁰ cm⁻³ and 5 MV/cm it adds 3–4 Å (3.6 Å worked): 3.5% of Cox lost at 100 Å, 19% at 15 Å. Matters below about 10 nm. p⁺ gates, active to only the mid-to-upper 10¹⁹ cm⁻³, suffer worse. Thickens the oxide electrically with no current: less inversion charge, less ID, lower speed. Heavier gate doping is the fix; a metal gate removes it — HfO₂ under TaN reached an EOT of 8 Å (Wolf). M06 §6, M07 §§4, 9
Boron penetration Boron from a p⁺ polysilicon gate diffusing through a thin gate oxide into the channel. Worse as the oxide thins and as the thermal budget after gate doping rises; phosphorus does not cause it (Lecture 6). Resisted by more than 5 at.% nitrogen at the poly/SiO₂ face and by 1000 °C RTP growth; enhanced by fluorine. Unacceptable p-channel threshold variation (notes); requirement 9 of a gate dielectric. In a high-k film, grain boundaries carry it; nitrogen in HfSiON and an amorphous film block it. M02 §8, M05 §3.2, M06 §9, M07 §§5.4, 8.5
Punch-through The source and drain depletion regions merge beneath the gate, the drain lowers the source barrier, and current flows that the gate cannot turn off. Avoided while L > 2W. Heavier body doping narrows W (as NA−1/2) and so reduces punch-through: at 5 V, W = 2.77, 0.880, 0.280 µm for 10¹⁵, 10¹⁶, 10¹⁷ cm⁻³. First at the surface in a uniform body; below it under a diffused well. Sets how short a channel can be for a given body doping; the price of the doping is mobility. M02 §5.2
Drain-induced barrier lowering
DIBL		 The short-channel effect in which the drain potential penetrates along a short channel and shares control of the source barrier with the gate. Raising the drain voltage lowers the barrier and the subthreshold current rises. Notes' remedies: a shallower extension junction xj and a shallower channel depletion region (heavier threshold-adjust implant), whose VT rise a thinner oxide offsets. Why the oxide must thin to permit the doping that permits the short channel. M05 §3.1
Threshold roll-off
VT roll-off		 Notes (p. 51): below 0.3 µm, VT begins to decrease as the gate length decreases, at constant channel doping and gate dielectric thickness. A short-channel effect: the source and drain junctions share control of part of the depletion charge under the gate. Wolf's Fig. 4-2a, a two-dimensional simulation of SOI transistors (Shimada and Ohmi): less roll-off with a Ta₂O₅ gate dielectric than with SiO₂. At the same physical thickness a larger k gives more gate capacitance and a stronger hold on the channel. At the same EOT the gain weakens, because the drain's field fringes through the edges of a physically thick film. A buried channel worsens it. M07 §§3, 5.1, 9
Subthreshold current
IDST; subthreshold swing; off-state leakage		 Drain current below threshold, falling exponentially with gate voltage; one component of off-state leakage. Typically one decade per 70–100 mV (the subthreshold swing); no steeper than about 60 mV per decade at room temperature. The notes take off-state leakage as 10 nA/µm of width. Chapter 4 of the notes writes the swing St (Quantities). Sets the VT = 0.25 V floor, and the yardstick for tolerable gate leakage. M05 §3.1, M02 §11, M06 §2, M07 §5.1
Series resistance The resistance in series with the channel: through the contact, the extension and the region beneath the spacer. Does not fall as the channel resistance falls, so it becomes a growing fraction of the total. Silicide on the source and drain removes its largest component. Why the LDD gave way to the SDE; the full budget is M10. M02 §§3.3, 6.2
Latch-up A CMOS failure in which the parasitic bipolar transistors formed by adjacent n and p regions turn each other on and conduct a large supply-to-ground current until power is removed. Needs substrate resistance to develop its trigger voltage. Suppressed by a heavily doped substrate under an epitaxial layer, and removed by SOI. M02 §§7, 10
Minimum oxide thickness The thinnest usable gate oxide, set by six factors (notes): reliability, tunnelling leakage, SILC, polysilicon depletion, process-induced damage and hot-carrier damage. Hu's production values, 110, 65 and 45 Å for 5, 3.3 and 2.5 V (80, 45 and 33 Å defect-free, at supply +10%, plus the notes' 30% defect margin, which the column does not quite follow). DRAM expected to stop near 3 nm. Defects and tunnelling set nearly the same limit, so neither rescues the other. M06 §7

Quantities, symbols and units

The course works in centimetre units throughout, and the notes' conventions are the course's. The formula sheet carries every equation in full; the entries here identify each quantity and the trap attached to it.

Threshold voltage
VT		 The gate voltage at which the surface reaches strong inversion: VT = φMS − Qi/Cox − Qd/Cox + 2φF. V. Worked n-channel case (NA = 10¹⁵ cm⁻³, Ni = 5×10¹¹ cm⁻², 1000 Å, Al gate): −2.24 V. The p-channel twin: −4.919 V (Lecture 3's answer; the course's constants give −3.51 V). Signs by device: n-channel terms −, −, +, +; p-channel all four negative. The first two terms dominate. M03 §§6–7
Work function difference
φMS = φM − φS		 Gate work function minus silicon work function; the voltage consumed cancelling the built-in potential between them. eV used directly as V. Read from a graph: Al on p-type at 10¹⁵ cm⁻³ gives −0.9 V (the formula with φM(Al) = 4.1 V gives −0.80 V). n⁺ and p⁺ poly on that body: −0.85 and +0.27 V. Negative for Al and n⁺ poly on either body type. The course reads the graph for its answer. M03 §§1, 8
Work function
photoelectric effect		 Energy to remove an electron from the Fermi level to the vacuum level; measured by the photon energy below which no photoelectrons are emitted. eV. Aluminium 4.1 eV in this course (measured polycrystalline Al about 4.28 eV). Gates (M07): n⁺ poly 4.05, p⁺ poly 5.17, silicon midgap 4.61 (Robertson 4.6); band-edge metal targets about 4.0 and 5.1 eV (Robertson). A constant for a metal, not for a semiconductor, whose Fermi level moves with doping. A metal's effective work function on a high-k film can differ from its vacuum value. M03 §1.1, M07 §9
Electron affinity
χ		 Energy from the conduction band edge to the vacuum level. 4.05 eV for Si. Silicon's work function runs from 4.05 eV (heavy n) to 4.05 + 1.12 = 5.17 eV (heavy p). Fixed only at body doping levels; above about 10¹⁸ cm⁻³ the gap narrows, by about 100 meV at 10²⁰ cm⁻³. M03 §1.2
Interface charge
Qi = qNi		 Charge per unit area at the oxide–silicon boundary in the threshold equation; the sum of the four oxide charges. C/cm². Ni typically 5×10¹⁰ to 10¹³ cm⁻². 5×10¹¹ cm⁻² gives 8×10⁻⁸ C/cm², and 2.32 V through 1000 Å. Sources quote the density in cm⁻²; multiply by q first. Positive in practice, so −Qi/Cox is negative for both device types. M03 §3
Depletion charge
Qd (M05: QD)		 The exposed ionised dopant charge in the maximum depletion width: −qNAWm for a p-type body, +qNDWm for n-type. C/cm². 1.39×10⁻⁸ C/cm² at 10¹⁵ cm⁻³, giving +0.402 V through 1000 Å. Signed: the term −Qd/Cox is positive for n-channel, negative for p-channel. |Qd| rises only as the square root of doping. M03 §4
Oxide capacitance
Cox = εrε0/tox		 Gate capacitance per unit area. F/cm². εr = 3.9, ε0 = 8.85×10⁻¹⁴ F/cm. 1000 Å: 3.45×10⁻⁸; 200 Å: 1.73×10⁻⁷; 100 Å: 3.45×10⁻⁷ F/cm². Always per unit area. Convert Å to cm first (1 Å = 10⁻⁸ cm); substituting ångströms is wrong by eight orders and still looks like a capacitance. M03 §2
Dielectric constant
k; relative permittivity		 The ratio of a film's permittivity to that of free space; for any planar film C = kε0/t per unit area. Dimensionless. SiO₂ 3.9; 1.5 nm of it gives 2.30×10⁻⁶ F/cm². The notes' high-k threshold is k > 7. Static k applies at every transistor frequency. Chapter 4 of the notes writes it three ways — k, ε in the p. 49 table, and εox, εhigh-k in the thickness formulas — and earlier modules write εr; all are the same ratio. In the CVD rate equation k is Boltzmann's constant. M07 §§1–2, M07b §5.1
Fermi potential
φF		 The separation of the Fermi level from the intrinsic level in the bulk, as a potential: (kT/q) ln(NA/ni) for p-type, −(kT/q) ln(ND/ni) for n-type. V. 0.288 V at NA = 10¹⁵ cm⁻³, so 2φF = 0.575 V. Natural logarithm. The n-type minus sign must be written in; substitution returns a positive number either way. M03 §5, M01 §2
Surface potential and strong inversion
φs = 2φF		 The total band bending from bulk to surface; strong inversion is the convention that it equals twice the Fermi potential. V. Then the surface electron density equals the bulk hole density. Above threshold φs rises only a few kT/q per decade of inversion charge. A convention, not a sharp threshold. M01 writes φs in (1.3) and ψs in its band diagrams. M01 §2
Maximum depletion width
Wm = √(4εsφF/(qNA))		 The depletion width at strong inversion, where it stops growing. cm. 0.867 µm at 10¹⁵ cm⁻³. Varies as NA−1/2: quadrupling the doping halves it. Use |φF|; the factor 4 comes from 2φF. Result in cm; ×10⁴ for µm. M03 §4
Intrinsic carrier concentration
ni		 Electron (and hole) density in undoped silicon at 300 K. 1.5×10¹⁰ cm⁻³, the course value; the accepted measured value is 9.65×10⁹ cm⁻³. Use the course value. The difference moves φF by about 11 mV and 2φF by about 23 mV. M01, M03 §5
Thermal voltage and constants
kT/q, q, ε0, εs		 The constants every calculation uses. kT/q = 0.0259 V at 300 K; q = 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ C; ε0 = 8.85×10⁻¹⁴ F/cm; εs = 11.8 × 8.85×10⁻¹⁴ = 1.044×10⁻¹² F/cm. ε0 in F/cm, not 8.85×10⁻¹² F/m. Silicon's 11.8 is the course value (Sze & Ng 11.9, Taur & Ning 11.7; M06's poly-depletion example uses 11.7). M03 §§2, 4
Photon energy and wavelength
E [eV] = 1.24/λ [µm]		 The photon energy a wavelength carries, or the longest wavelength a band gap absorbs. Si (1.12 eV) absorbs below 1.107 µm; GaN (about 3.4 eV) below 0.365 µm. The exact constant is 1.2398. eV pairs with µm only. M03 §1.1
Band gaps and barriers
qφox		 Silicon's gap, the oxide's gap, and the conduction- and valence-band steps between them. Si 1.12 eV. SiO₂ 8.9 eV (notes). Electron barrier 3.1 eV, hole barrier 4.6 eV: 3.1 + 1.12 + 4.6 = 8.8 ≈ 9 eV. The hole barrier is the larger. The notes label the 3.1 eV barrier Eox; M06 writes qφox and keeps Eox for the field. These are SiO₂'s band offsets; the high-k values are under Band offset. M06 §1.1, M07 §5.2
Oxide field
Eox = V/tox		 The uniform field across a parallel-plate oxide. V/cm; 1 MV/cm = 10⁶ V/cm. 5 V on 5 nm is 10 MV/cm. The Fowler–Nordheim equation needs V/m and returns A/m² (1 A/m² = 10⁻⁴ A/cm²); fed V/cm, its exponent grows by exactly 100. M05 §4.2, M06 §1.2
Gate-leakage ceiling
Jmax = (Ioff/W)/L		 The largest tolerable gate tunnelling current density: gate current equal to the off-state subthreshold leakage. 1 A/cm² at 10 nA/µm and L = 1 µm (notes). 100 A/cm² at 100 nA/µm and L = 0.1 µm. Convert A/µm² with 10⁸ µm²/cm², not 10⁴. It carries a factor 1/L, so 1 A/cm² belongs to a 1 µm gate. M06 §2
Subthreshold swing
St = 2.3(kT/q)(1 + Cd/Cox)		 The gate-voltage decrease that cuts the subthreshold drain current tenfold; in the notes, a measure of how effectively a MOSFET is turned off below VT. The formula is the long-channel form (Taur & Ning). mV/decade. Ideal ln 10 × 25.85 mV = 59.5 mV/decade at 300 K. Notes (p. 51): 80 mV/decade achieved with Ta₂O₅; smaller is better. Wolf: below 100 very good; Taur & Ning: 70–100 typical. Interface traps add Cit to Cd. The notes' 80 is a short-channel figure: Wolf's Ta₂O₅ device at 0.05 µm, where SiO₂ reaches about 123; the excess over 60 is a two-dimensional drain effect the formula lacks. At equal threshold, 80 against 60 mV/decade over 0.3 V leaks about 18 times more. M07 §5.1
Threshold shift from oxide charge
ΔVT = −Qox/Cox		 The shift a sheet of oxide charge produces. V. One in 1000 of the (100) atoms dangling, 20 nm oxide: 6.8×10¹¹ cm⁻², 1.09×10⁻⁷ C/cm², |ΔVT| = 0.63 V; one in 10⁴ gives 0.063 V. Charge at distance x from the gate counts only x/tox of its interface value: exact for Qit, an upper bound for Qot and Qm. M05b §3.3
Interface-trap density
Dit = (1/q) dQit/dE		 Interface traps per unit area per unit energy in the gap. cm⁻² eV⁻¹; integrated over an energy range it gives Nit in cm⁻². Roughly constant near midgap, rising toward both band edges (U-shaped). Three units for one population: Qit (C/cm²), Nit (cm⁻²), Dit (cm⁻² eV⁻¹). M05b §3.5
Interface-trap capacitance
Cit = q²Dit		 The capacitance from charge flowing into and out of interface traps as the gate voltage changes. F/cm². With Dit in cm⁻² eV⁻¹, Cit = q × Dit numerically. Only traps faster than the measurement signal contribute. In parallel with the depletion capacitance CD, and that pair in series with Cox — the notes' “silicon gate capacitance” is CD; Lecture 7's “parallel to Cox” is flagged. M05b §3.7
Gate length and channel dimensions
L, LE, LL, Z		 L is the source-to-drain channel length; LE the gate length in the scaling rule; LL the drawn gate length; Z the channel width. µm or nm. Drain current scales as Z/L. The professor writes Z; Wolf and Taur & Ning write W, as does M06's leakage ceiling, and W also names the depletion width in L > 2W. From the 0.25 µm generation the node name is not the physical gate length (130 nm generation: 70 nm gates). M01 §§4, 8, M02 §1
Oxide thickness
tox		 Physical thickness of the gate oxide. Quoted in both ångströms and nanometres, in the notes and in lecture; 10 Å = 1 nm = 10⁻⁷ cm. Convert to cm before any equation. A 2 nm film is 10–12 atomic layers in the notes (8 to 12 by the counting conventions of M05a). M05a §3
Scaling rule
LE/tox ≈ 45		 The ratio of gate length to oxide thickness the industry kept to preserve gate control. Dimensionless. Notes' points: 200 Å at 1 µm, 40 Å at 0.18 µm, about 20 Å below 0.1 µm (ratios 50, 45, 50). A design rule, not a law; both lengths in one unit. The 40 Å point divides a generation name, not a physical gate length. M02 §4
Equivalent oxide thickness
EOT; tox,eq		 The thickness of SiO₂ that would give the capacitance actually measured or specified. For a high-k film, tox,eq = thigh-k(εox/εhigh-k) = thigh-k(3.9/k) (notes p. 50). In a stack 1/Ctot = 1/C1 + 1/C2, so the equivalent thicknesses add: tox,eq = tSiO₂ + thigh-k(3.9/k) (notes p. 51). Å or nm. Notes' example: 10 nm at k = 20 behaves like ~2 nm of SiO₂ (1.95 nm). A 1.0 nm EOT needs 6.2 nm of HfO₂ (k 24) or 2.3 nm of Al₂O₃. The notes label their per-generation oxide row “equivalent thickness”. Polysilicon depletion adds about 3.6 Å at 10²⁰ cm⁻³ and 5 MV/cm. The lowest-k layer dominates and sets the minimum (notes): no k removes the interlayer term. Equivalent in the vertical direction only — the drain's fringing field sees the physical thickness. Bias-dependent where poly depletion causes it. M05 §2, M06 §6.2, M07 §§3–4, M07b §3
Poly depletion capacitance
Cpolydep = εsi/Wpd		 The capacitance of the depleted layer in the gate, with Wpd = Qg/(qNp); in series with Cox. F/cm². 9.6×10⁻⁶ F/cm² at 10²⁰ cm⁻³ and 5 MV/cm (Wpd = 1.08 nm). The notes call it constant; it is constant only at fixed doping and fixed gate charge, and it falls as the oxide field rises. M06 §6.2
Junction depletion width and built-in potential
W, Vbi, Emax		 One-sided junction: W = [2εSi(Vbi + VR)/(qNA)]1/2, Vbi = (kT/q) ln(NAND/ni²), peak field Emax = 2(Vbi + VR)/W. 10¹⁶ cm⁻³ against 10²⁰ cm⁻³: Vbi = 0.933 V; W = 0.880 µm at 5 V. Emax 4.2×10⁴ V/cm at 10¹⁵ and 4.3×10⁵ V/cm at 10¹⁷ cm⁻³. Almost all of W lies in the lightly doped body. VR is the drain voltage for the drain junction, zero for the source. M02 §§5.2, 6.1
Implant range and straggle
Rp, ΔRp, Cp		 The depth of the implant peak, the standard deviation of stopping depths, and the peak concentration of a Gaussian profile, Cp = Q/(√(2π)ΔRp). cm, cm, cm⁻³, for dose Q in cm⁻². Both lengths grow with energy and shrink with ion mass. At equal energy a heavier ion stops shallower, not deeper; depth is set by energy (Lecture 3's mass reason is flagged). M03 §9.2, M02 §5.3
Two-junction condition
Rp > ΔRp √(2 ln(Cp/NB))		 When an opposite-type implant into bare silicon crosses the background twice, forming a buried layer. Dimensionless test; for a peak 10⁴ times the background, Rp > 4.3ΔRp. Natural logarithm. Applies only to opposite-type implants. M03 §9.2
Silicon consumption ratio
xSi = 0.46 xox		 Silicon consumed per unit thickness of thermal oxide grown. 0.46 (2.28×10²²/5.00×10²² = 0.456); 0.54 of the film lies above the original surface. 1 µm of oxide consumes 0.46 µm of Si. Each silicon's volume grows 2.2× (20.0 → 43.9 Å³). Texts give 0.44–0.46 (Plummer 0.45) from different oxide densities; the course uses 0.46. M05a §4
Bond energy Mean energy to break one bond, from tabulated means over many compounds. eV per bond: Si–Si 2.30, Si–H 3.30, Si–N 3.68, Si–O 4.68, Si–F 5.86. 1 eV per bond = 96.485 kJ/mol. Means, not site values: interfacial Si–H on (111) breaks at about 2.83 eV. M05a §1
Electronegativity
X		 The power of an atom in a molecule to attract electrons to itself. Dimensionless; Pauling scale as revised by Allred: Si 1.90, B 2.04, H 2.20, N 3.04, O 3.44, F 3.98. High-k metals (M07): Al 1.61, Ta 1.5, Zr 1.33, Hf 1.3. Pauling's 1960 values (Si 1.8, O 3.5) give 51% ionic for Si–O instead of 45%. M05a §1, M07 §2
Ionic character
I = 1 − exp(−(XA − XB)²/4)		 Pauling's fraction of a bonding pair effectively transferred to the more electronegative atom. Dimensionless. Si–H 2%, Si–N 28%, Si–O 45%, Si–F 66%. Metal oxides (M07): Al–O 57%, Ta–O 61%, Zr–O 67%, Hf–O 68%. An empirical estimate of polarity, not a measured charge — and only a rough first estimate of the Born effective charge that sets k. M05a §1, M07 §2
Carrier mobility
µn, µp		 Drift velocity per unit field. cm²/V·s. Bulk, lightly doped: 1450 (electrons), 500 (holes) (Sze & Ng); the professor quoted 1300 and 400–500. Electrons fall to about 1200 at 10¹⁶, 800 at 10¹⁷ and 300 at 10¹⁸ cm⁻³. Inversion-layer mobility is roughly half the bulk value, set mainly by the transverse field, not the doping. M01 §4, M02 §5.2
Drain current
ID, ID,sat		 Linear: µnCox(Z/L)[(VGS − VT)VDS − VDS²/2]; saturated: µnCox(Z/2L)(VGS − VT)². A. Saturation currents stand 1 : 4 : 9 for overdrives 1, 2, 3. Linear form only below VDS = VGS − VT.
