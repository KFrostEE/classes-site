Module 07

High-k Gate Dielectrics

Why the gate dielectric of a deep-submicron MOSFET had to become a material with a higher dielectric constant than silicon dioxide, how a physically thicker film is made to behave electrically like a thinner one, what else a candidate must satisfy, how a film a few nanometres thick is deposited, and the candidates the notes treat — Ta₂O₅, Al₂O₃, ZrO₂, HfO₂, hafnium silicate and Pr₂O₃.

Prerequisites M05a, M06 Read time ~60 min Sources Class notes pp. 49–52, 59–60, 65–67 (Chapter 4); Lectures 2, 3, 6, 9; Wolf Vol. 4 ch. 4; Taur & Ning §4.6.1.6; Wilk, Wallace & Anthony (2001); Robertson (2004)

M06 ended with silicon dioxide running out of room. Direct tunnelling grows exponentially as the film thins, and below about 1.4–1.5 nm the gate leaks as much current as the off-state channel it is meant to control. The transistor, however, keeps demanding more gate capacitance per unit area, because the inversion charge, the drive current and the gate's control over a short channel all scale with it (M02 §4). Capacitance per unit area is the product of two factors, the dielectric constant of the film and the reciprocal of its thickness. Thinning had supplied every increase for forty years; once thinning stopped, the only factor left was the dielectric constant. Chapter 4 of the notes is about high-k dielectrics, which the notes define as materials whose dielectric constant exceeds that of silicon nitride, k > 7 (§1.1), and this module takes the half of that chapter that concerns the gate.

The module runs from principle to material. It opens with the notes' definition of high-k and the history that forced the change (§1), then the physical origin of a large dielectric constant (§2), which the notes assume. The equivalent oxide thickness (§3) and its extension to layered stacks (§4) are the quantitative core, and they explain why the lowest-k layer in a stack sets the floor. §5 collects what else a replacement must satisfy: the notes' two device criteria and their drawback of interface states, then the band-offset, thermodynamic and crystallisation criteria that decided which candidates survived. §6 treats the thin silicon dioxide layer left deliberately beneath the high-k film, §7 the deposition methods, with atomic layer deposition in detail, and §8 the candidates in the notes' order — Ta₂O₅, Al₂O₃, ZrO₂, HfO₂, hafnium silicate and Pr₂O₃. §9 closes on the gate electrode that sits on top of the dielectric. Physics that the notes do not state but that is needed to reason about their statements is marked beyond the notes. The chapter's other half, high-k dielectrics in memory capacitors, is M07b.

1 · Why the dielectric constant has to rise

The notes open Chapter 4 with a definition, the three places a high-k film is used, a table of candidates, and a date by which silicon dioxide was expected to fail. Each rests on one relation, the capacitance per unit area of a planar insulating film, which M03 §2 introduced for the oxide:

C = k ε0t
(7.1)
C capacitance per unit area of the film (F/cm²) · k its relative permittivity, the dielectric constant (dimensionless; 3.9 for SiO₂) · ε0 permittivity of free space, 8.85×10⁻¹⁴ F/cm — the centimetre form, so that t must be in cm · t physical thickness of the film (cm; 1 nm = 10⁻⁷ cm). Parallel-plate result: valid when the film is much thinner than the plate is wide, which every gate dielectric is. For SiO₂ it is Cox = 3.9ε0/tox; 1.5 nm of SiO₂ gives 2.30×10⁻⁶ F/cm².

The symbol needs one remark. The notes write the dielectric constant three ways in this chapter: k in the title and the text, ε at the head of the p. 49 table, and εox, εhigh-k in the thickness formulas of pp. 50–51. All three mean the same dimensionless ratio of the film's permittivity to that of free space. This module writes k throughout, and writes ε0 only for the permittivity of free space.

Equation (7.1) offers two ways to raise C. Thinning the film was the route taken for every generation up to the 90 nm generation (§1.2), and M06 §2 and §7 showed where it ends: direct tunnelling current rises roughly six to seven decades per nanometre of thinning, and a good thermal oxide crosses the 1 A/cm² leakage criterion at about 1.4 nm. The notes restate the limit on p. 50: tunnelling current increases exponentially with decreasing thickness, so it may not be feasible to use SiO₂ films thinner than 1.5 nm. The other route is to raise k. A film with a larger dielectric constant can be made thicker, which suppresses tunnelling, while giving the same capacitance and therefore the same drive current as the thin oxide it replaces. That is the whole argument of the chapter, and §3 makes it quantitative.

1.1 What counts as high-k

The notes define the class by a threshold: a high-k dielectric has k > 7, that is, a dielectric constant above that of silicon nitride (p. 49, following Wolf p. 145). The threshold is a convention, and other authors draw it elsewhere. Some call any material above SiO₂'s 3.9 high-k; Robertson's review asks for k above 10, and preferably 25–30, so that a replacement lasts several generations before it too is scaled out. By the notes' definition the nitrided oxides and oxynitrides of M06 §9, whose dielectric constant lies between 3.9 and 7, are not high-k; they were the bridge that carried SiO₂-based films down to the limit.

The notes name three applications for such materials (p. 49): as the gate dielectric, replacing SiO₂ in MOSFETs; as the dielectric of the storage capacitor in dynamic random-access memory (DRAM); and as the dielectric of the capacitor in non-volatile ferroelectric memory (FeRAM). They list the candidates Si₃N₄, Al₂O₃, Ta₂O₅, ZrO₂, HfO₂, barium strontium titanate (BST) and lead lanthanum zirconium titanate (PLZT), and tabulate them:

1.2 When silicon dioxide was expected to fail

The notes date the problem (p. 49): in 2001, for the 0.13 µm device, the gate oxide had a thickness of about 3.0 nm, and for the 0.1 µm device an oxide thickness under 1.5 nm is needed — the figure of the 1999 International Technology Roadmap for Semiconductors, which Wolf cites. Set against the tunnelling floor of the same page, 1.5 nm, the conclusion follows at once: the generation after 0.13 µm would need a film at or below the thickness at which SiO₂ stops being usable.

The notes' 3.0 nm at 0.13 µm describes a less aggressive oxide than leading-edge logic used

The notes, like Wolf p. 145, give about 3.0 nm for the gate oxide of a 0.13 µm device in 2001. The leading-edge 130 nm logic process published in 2000 used a 1.5 nm gate oxide at a 1.3 V supply (Tyagi et al., IEDM 2000), and the 2003 roadmap lists 1.5 nm for high-performance logic at the 130 nm node. At 1.5 nm, high-performance logic had already reached the tunnelling floor one generation earlier than the notes' dating implies, and SiO₂-based films were then pushed to about 1.2 nm physical thickness at the 90 nm generation (Thompson et al., IEDM 2002) before high-k films entered production (§9). The notes' value is 3.0 nm; the physics of the argument does not depend on which generation first met the floor.

The same limit applies in memory: a DRAM cell must hold a fixed charge in a capacitor whose area shrinks every generation, which is the second application and the subject of M07b. The remainder of this module concerns the gate.

2 · Where a dielectric constant comes from

The notes give dielectric constants as values to be looked up. Why hafnium oxide has six times the dielectric constant of silicon dioxide, and why its band gap is smaller, both follow from how the bound charges of a solid respond to an electric field. This section is beyond the notes. It is included because it turns the p. 49 table from a list into a pattern, and because the pattern — a larger dielectric constant comes with a smaller band gap — is the trade-off that §5 shows decided which candidates survived.

An insulator has no free carriers, but its bound charges move slightly when a field is applied: positive charge shifts along the field and negative charge against it. Each displaced pair of charges is an electric dipole, and the polarisation P is the dipole moment per unit volume. The polarisation places a layer of bound charge on each face of the film, opposite in sign to the charge on the adjacent plate, so that at a given voltage the plates must carry more charge than they would across a vacuum. The factor by which the charge increases is the dielectric constant: k = 1 + χ, where the susceptibility χ is defined by P = ε0χE. A material has a large dielectric constant when its charges are easily displaced.

Three mechanisms displace charge. In electronic polarisation the electron cloud of each atom shifts relative to its nucleus. In ionic polarisation, also called lattice polarisation, the positive and negative ions of a partly ionic solid shift relative to each other, the whole cation sublattice moving along the field and the anion sublattice against it. In orientational polarisation molecules that carry a permanent dipole rotate toward the field; it operates in liquids such as water and does not operate in these solids (Wilk, Wallace & Anthony 2001). Gate dielectrics therefore have two contributions.

The electronic contribution is the dielectric constant the material shows at optical frequencies, where only electrons are light enough to follow the field. It equals the square of the refractive index, n². For silicon dioxide n ≈ 1.46, so the electronic part is about 2.1 of its 3.9; for the wide-gap metal oxides of interest it is typically 4–5 (Robertson 2004). Electronic polarisation alone therefore cannot produce a dielectric constant of 20–25. The remainder is ionic:

k = n2 + klat,    klat ∝ N Z*2μ ωTO2
(7.2)
k static dielectric constant (dimensionless) · n optical refractive index (dimensionless); n² is the electronic part · klat the ionic, or lattice, part · N number of ions per unit volume · Z* transverse, or Born, effective charge, in units of the electron charge: the polarisation produced per unit displacement of an ion, which can exceed the ion's nominal charge · μ reduced mass of the cation–anion pair · ωTO angular frequency of the transverse optical phonon, the vibration in which the two sublattices move against each other. In a harmonic lattice ωTO2 = f/μ, with f the restoring force constant of that vibration (N/m), so the denominator μωTO2 is simply f and the masses cancel. Written as a proportionality to show the dependences; Robertson (2004, eq. 11) gives the full expression. Applies at frequencies well below the infrared, which includes every frequency a transistor operates at.

Equation (7.2) names what a large dielectric constant needs: a large effective charge, and a soft restoring force against the displacement of one sublattice relative to the other — a small force constant, which appears as a low optical-phonon frequency. The ionic masses do not enter: a heavier ion lowers ωTO but raises μ by exactly the compensating amount, and in a harmonic lattice a static response cannot depend on how heavy the ions are. The usual first estimate of the effective charge is the ionic character of the bond, which can be estimated from electronegativities with equation (5a.1) of M05a §1. With Pauling electronegativities of oxygen 3.44, silicon 1.90, aluminium 1.61, tantalum 1.5, zirconium 1.33 and hafnium 1.3, the Si–O bond is 45% ionic, Al–O 57%, Ta–O 61%, Zr–O 67% and Hf–O 68%. For Hf–O the electronegativity difference is 3.44 − 1.3 = 2.14, (2.14)²/4 = 1.14, and 1 − e−1.14 = 0.68.

Ionic character is only a rough guide, for two reasons. The first is that the effective charge is not the static charge of the ion. In HfO₂ it is anomalously large — calculated as 5.85 for hafnium in cubic HfO₂, against a nominal ionic charge of +4 — because electronic charge flows along the Hf–O bond as its length changes, which requires the bond to be partly covalent; a purely ionic bond would give about the nominal charge (Zhao & Vanderbilt 2002). The second is that (7.2) has a denominator as well as a numerator. Al₂O₃ is more ionic than SiO₂ yet reaches only k = 9, because its Al–O bonds are stiff and its effective charges modest. Hafnium and zirconium oxides combine large effective charges with soft polar vibrations and reach 20–25; Robertson calls oxides of this kind incipient ferroelectrics, materials whose polar vibration is so soft that the lattice is close to distorting spontaneously into a polarised state. Ta₂O₅, less ionic than either by the electronegativity estimate, matches them. Of HfO₂'s dielectric constant of about 24, roughly 4 is electronic and roughly 20 ionic; of SiO₂'s 3.9, about 2.1 is electronic and 1.8 ionic.

The same electronic structure that gives a large dielectric constant gives a small band gap. In SiO₂ the gap separates oxygen non-bonding states at the top of the valence band from Si–O antibonding states at the bottom of the conduction band, and it is about 9 eV. In a transition-metal oxide such as HfO₂, ZrO₂ or Ta₂O₅ the valence band is again oxygen-like, but the conduction band is formed from the metal's empty d orbitals, which lie much lower in energy, so the gap narrows to 4–6 eV (Wilk et al. 2001; Robertson 2004). Across the candidates the dielectric constant and the band gap vary inversely. The relation is empirical, with exceptions, but it holds well enough to make every high-k choice a trade.

The consequence for the gate is a compromise. A dielectric constant in the hundreds, such as that of the perovskite titanates, would allow a very thick film, but it comes with a band gap near 3 eV and, as §5 shows, a barrier to electrons at the silicon interface that is close to zero. The gate candidates therefore sit in the middle of Figure 4, at k between 9 and 26 and band gaps between 4.5 and 9 eV.

3 · Equivalent oxide thickness

A replacement dielectric must be thicker than the oxide it replaces, to cut the tunnelling current, and yet must behave in the transistor exactly as that oxide did. The notes (p. 50) reconcile the two with a single quantity, the equivalent oxide thickness. It is the most-used number in the high-k literature, and every film in §8 is described by it.

The notes state the requirement: the replacement oxide should be thicker to reduce the tunnelling current yet maintain the same MOSFET drive current capability, and it must be possible to achieve the same equivalent thickness as required for SiO₂. The drive current, the threshold voltage (M03) and the gate's control of the channel all depend on the dielectric through its capacitance per unit area, so two films that give the same capacitance are interchangeable as far as those quantities go. Setting the capacitance of a high-k film, equation (7.1), equal to that of an SiO₂ film of thickness tox,eq gives kε0/thigh-k = 3.9ε0/tox,eq, and so:

What the extra thickness buys is fixed by tunnelling, whose probability depends on the physical thickness, not the equivalent one. For direct tunnelling through a barrier of height qφB and thickness t, the Wentzel–Kramers–Brillouin (WKB) approximation — the standard estimate of the probability that a particle crosses a classically forbidden region — gives, at low bias:

T ≈ exp (−2t√2m*qφBħ)
(7.4)

The exponent is linear in the physical thickness, so at a fixed equivalent thickness a film with a larger k is proportionally thicker and its tunnelling probability is smaller by an exponential factor. The same exponent also contains the barrier height and the effective mass, under a square root, and these are generally smaller in a high-k material than in SiO₂. Taur and Ning therefore rank candidates by a figure of merit that combines all three, k√(m*φB), relative to its value for SiO₂: a high dielectric constant is worth little if it comes with a small barrier. §5.2 returns to the barrier.

The notes put a number on the benefit for one real film (p. 67): an HfO₂ capacitor with an EOT of 12 Å leaked four orders of magnitude less current than an SiO₂ capacitor of the same 12 Å. The HfO₂ film was 38 Å thick (Wolf p. 175), about three times the SiO₂. Across the same 26 Å of extra thickness, SiO₂'s own leakage would fall by far more than four decades (M06 §2 puts the slope at six to seven decades per nanometre). The HfO₂ film gains less per nanometre because its barrier to electrons is lower, about 1.4 eV against 3.1 eV for SiO₂ (§5.2), and its electron effective mass is smaller: both enter the exponent of (7.4) through the square root.

Equivalent in the vertical direction only — beyond the notes

EOT describes the field perpendicular to the channel, which sets the channel charge. A short-channel transistor is also governed by the field that fringes from the drain through the edges of the gate dielectric toward the source, and that field depends on the physical thickness, not on k. Taur and Ning (§4.6.1.6) state that the physical thickness of the gate insulator still needs to scale with the channel length however high k is, and Robertson notes that a very large k gives undesirably strong fringing fields at the source and drain. At a fixed equivalent thickness, therefore, the largest available k is not automatically the best: it makes the film physically thick, and the drain's field reaches the channel through the film's edges. §5.1 gives a second, separate reason for an optimum dielectric constant, found at a fixed physical thickness.

4 · Dielectrics in series

A high-k gate dielectric is almost never a single layer. A thin layer of silicon dioxide lies between the high-k film and the silicon, sometimes grown on purpose (§6) and sometimes formed unintentionally during deposition or annealing (§5.3), and the notes turn to it immediately after the equivalent thickness: "an interfacial layer of SiO₂ will limit the highest possible gate stack capacitance" (p. 50). This section derives the rule for a stack and the floor that the lowest-k layer places under it.

In a stack of dielectric layers between the gate and the channel, the same charge per unit area terminates on each interface, because no free charge sits between the layers, while the applied voltage divides among the layers in proportion to their thickness divided by their dielectric constant. That is the definition of capacitors in series, and the notes write it for two layers (p. 51):

The dielectric stack is not the only thing in series with the channel. The depleted layer at the bottom of a polysilicon gate is a further series capacitance, M06 §6, adding a few ångströms of equivalent thickness. The inversion layer adds another few ångströms, because quantum confinement holds the channel electrons a short distance below the interface rather than at it. Robertson collects all three as an electrical thickness, EOT + tgate + tSi. The gate term can be removed by a metal gate, which does not deplete (§9); the channel term cannot.

A process leaves 0.8 nm of interfacial SiO₂ that cannot be thinned further. What thickness of HfO₂ (k = 24) gives a stack EOT of 1.0 nm, and what does the answer say about the process? Can the same interlayer ever reach an EOT of 0.7 nm?

From (7.6), thigh-k = (tox,eq − tSiO₂) × k/3.9 = (1.0 − 0.8) nm × 24/3.9 = 1.23 nm. The whole stack is then only 0.8 + 1.23 = 2.0 nm thick physically, against 1.0 nm for plain SiO₂ at the same EOT: most of the leakage benefit of the high-k film has been spent on the interlayer, and the high-k layer itself is barely thicker than a few atomic layers.

An EOT of 0.7 nm is impossible with this interlayer at any dielectric constant, because the SiO₂ term alone is 0.8 nm and the high-k term cannot be negative. Reaching it requires a thinner interlayer or an interlayer of higher k — the reason for the silicate and oxynitride interfaces of §6.

5 · What else a replacement must satisfy

A high dielectric constant is necessary and nowhere near sufficient. Silicon dioxide had been the gate dielectric for forty years because it satisfied a long list of requirements at once — the ten of M05 §4 — and a replacement is judged against the same list. The notes (p. 51) name two device consequences of a high-k gate and one drawback. The literature adds the criteria that in the end decided which candidates survived: an energy barrier high enough to stop electrons, chemical stability in contact with silicon, and resistance to crystallisation. Robertson condenses the full list into six: enough k to last several generations; thermodynamic stability on silicon; kinetic stability through processing at 1000 °C for 5 s; band offsets with silicon above 1 eV; a good electrical interface with silicon; and few electrically active defects in the bulk. §5.1 takes the notes' points; §5.2–5.4 are beyond the notes and explain the fates of the candidates in §8.

5.1 Threshold roll-off, subthreshold swing and interface states

The notes' first point (p. 51): for MOSFETs scaled below 0.3 µm, the threshold voltage VT begins to decrease as the gate length is decreased, for a constant channel doping and gate dielectric thickness. This is threshold roll-off, a short-channel effect. In a long transistor the gate alone controls the depletion charge under it; in a short one the source and drain junctions share control of part of that charge, so less gate voltage is needed to invert the channel and VT falls (M05 §3.1 treats the related drain-induced barrier lowering). Wolf's Fig. 4-2a, from a two-dimensional device simulation of silicon-on-insulator (SOI) transistors (M02 §10; Shimada and Ohmi 1996), shows smaller roll-off with a Ta₂O₅ gate dielectric than with SiO₂. The explanation is the gate's share of control: at the same physical thickness, a larger dielectric constant gives a larger gate capacitance per unit area, which strengthens the gate's hold on the channel against the source and drain. At the same equivalent thickness the argument weakens, for the reason given in the box at the end of §3.

The second point concerns the subthreshold swing, St in the notes, which they define as a measure of how effectively a MOSFET is turned off as the gate voltage is decreased below VT. Below threshold the drain current does not stop; it falls exponentially with gate voltage (M05 §3.1), and the swing is the gate-voltage decrease needed to cut it by a factor of ten:

St = 2.3 kTq (1 + CdCox)
(7.7)

The gate voltage divides between the dielectric and the silicon depletion layer, which act as capacitors in series; only the part that reaches the silicon surface moves the current, so the swing is the ideal value multiplied by 1 + Cd/Cox. The ideal, reached when Cox is much larger than Cd, is ln 10 × 25.85 mV = 59.5 mV per decade — the familiar 60 mV/decade at room temperature, which no MOSFET can beat. The notes report that a value of 80 mV/decade can be achieved for a MOSFET built with Ta₂O₅, and that a smaller value of St is better. Wolf adds that values below 100 mV/decade are considered very good, and Taur and Ning give 70–100 mV/decade as typical.

The notes' 80 mV/decade is a short-channel figure. In Wolf's Fig. 4-2b, from the same simulation as the roll-off data, it is the swing of the Ta₂O₅ device at the shortest channel plotted, 0.05 µm, where the SiO₂ device reaches about 123 mV/decade; at 0.1 µm the pair is about 65 and 75 mV/decade, and from 0.2 µm upward both lie near 60 mV/decade. Hence Wolf's wording, that 80 mV/decade "can still be obtained" with the high-k film. The excess over 60 mV/decade at short channel lengths comes from the drain's influence on the channel, a two-dimensional effect that equation (7.7) does not contain, so it is not a measure of Cd/Cox; the long-channel swing of the same device is close to the ideal.

Wolf adds a qualification the notes leave out: for a 5 nm dielectric the optimum dielectric constant is 25, the value of Ta₂O₅, and for a given dielectric thickness there is a specific k at which circuit performance is best. Wolf gives no mechanism; the result is Shimada and Ohmi's device and circuit simulation, the source of Fig. 4-2, made at a fixed physical thickness. Raising k raises the gate capacitance, but the drive current does not rise in proportion, because the inversion layer's own capacitance — the few ångströms of equivalent thickness by which the channel charge sits below the interface (§4) — is in series with the dielectric's and caps the total. In their simulation the delay of a CMOS inverter driving a 1 mm interconnect fell substantially as k rose to about 25, with little to gain beyond it. This optimum is a circuit-speed limit at fixed physical thickness, separate from the fringing-field limit of §3, which applies at fixed equivalent thickness.

The notes then name the drawback (p. 51): if high-k dielectrics are deposited directly onto silicon, they tend to have a high level of interface states, Dit — the density of interface traps per unit area and energy of M05b §3. Interface traps shift the threshold voltage as they charge, scatter channel carriers, and add Cit to equation (7.7), undoing part of the gain in swing. The interface density can be reduced by growing a thin layer of SiO₂, one to two atomic layers, on the silicon to passivate the surface. That layer is the interfacial oxide of §4 and §6: the price of a good interface is a floor under the equivalent thickness.

5.2 The barrier to electrons: band offsets

Equation (7.4) showed that the tunnelling current depends on the barrier height as well as on the thickness. A high-k film is useful only if it presents a substantial barrier to the electrons in the channel and the holes beneath it. The notes do not discuss this, and it is, with Ta₂O₅'s reactivity toward silicon (§5.3), one of the two criteria that eliminated Ta₂O₅ as a gate dielectric.

When a dielectric is placed on silicon, its wider band gap straddles the silicon gap. The conduction-band offset, ΔEc, is the energy by which the dielectric's conduction-band edge lies above silicon's; it is the barrier an electron in the silicon conduction band faces. The valence-band offset, ΔEv, is the energy by which the dielectric's valence-band edge lies below silicon's, the barrier for holes. The two offsets and the silicon gap together make up the dielectric's gap: ΔEc + Eg,Si + ΔEv = Eg. For SiO₂ they are the 3.1 eV and 4.6 eV of M06 §1.1. An offset is not fixed by the two gaps alone: how the gap of the dielectric is placed relative to silicon's depends on the charge transferred across the bonds at the interface, and it has to be measured, by photoemission, or calculated.

Robertson's criterion is that both offsets must exceed about 1 eV, to inhibit conduction by Schottky emission — thermal emission of electrons or holes over the barrier into the dielectric's bands. Wilk and co-authors put the same threshold on the conduction-band offset and add the second route: with an offset much below 1 eV, electron transport by thermal emission or by tunnelling gives unacceptably high leakage. In these oxides the conduction-band offset is usually the smaller of the two, so in practice the dielectric needs a band gap above about 5 eV. Figure 8 sets out the offsets.

Three results follow. First, Ta₂O₅ fails outright: with a conduction-band offset of 0.35 eV (Robertson's calculated value; the measured value he tabulates is 0.3 eV), electrons in the channel face almost no barrier, and the leakage advantage of its physical thickness is lost. This, together with its reaction with silicon (§5.3), is why the material the notes put first — and the one the industry tried first, as Lecture 2 recounted — did not become the gate dielectric; it remained a capacitor dielectric (M07b). Wilk's 2001 review lists its offset as 1–1.5 eV in its table, but the same review already cites calculations (Robertson and Chen 1999) placing the conduction-band offsets of Ta₂O₅ and SrTiO₃ below 0.5 eV; photoemission (Miyazaki 2001) then measured 0.3 eV. Second, HfO₂ and ZrO₂ pass, with offsets of 1.4 and 1.5 eV, less than half of SiO₂'s. Third, the perovskites that carry dielectric constants of hundreds or thousands — SrTiO₃ and the titanates of M07b — have conduction-band offsets near zero, which is why no material from the right-hand end of Figure 4 can insulate a gate. Robertson observes that the oxides passing the band-offset criterion are the same ones that pass the stability criterion of §5.3, because in ionic compounds a large heat of formation goes with a wide band gap.

5.3 Chemical stability against silicon

The notes attach a stability statement to three candidates: ZrO₂ is "thermodynamically stable with Si", Al₂O₃ "exhibits excellent thermal stability when it is in contact with Si", and HfO₂ "shows good thermal stability with Si" (p. 66). Silicon dioxide never raised the question, because it is the product of silicon's own reaction with oxygen. Any other oxide placed on silicon and heated may exchange its oxygen with the silicon.

For a metal oxide MO₂ in contact with silicon, the two reactions to avoid are (Robertson 2004) MO₂ + Si → M + SiO₂ and MO₂ + 2Si → MSi + SiO₂. Both harm the gate. The SiO₂ they produce is an extra low-k layer in series, which by equation (7.6) adds its full thickness to the EOT and negates the point of the high-k film; the silicide MSi is generally metallic and would short the dielectric. The oxide is stable against both when the metal holds its oxygen more tightly than silicon would — when its heat of formation, counted per oxygen atom, exceeds that of SiO₂. Hubbard and Schlom surveyed the binary oxides on this basis in 1996. The oxides that pass are few: Al₂O₃, ZrO₂, HfO₂, Y₂O₃, La₂O₃ and the lanthanide oxides — Pr₂O₃ among them — plus the alkaline-earth oxides, which react with water. Ta₂O₅, TiO₂ and the titanates SrTiO₃ and BaTiO₃ fail.

Ta₂O₅ fails in the way that matters most. At equilibrium, Ta₂O₅ and silicon can coexist only with SiO₂ between them, so a Ta₂O₅ film on silicon grows an oxide beneath itself. Wolf (p. 160) reports that when Ta₂O₅ is deposited directly on silicon, or annealed in oxygen afterwards, as much as 2 nm of SiO₂ grows under it — by equation (7.6), 2 nm of equivalent thickness added to whatever the Ta₂O₅ contributes. The same reactivity at the top surface is why a polysilicon gate on Ta₂O₅ needs a barrier layer (§8.1, §9).

Stability is necessary, not sufficient, because a stable film can still let oxygen through. The notes record the remedy for ZrO₂ (p. 66): the addition of Al reduces the rate of oxygen diffusion through ZrO₂. Wolf explains the problem it solves (p. 174): undoped ZrO₂ has a high oxygen diffusivity, so oxygen from a post-deposition anneal crosses the film and reacts with the silicon to form an interfacial SiO₂ layer, with significant growth at 400–600 °C even at low oxygen partial pressure. The interfacial layer can therefore thicken under a film that does not itself react with silicon, whenever the process supplies oxygen.

Among the three group IVA metals — titanium, zirconium, hafnium — hafnium forms the most stable oxide, with the highest heat of formation, and HfO₂'s high density, 9.68 g/cm³, makes it resistant to impurity diffusion and intermixing at the silicon interface (Wolf p. 175). Those two properties are the chemical reasons HfO₂ displaced ZrO₂.

"Thermodynamically stable with Si" for ZrO₂ was later revised

The notes (p. 66), following Wolf p. 173 and the thermodynamic data then available, call ZrO₂ thermodynamically stable with silicon. Robertson's 2004 review reports that, with better thermodynamic data, ZrO₂ turned out to be slightly unstable: it can react with silicon to form the silicide ZrSi₂. That is one reason HfO₂ became the preferred high-k oxide over ZrO₂. The notes' statement reflects the data of 2001; the current understanding is that HfO₂, not ZrO₂, is the stable member of the pair.

5.4 Crystallisation

The last column of the notes' table (p. 49) gives each material's crystal structure: SiO₂, Si₃N₄ and Al₂O₃ amorphous, HfO₂ and ZrO₂ monoclinic, Ta₂O₅ orthorhombic. For a gate dielectric that column matters as much as the dielectric constant. An amorphous solid has no long-range order: each atom has a regular set of neighbours, but the arrangement does not repeat over distance. A polycrystalline film consists of many small crystals, or grains, each ordered internally and each oriented differently; where two grains meet lies a grain boundary, a thin disordered region of broken and strained bonds. The notes observe that HfO₂ crystallises in a 1000 °C anneal (p. 67), and Wolf states the general rule (p. 177): dielectric layers of MOS capacitors are ordinarily amorphous, and a film that becomes polycrystalline becomes leaky.

Whether a solid stays amorphous depends on how many neighbours each atom has, its coordination number. M05a §3 built the SiO₂ network: each silicon bonds to 4 oxygens at the corners of a tetrahedron, each oxygen bridges 2 silicons, and the Si–O–Si angle at the bridge ranges, in the notes, from 110° to 180° (X-ray diffraction of vitreous silica gives 120° to 180°, peaking at 144°; M05a §3). With so few bonds per atom and a freely variable bridge, the network can absorb disorder without breaking bonds — which makes SiO₂ an excellent glass former, and lets it stay amorphous through any process step. HfO₂ and ZrO₂ are built differently. Their bonds are more ionic (§2) and their coordination is high: in the monoclinic structure each metal atom has 7 oxygen neighbours, and in the cubic and tetragonal forms 8, with each oxygen bonded to 4 metal atoms (Robertson 2004); in monoclinic HfO₂ half the oxygens have 3 hafnium neighbours and half have 4 (Zhao & Vanderbilt 2002).

The greater ionic character of the bonding and the higher coordination numbers together make HfO₂ and ZrO₂ poor glass formers (Robertson 2004): a network in which every atom is tied to seven or four neighbours cannot absorb disorder by bending a two-bonded bridge, and it crystallises at low temperature. Robertson's criterion is that a gate dielectric stay amorphous through processing up to 1000 °C for 5 s; SiO₂ passes easily, Al₂O₃ is a reasonably good glass former and the next best, Ta₂O₅ is a moderately good glass former that was eliminated because it is reactive, and "all the other oxides crystallise well below 1000 °C". The same structural rigidity explains why the high-k oxides carry many more electrically active defects than SiO₂: a network that cannot relax cannot rebond around a broken bond, and oxygen vacancies and interstitials persist as charge traps. Wolf reports electron trapping and reduced channel mobility for Al₂O₃ and HfO₂ films (§8), with the cause of the trapping still unknown when his account was written.

Wilk and co-authors list the consequences of a polycrystalline gate film: grain boundaries serve as high-leakage paths, and grain size and orientation change the dielectric constant from place to place, giving irreproducible properties; Robertson adds that grain boundaries are easy diffusion paths for boron or phosphorus from a polysilicon gate above. The leakage penalty proved smaller than feared — Robertson cites a study that found crystallised HfO₂ leaking about as much as amorphous HfO₂ — but the diffusion and uniformity arguments stand. The remedy is to alloy the crystallising oxide with a glass former. A silicate — HfO₂ or ZrO₂ mixed with SiO₂ — or an aluminate, mixed with Al₂O₃, stays amorphous to close to 1000 °C, and adding nitrogen raises the crystallisation temperature further, enough for hafnium silicate to pass Robertson's criterion. The cost is dielectric constant, which falls roughly in proportion to the glass former added: HfSiO₄ has k ≈ 11 against 25 for HfO₂ (Robertson Table 2). The notes' Zr-Al-Si-O and HfSiON (§8) are films of exactly this kind.

6 · The interfacial layer

The thin SiO₂ layer under the high-k film has now appeared three times: as the floor under the equivalent thickness (§4), as the notes' cure for interface states (§5.1), and as the product of an unwanted reaction (§5.3). The gate stacks the notes describe keep one deliberately. This section explains why a layer that costs equivalent thickness is worth keeping, what the notes describe as the ideal version of it, and how thin it can be made.

The notes' reason is the interface-state density: deposited directly on silicon, high-k films have a high Dit, and one to two atomic layers of SiO₂ passivate the surface (p. 51). Lecture 3 put the same point in terms of how each interface is made. A thermally grown oxide forms its interface by consuming silicon: oxygen reaches the silicon beneath the growing film and reacts with it, so the Si/SiO₂ interface is created inside the original crystal, below the old surface and whatever contamination it carried (M05a §4) — in the lecture's words, the interface is clean. A deposited film forms its interface at the surface as it was when deposition began. Placing hafnium oxide or hafnium silicate directly on silicon leaves an interface that, again in the lecture's words, "is not clean". The bonding reinforces the point. Robertson attributes the quality of the Si/SiO₂ interface to SiO₂'s low coordination, which lets the network relax and rebond around what would otherwise be dangling bonds; the ionic, highly coordinated high-k oxides (§5.4) lack that freedom. And the channel carriers travel within a few ångströms of the interface, so its defects scatter them and trap charge. A layer of thermal SiO₂ keeps the interface that M05b characterised, and the high-k film sits on top of it.

The notes describe the ideal (p. 52): a gate stack whose interface consists of a few atomic layers of Si–O, possibly containing N, forming a pseudo-binary oxide — a mixture of two oxides, such as SiO₂ and HfO₂, whose composition can vary continuously between the two pure end members. Such an interface keeps the high quality of the SiO₂ interface while providing a higher k than pure SiO₂, and a different, higher-k material can then be placed on top of it. The stack the notes write at the head of the same page, SiON/Ta₂O₅/TiN, is one realisation: an oxynitride interlayer, a Ta₂O₅ film and a titanium nitride gate, reported with an equivalent thickness of 1.8 nm (Park et al., IEDM 1998, as cited by Wolf). The notes' ZrO₂ films grown by atomic layer deposition form another naturally: a 9 Å interfacial silicate layer between the ZrO₂ and the silicon (p. 66) — a Zr–Si–O pseudo-binary layer of the kind p. 52 describes. Wolf adds that high-temperature annealing thickens that silicate and converts it toward stoichiometric SiO₂ (p. 174).

Robertson (2004, §3.4) separates the deliberate layer from the accidental one. The accidental layer under HfO₂ usually does not come from a reaction with the silicon, since HfO₂ was chosen to avoid that; it comes from oxygen diffusing through the HfO₂ during the post-deposition anneal and oxidising the silicon beneath, a process that HfO₂ and ZrO₂ even catalyse. Both belong to the family of fluorite-related oxides, which conduct oxygen ions well. Alloying with SiO₂ or adding nitrogen slows that diffusion (§5.4, §8.3). The deliberate layer earns its place three ways: it gives the well-understood Si/SiO₂ interface, whose remaining defects forming-gas annealing can passivate; it serves as the starting surface for atomic layer deposition (§7.2); and it spaces the channel a few ångströms away from the high-k film, which reduces the loss of carrier mobility that the high-k film causes (§8.4).

The trade is the one §4 quantified. Every ångström of interlayer is an ångström of equivalent thickness that no dielectric constant can remove, and Taur and Ning conclude that the interfacial SiO₂ still needed for interface integrity and reliability makes it difficult for the composite EOT to go significantly below 1.0 nm. Deposition adds a further constraint (§7): atomic layer deposition of HfO₂ and ZrO₂ nucleates poorly on a bare, hydrogen-terminated silicon surface and is usually started on a thin chemically grown oxide, which then becomes part of the interlayer. The engineering target is therefore an interlayer a few ångströms thick, of the highest quality, and where possible of raised k — nitrided, or graded into a silicate — which is exactly the notes' pseudo-binary ideal.

Silicon dioxide is grown from the wafer; every high-k film must be deposited onto it. The notes name the deposition method for each candidate as they describe it (pp. 59–60, 66–67) and give one method, atomic layer deposition, a page of its own (p. 65). Sputtering is the removal of atoms from a solid target by energetic ions from a plasma, and the ejected atoms condense on the wafer; in reactive sputtering a reactive gas, here oxygen, is added so that the film forms as a compound. Because the atoms travel in straight lines from the target, the film is thinner on vertical walls than on flat surfaces, and the ions and energetic neutrals that strike the wafer can damage the thin interfacial layer beneath. In chemical vapour deposition the film forms by chemical reaction of gases at the heated wafer surface, which lets it reach surfaces that do not face a source. The notes' own contrast (p. 65) is the key to what follows: in CVD, two or more gases are mixed in the reaction chamber, where they react in the gas phase and deposit on the wafer surface. Wolf, whom the notes follow, gives both paths — the gases react in the gas phase and fall on the wafer, or react on the wafer surface (p. 171) — and Plummer, Deal and Griffin define CVD by the second, reaction of the gases on the substrate surface (p. 511). Wolf's Ta₂O₅ process, for example, is run where the surface reaction limits the rate, because that regime covers steps evenly (p. 156). What matters for ALD is the feature common to both paths: in CVD the reactants are present together.

7.2 Atomic layer deposition

Atomic layer deposition separates the two reactions of CVD in time. In the notes' words (p. 65): in ALD, one reactant gas is introduced at a time. A monolayer of the first gas is adsorbed on the wafer surface by chemisorption. Individual precursors are pulsed onto the wafer surface in a sequential manner, without mixing in the gas phase, and each reacts with the surface in such a way that only one layer can form at a time: the surface reaction goes to completion and permits no more than one layer to be deposited.

Two terms carry that description. Chemisorption is adsorption by the formation of a chemical bond between a gas molecule and a reactive site on the surface — for oxides, usually a surface hydroxyl group, –OH — as distinct from physisorption, in which a molecule is held only by weak van der Waals forces and leaves again. A reaction is self-limiting when it stops by itself once the surface sites are used up: when every site has reacted, further precursor has nothing to bond to, and the excess stays in the gas and is purged. That property is what separates ALD from CVD. Each pulse deposits a fixed amount of material however long the pulse and however large the dose, so the thickness of the film is set by counting cycles, not by controlling time, temperature and gas flow.

The notes' Al₂O₃ process uses trimethylaluminium, Al(CH₃)₃, abbreviated TMA, with ozone at 450 °C (p. 66); the notes do not name the method, and Wolf (p. 172) identifies it as ALD. Wolf's illustration of the cycle (Fig. 4-15) uses water, and the two half-reactions with water are the textbook example of ALD (George 2010). Writing ‖ for the surface:

On an oxidised silicon surface the hydroxyls of the first cycle are bonded to silicon, so the first zirconium atoms are bonded through oxygen to silicon, Si–O–Zr. The notes' 9 Å interfacial silicate (§6) is about three atomic layers thick, more than that single bonding plane, and Wolf reports it for ZrO₂ films however they are deposited (p. 174): it belongs to the ZrO₂/silicon interface, not to the ALD chemistry.

The notes list ALD's advantages (p. 65), and each follows from self-limiting chemisorption. It deposits very thin films, because the thickness is a count of cycles. It runs at low temperature, below 400 °C, because chemisorption needs only enough thermal energy to drive the surface reaction (Wolf p. 172), and the temperature must in any case stay below that at which the precursor decomposes on its own, which would turn the process back into CVD. The 400 °C figure is typical rather than a limit: the notes' own Al₂O₃ process, ALD by Wolf's account, runs at 450 °C (p. 66). It achieves 100% step coverage and excellent conformality, because every exposed surface saturates whatever its orientation or depth in a trench, so the film follows the topography exactly. It accepts a wide range of precursors and gives low impurity levels, and its composition can be controlled at the atomic level, by alternating cycles of different precursors to build a mixed oxide such as an aluminate or silicate layer by layer. Because the wafer needs only to be saturated, not uniformly supplied, the chamber can be very small — Wolf gives 4 mm high — and switched quickly.

One limitation bears directly on §6. Nucleation proceeds readily on a slightly oxidised surface, so production ALD is usually started on a thin chemical oxide formed by an ozone clean — which then becomes part of the interfacial layer and part of the EOT floor (Robertson 2004).

8 · The candidates, in the notes' order

With the criteria of §3–§5 and the methods of §7 in hand, the notes' candidates can be read as a sequence of attempts, each failing or succeeding for reasons that are now visible. The notes treat Ta₂O₅ as a gate dielectric first (pp. 59–60), then Al₂O₃, ZrO₂ and HfO₂ together (pp. 66–67), with HfSiON and Pr₂O₃ at the end (p. 67). Lecture 2 told the same history as a progression: tantalum pentoxide came first and "has got some problems"; the field moved to hafnium oxide, "a better material"; and then to hafnium silicate, which is "more stable". Each subsection gives the notes' statements first and then what they mean. A summary table closes the section.

8.1 Tantalum pentoxide, Ta₂O₅

Ta₂O₅ has k = 25 in the notes' text (pp. 54, 58) and 26 in their table (p. 49); the difference is inherited from Wolf, whose text and table disagree in the same way, and Robertson gives 22. It came first partly because the industry had been developing Ta₂O₅ films since the early 1980s, for DRAM capacitors (Wolf §4.4; M07b), and its dielectric constant is six times that of SiO₂. The notes' subthreshold swing of 80 mV/decade (p. 51) was obtained with a Ta₂O₅ gate, and Wolf's roll-off comparison behind the notes' first device point (Fig. 4-2) is for Ta₂O₅ as well.

Its first problem is the reaction of §5.3: SiO₂ grows beneath it, up to 2 nm. The first Ta₂O₅ MOSFETs therefore controlled the interlayer instead of fighting it. In the notes' account (p. 59), a 2 nm thick oxide layer was grown on the silicon, followed by N₂ annealing at 800 °C for 30 (nitridation); this nitrided SiO₂ acts as a diffusion barrier that prevents oxygen from reaching the silicon, so no further oxide grows during later heat treatments, and a TiN gate electrode is employed instead of polysilicon. The notes do not give the unit of the "30"; Wolf, whose account the notes follow, gives 30 min (p. 160). Wolf reports that the leakage of this transistor was equivalent to that of a MOSFET with an SiO₂ gate dielectric of identical thickness.

"N₂ annealing at 800 °C" as a nitridation

The notes (p. 59) and Wolf (p. 160) describe the 2 nm interlayer as nitrided by annealing in N₂ at 800 °C for 30 minutes. Molecular nitrogen is nearly inert toward SiO₂ at 800 °C: its triple bond is too strong to dissociate at that temperature, which is why the nitridation processes of Chapter 3 use ammonia, nitrous oxide or nitric oxide (M06 §9), and why Lecture 9 stressed that the oxide is nitrided in reactive gases, not in N₂ itself: "This is called nitric oxide. Nitric Oxide. Not nitrogen." The notes' conditions are given above as written; the original report (Momiyama et al., VLSI Symposium 1997, as cited by Wolf) has not been checked, and how much nitrogen such an anneal incorporated is uncertain.

The notes' second approach (p. 60) uses exactly the gas Chapter 3 recommends: grow an interfacial layer on the silicon substrate with NO at 800 °C for 20 s, then deposit Ta₂O₅ by CVD, to produce an equivalent thickness of 2.3 nm. The notes report that this structure showed 1000 times smaller leakage current. The comparison is between two interlayers under the same Ta₂O₅: Wolf (p. 161) states that the leakage was 1000× smaller "than when an oxide was used as the interfacial layer". A similar process nitrided the silicon surface in NH₃ at 800 °C for 30 s instead (Wolf p. 161).

1000× smaller than what

The notes (p. 60) give the NO-interlayer result as "1000× times smaller leakage current" without a comparator. The comparator in the source is a Ta₂O₅ stack of the same kind with an SiO₂ interlayer instead of the NO-grown one (Wolf p. 161) — not an SiO₂ gate dielectric of the same equivalent thickness. The figure measures how much the interlayer matters, not how much Ta₂O₅ improves on SiO₂.

The top interface raises the same chemistry. The notes (p. 60): a barrier over the Ta₂O₅ is needed to prevent silicide forming with a polysilicon gate, and usually TiN or WN is used. The barrier must conduct, since it is part of the gate electrode, and in practice it becomes the gate: the notes' drawing of the transistor (p. 60) has a TiN gate directly on the Ta₂O₅.

The metal gate has its own cost, which the notes state (p. 60): a metallic gate increases VT by about 0.6 V, so proper VT adjustments must be employed, and this produces buried-channel MOSFETs, which degrades their short-channel behaviour. §9 derives the 0.6 V from the work functions. The mechanism of the second half is the one M02 §8 and M03 §8 taught for an n⁺ polysilicon gate on a p-channel device: lowering the threshold by implanting dopant of the opposite type near the surface moves the conducting channel below the surface, where the gate controls it less well.

Ta₂O₅ then failed on the two criteria of §5 that the notes do not discuss. Its conduction-band offset on silicon is only about 0.35 eV (§5.2), so it barely blocks electrons, and it is not thermodynamically stable on silicon (§5.3). Robertson summarises that Ta₂O₅ was a moderately good glass former but "was eliminated because it is reactive".

8.2 Aluminium oxide, Al₂O₃

The notes (p. 66): Al₂O₃ films are made with the precursor TMA (trimethylaluminium) and ozone at 450 °C, have k = 9 and Eg ≈ 8.3 eV, and exhibit excellent thermal stability when in contact with silicon. The notes do not name the method; Wolf (p. 172) gives it as atomic layer deposition, the cycle of §7.2. The virtues are the ones §5 predicts: a wide band gap with a large conduction-band offset — 2.8 eV for bulk-like Al₂O₃ (Figure 8), and about 2.2 eV measured on ALD films, whose gap is smaller (Robertson Tables 2 and 5) — stability on silicon (§5.3), and an amorphous film, since Al₂O₃ is the best glass former after SiO₂ (§5.4). Wolf adds (pp. 172–173) that it is compatible with a polysilicon gate without any barrier layer, and that its capacitors leak 2–3 orders of magnitude less than SiO₂ capacitors of the same equivalent thickness.

Its weakness is its dielectric constant. At k = 9 a film is only 2.3 times thicker than the SiO₂ it replaces (§3), and Wolf reports that the effective k of Al₂O₃ gate stacks is lower still, 5.5–6.0, because a thin interfacial SiO₂ layer inevitably forms between the Al₂O₃ and the silicon during deposition — equation (7.6) at work. Wolf judged that the improvement might be adequate for only a single technology node. Transistors with Al₂O₃ gates also showed lower channel mobility than SiO₂ ones, attributed to electron trapping in the Al₂O₃.

Al₂O₃'s band gap: 8.3 eV on p. 66, 8.7 eV on p. 49

The notes give Al₂O₃ a band gap of 8.7 eV in the table on p. 49 and ≈ 8.3 eV on p. 66; Wolf's table and text disagree in the same way. Robertson gives 8.8 eV for bulk Al₂O₃ and notes that films grown by atomic layer deposition have a much smaller gap, about 6.8 eV, so the gap of a gate film depends on how it was made. Both are the notes' values; the p. 49 figure is the closer to the bulk value.

8.3 Zirconium oxide, ZrO₂

The notes (p. 66): ZrO₂ has k = 20–25, is thermodynamically stable with silicon, has Eg ≈ 5.1 eV, and its lattice mismatch with silicon is only 2.1%. It is grown by reactive sputtering in O₂ + Ar or by oxidising a Zr film in an RTA process; it can be etched in HF solutions; and doping with Al, by co-sputtering from an Al target, produces films of better uniformity. Wolf adds that the Al-doped films are amorphous and have a slightly lower dielectric constant (p. 174) — the aluminate route of §5.4.

By ALD, the precursors are ZrCl₄ and H₂O (§7.2). The films form an interfacial silicate layer 9 Å thick (§6), and a post-deposition anneal in O₂ or inert gas, at 400–600 °C in the notes, is required to reduce the leakage current. Films of aluminium-doped zirconium silicate, Zr-Al-Si-O, have been studied as a high-k candidate: the addition of Al reduces the rate of oxygen diffusion through ZrO₂ (§5.3), and the silicon and aluminium keep the film amorphous. Finally, the notes record that yttria-stabilised zirconium oxide can also be used for MOSFETs. Adding yttrium oxide, Y₂O₃, stabilises ZrO₂ in its cubic, fluorite structure (Robertson), and Wolf reports that such films can be grown epitaxially on silicon — as a crystalline film whose lattice takes its orientation from the substrate's — by pulsed laser ablation, without an interfacial oxide (p. 174).

Three ZrO₂ statements in the notes that the literature revises

Band gap. The notes give 7.8 eV in the p. 49 table and ≈ 5.1 eV on p. 66; Wolf has the same pair. The 7.8 eV value is an early one carried over from Wilk et al. (2001); Robertson gives 5.8 eV, the same as HfO₂ (Figure 4).

Lattice mismatch. The notes, following Wolf p. 173, give 2.1%. Robertson gives lattice constants of 5.43 Å for silicon and 5.07 Å for cubic ZrO₂, which differ by (5.43 − 5.07)/5.43 = 6.6%. The mismatch matters only for epitaxial films, such as the yttria-stabilised ones; the amorphous films of a production gate have no lattice to match.

Stability. "Thermodynamically stable with Si" was the view in 2001; later data showed ZrO₂ to be slightly unstable against forming ZrSi₂ (§5.3).

One smaller difference: the notes' 400–600 °C anneal range is Wolf's range for significant SiO₂ growth at the ZrO₂/Si interface; Wolf gives 400–500 °C for the anneal that reduces leakage (p. 174). The values in §8.3 are the notes'.

8.4 Hafnium oxide, HfO₂

The notes (pp. 66–67): HfO₂ has k = 24–40, shows good thermal stability with silicon, and has Eg ≈ 5.7 eV. It is deposited by PVD (reactive sputtering) or by in-situ RTCVD. It does not form an interfacial layer with polysilicon if the polysilicon deposition temperature is kept below 600 °C and the RTA anneals are carried out at 1000 °C. Crystallisation of the HfO₂ is observed. Leakage currents were four orders of magnitude smaller than those of an SiO₂ MOS capacitor with the same 12 Å EOT.

Each statement connects to an earlier section. The stability is Hf's: the most stable oxide of its group, dense and resistant to intermixing (§5.3). The band gap near 5.7 eV and the conduction-band offset of about 1.4 eV (Figure 8) make it an insulator by Robertson's criterion, though with less than half SiO₂'s barrier. The crystallisation is §5.4's weakness of an ionic, seven-coordinated oxide. The 12 Å capacitor is the one worked in §4 and drawn in Figure 12 — with the physical thicknesses Wolf reports, 38 Å of HfO₂ plus about 6 Å of interlayer — and four decades less leakage than 12 Å of SiO₂. Wolf adds a problem and a result. Like the other high-k films, HfO₂ traps electrons and degrades the carrier mobility in the channel; and in a separate report (B. H. Lee et al., IEDM 2000), HfO₂ films with a TaN metal gate, which has no polysilicon-depletion layer, reached an EOT as small as 8 Å (§9). Robertson traces part of the mobility loss to §2: the soft, low-frequency polar vibrations that give HfO₂ and ZrO₂ their large lattice contribution to k couple to the electrons in the channel just beneath and scatter them, an effect that is small in Al₂O₃ and in the silicates, which lack such soft modes.

"No interfacial layer" refers to the gate side only

The notes' sentence (pp. 66–67), that HfO₂ does not form an interfacial layer with polysilicon under those conditions, describes the top interface, between the HfO₂ and the polysilicon gate. The same report found an unwanted interfacial SiO₂ layer at the bottom interface, between the HfO₂ and the silicon substrate, formed during the HfO₂ deposition, about 6 Å thick in its micrographs (Wolf p. 175 and Fig. 4-17; Kang et al., IEDM 2000). HfO₂ does not sit directly on the silicon; the 12 Å EOT includes that layer.

k = 24–40 for HfO₂

The notes give k = 24–40 on p. 66 (after Wolf p. 175) and 24 in the p. 49 table. Measured amorphous and monoclinic HfO₂ has k of about 20–25: Taur and Ning give 20–25, Wilk et al. and Robertson 25. Higher values belong to the tetragonal and cubic phases, which calculations predict to have larger dielectric constants than the monoclinic phase and which are not the phases of an ordinary film. Use 24 for calculations, as the notes' own table does; treat the upper end of 24–40 as phase-dependent, not as a property of a typical HfO₂ gate film.

8.5 Hafnium silicate and HfSiON

The notes give HfSiON one sentence (p. 67): it is also looked into as a possible candidate high-k material, and exhibits smaller leakage currents than SiO₂ at the same equivalent oxide thickness. The lectures gave it more weight than any other candidate: hafnium oxide and hafnium silicate were named together in Lectures 2, 3 and 6, and Lecture 2 called the silicate the more stable material.

A hafnium silicate is a mixture of HfO₂ and SiO₂, written HfSiO or (HfO₂)x(SiO₂)1−x; it is the pseudo-binary oxide of the notes' ideal interface (§6), and the crystalline compound at x = 0.5 is HfSiO₄. HfSiON is the same material with nitrogen added. Both are responses to the weaknesses of pure HfO₂ set out in §5, and each addition trades dielectric constant for stability:

  • Crystallisation. SiO₂ is a glass former, so the silicate stays amorphous to close to 1000 °C, and nitrogen raises the crystallisation temperature further, enough for hafnium silicate to pass the 1000 °C, 5 s criterion (Robertson). Wolf describes HfSiON as amorphous, with films below 13 Å EOT showing good reliability (p. 176).
  • Barrier and interface. Mixing in SiO₂ widens the band gap and raises the conduction-band offset: Robertson gives HfSiO₄ a gap of 6.5 eV and an offset of 1.8 eV against 5.8 and 1.4 eV for HfO₂. Near the silicon, a silicon-rich composition approaches the SiO₂ interface the notes call ideal.
  • Dopant penetration. Nitrogen in the film blocks boron diffusing from a p⁺ gate, as it does in the oxynitrides of M06 §9, and an amorphous film has no grain boundaries to carry it (§5.4).
  • The cost. The dielectric constant falls roughly in proportion to the SiO₂ added: HfSiO₄ has k ≈ 11 against 25 for HfO₂ (Robertson). The composition is chosen to keep enough hafnium for a useful k while keeping the film amorphous.

"More stable", in the lecture's sense, is therefore the whole of §5.3 and §5.4 applied at once: a hafnium-based film that stays glassy, reacts less, keeps boron out and presents a higher barrier, bought with part of the dielectric constant.

8.6 Praseodymium oxide, Pr₂O₃

The notes (p. 67): MOS capacitors based on Pr₂O₃ exhibited ultra-low leakage current densities of 5 nA/cm² at 1 V with an EOT of 14 Å; k ≈ 31; no hysteresis is observed in the C–V measurements. The films are deposited by MBE between 625 and 725 °C at a growth rate of 0.1 Å/s, and subsequently annealed in a furnace at 600 °C in dry nitrogen, or by RTA at 1000 °C for 15 s in N₂.

One term needs definition. Hysteresis in a C–V measurement is a difference between the capacitance–voltage curve swept upward and the curve swept back down; it appears when charge is trapped in the dielectric during one sweep and released during the other, so its absence shows that the film has few traps able to charge and discharge on the time scale of the measurement. The leakage figure, 5×10⁻⁹ A/cm², is small: Wolf, reporting the same work (Osten et al., IEDM 2000), calls it four orders of magnitude lower than the best results then reported for other high-k materials. Wolf adds that the furnace anneal lasted 5 min, that the films survived the 1000 °C, 15 s RTA with no change in structure or electrical properties, and that the Pr₂O₃ layers were polycrystalline — an exception to §5.4, since these crystalline films were not leaky. The group that made the films describes them differently: Pr₂O₃ grown epitaxially (§8.3) on Si(001), as domains in two perpendicular in-plane orientations (Osten et al., Microelectron. Reliab. 2001). Domains locked to the substrate in two orientations are a far more ordered structure than the randomly oriented grains of Figure 11, so the low leakage is less of an exception to §5.4 than Wolf's word suggests. The polysilicon/Pr₂O₃ interface was rough, so the top electrodes were gold, evaporated through a shadow mask. Praseodymium is a lanthanide, and the lanthanide oxides are among those thermodynamically stable on silicon (§5.3).

Cerium dioxide, CeO₂ — from Lecture 2, not in the notes

Lecture 2 added a candidate the notes do not contain, from the professor's own research: cerium dioxide, whose advantage is a very close lattice match to silicon, so that crystalline, epitaxial gate insulators can be made. Epitaxial growth (§8.3) in a single orientation removes grain boundaries altogether (§5.4) and can give an ordered interface. CeO₂ has the cubic fluorite structure, in which each cerium has 8 oxygen neighbours and each oxygen 4 cerium neighbours, with a lattice constant of 5.411 Å (ICDD reference pattern 43-1002) against 5.431 Å for silicon: a mismatch of (5.431 − 5.411)/5.431 = 0.37%, against 6.6% for cubic ZrO₂. The same material appears later in the course as a polishing abrasive in chemical–mechanical polishing (M13).

9 · The gate electrode

Changing the dielectric changes the electrode on top of it. The notes meet the problem three times: a polysilicon gate reacts with Ta₂O₅ and needs a TiN or WN barrier (p. 60); a metal gate raises the threshold voltage by about 0.6 V (p. 60); and a polysilicon gate on HfO₂ must be deposited below 600 °C (p. 67). This section takes each in turn, and ends with what the industry eventually built.

Doped polysilicon had been the gate electrode since the 1970s for the reasons of M03 §8: it survives the source–drain anneal, it self-aligns the source and drain, and its work function is set by its doping, n⁺ for the n-channel transistor and p⁺ for the p-channel one. On a high-k film it has two problems. The first is chemical. Silicon reacts with an oxide that is not stable against it (§5.3), forming a silicide or SiO₂ at the top of the dielectric, which is why the notes' Ta₂O₅ needs a conductive barrier, TiN or WN, between it and any polysilicon. HfO₂ is stable against silicon, so the top interfacial layer that the notes avoid by depositing the polysilicon below 600 °C is not that reaction; Wolf gives the temperature condition without a mechanism (p. 175). The second problem is electrical: a polysilicon gate depletes at its bottom surface under bias (M06 §6), adding a few ångströms of equivalent thickness in series with the dielectric. At an EOT of 12 Å those few ångströms are a large fraction of the total. In a separate report, HfO₂ with a TaN metal gate, which does not deplete, reached an EOT of 8 Å (Wolf p. 176, after B. H. Lee et al., IEDM 2000).

A metal gate solves both problems and creates a third, which the notes state: it raises VT by about 0.6 V. The threshold voltage contains the work-function difference φMS between the gate and the silicon (M03 §1). With every other term unchanged, replacing one gate material by another shifts the threshold by the difference of their work functions:

ΔVT = φM,new − φM,old
(7.9)
ΔVT change in threshold voltage (V) · φM,new, φM,old work functions of the new and old gate materials, expressed in volts (a work function of 4.6 eV is 4.6 V in the equation). Follows from φMS = φM − φS, with the silicon, the dielectric and its charges unchanged. For n⁺ polysilicon φM ≈ 4.05 eV, the electron affinity of silicon, because its Fermi level is at the conduction-band edge; for p⁺ polysilicon ≈ 4.05 + 1.12 = 5.17 eV. A metal's effective work function on a high-k film can differ from its vacuum value.

TiN, the metal of the notes' Ta₂O₅ transistor, has a work function that places its Fermi level near the middle of the silicon gap — a midgap metal (Wilk et al. 2001). Silicon's midgap lies 4.05 + 1.12/2 = 4.61 eV below the vacuum level; Robertson takes 4.6 eV. Replacing an n⁺ polysilicon gate on an n-channel transistor by a midgap metal therefore shifts the threshold by 4.6 − 4.05 ≈ +0.55 V, the notes' "about 0.6 V". The p-channel transistor, with a p⁺ polysilicon gate at 5.17 eV, shifts by 4.6 − 5.17 ≈ −0.57 V, which is also an increase in the magnitude of its (negative) threshold. A single midgap metal is the simplest metal gate — one material, one mask, symmetrical thresholds — but both transistors come out with thresholds near 0.5 V in magnitude, which Wilk calls much too large for supply voltages at or below 1 V.

Changed numbers: a midgap gate on a low-voltage transistor

Given an n-channel transistor with an n⁺ polysilicon gate and VT = 0.25 V, run from a 1.0 V supply. The gate is replaced by a midgap metal, 4.6 eV, with the channel doping and dielectric unchanged. Find the new threshold and the gate overdrive VDD − VT before and after.

Equation (7.9). ΔVT = 4.6 − 4.05 = 0.55 V, so VT = 0.25 + 0.55 = 0.80 V.

Overdrive. Before, 1.0 − 0.25 = 0.75 V; after, 1.0 − 0.80 = 0.20 V.

VT = 0.80 V, and the overdrive — which sets the drive current — falls from 0.75 V to 0.20 V. The threshold must be brought back down.

Bringing it down is the notes' "proper VT adjustment", and they name its price. Lowering the threshold of an n-channel device with a gate that cannot be changed means implanting donors into the p-type channel region, counter-doping its surface. A donor dose large enough to remove half a volt of threshold can make the surface layer n-type, and the channel then forms at the bottom of that layer, below the surface, rather than at the interface: a buried channel, as in the p-channel transistor with an n⁺ gate of M02 §8. A buried channel sits farther from the gate, so the gate's control weakens relative to the drain's, and the short-channel behaviour — threshold roll-off, swing, drain-induced barrier lowering — degrades, which is exactly what the notes say. The alternative that avoids counter-doping is to use two metals, one for each transistor, with work functions near the two silicon band edges: Robertson gives the targets as about 4.0 eV for the n-channel gate and 5.1 eV for the p-channel gate, noting that the first must be a reactive metal and the second a noble one that is hard to etch.

What the industry did next — beyond the notes

The notes, like Wolf's 2002 text they follow, describe the field as it stood in 2001, when no high-k material was yet ready for production and Wolf reported a forecast of about five years before one could be introduced. The dated forecasts in the chapter — Ta₂O₅ first among the gate candidates, ZrO₂ as one of the most promising, HfO₂ at k up to 40 — should be read as that snapshot. What followed: in January 2007 Intel announced transistors with a hafnium-based high-k gate dielectric and different metal gate electrodes for the n-channel and p-channel devices, and its 45 nm logic process, described by Mistry et al. at IEDM 2007, put high-k plus metal gate transistors into high-volume manufacturing for the first time, with a 1.0 nm EOT high-k dielectric and dual band-edge work-function metal gates. The solution combined pieces of this module: a hafnium-based dielectric (§8.4–8.5), with the thin interfacial oxide that Taur and Ning describe as still needed beneath HfO₂ (§6), and two metals with band-edge work functions in place of polysilicon, avoiding both poly depletion and the buried channel. Robertson's 2004 review, written as the choice was being made, already named HfO₂ and hafnium silicate as the preferred oxides.

Summary

  • High-k in the notes means k > 7, above Si₃N₄ (others use > 3.9 or > 10). Three uses: gate dielectric, DRAM capacitor, FeRAM capacitor. C = kε0/t: with thinning stopped at about 1.5 nm by direct tunnelling, only k can rise. Notes' dating: ~3.0 nm at 0.13 µm in 2001, < 1.5 nm needed for 0.1 µm (leading-edge 130 nm logic already used 1.5 nm).
  • Notes' table (p. 49), k / Eg: Ta₂O₅ 26 / 4.5 orthorhombic; ZrO₂ 25 / 7.8 monoclinic; HfO₂ 24 / 5.7 monoclinic; Al₂O₃ 9 / 8.7, Si₃N₄ 7 / 5.1, SiO₂ 3.9 / 8.9 amorphous; BST 300, PLZT 1000. Beyond the notes: k = n² + lattice term, klat ∝ NZ*²/μωTO²; the ionic displacement supplies most of a large k, and needs a large Born effective charge (anomalously large in HfO₂, 5.85 for Hf against a nominal +4) and a soft polar vibration — the ionic masses cancel. Hf–O is 68% ionic, Si–O 45%. The d-state conduction band makes the gap small: k and Eg run inversely.
  • EOT = thigh-k(3.9/k): 10 nm at k = 20 behaves like ~2 nm (1.95 nm) of SiO₂; a 1.0 nm EOT needs 6.2 nm of HfO₂ (k 24) or 2.3 nm of Al₂O₃. Tunnelling falls exponentially with the physical thickness, but also with the square root of barrier height and effective mass, both lower in high-k films.
  • In series, 1/C = 1/C1 + 1/C2, so EOT = tSiO₂ + thigh-k(3.9/k): the lowest-k layer dominates and sets the minimum EOT. 38 Å HfO₂ at 12 Å EOT implies ≈ 6 Å of interlayer, seen in micrographs.
  • Notes' integration points: below 0.3 µm VT rolls off with gate length; a high-k gate gives subthreshold swing St = 80 mV/decade with Ta₂O₅ (at a 0.05 µm channel, against ≈ 123 for SiO₂, in Wolf's figure), smaller is better (ideal 60 mV/decade; long-channel St = 2.3(kT/q)(1 + Cd/Cox)). Wolf: optimum k = 25 for a 5 nm film, from a circuit simulation at fixed physical thickness. Drawback: high Dit on bare Si, cured by 1–2 atomic layers of SiO₂.
  • Beyond the notes: band offsets must exceed ~1 eV (Ta₂O₅ 0.35 eV fails; HfO₂ 1.4, ZrO₂ 1.5, Al₂O₃ 2.8, SiO₂ 3.1); the oxide must not react, MO₂ + Si → M or MSi + SiO₂ (Ta₂O₅ grows up to 2 nm SiO₂ beneath it; ZrO₂, "stable" in the notes, is marginal); and it should stay amorphous — seven-coordinated ionic HfO₂ and ZrO₂ crystallise, and silicates, aluminates and nitrogen raise the crystallisation temperature at a cost in k.
  • The interfacial SiO₂ is kept on purpose: a thermally grown interface is clean, a deposited one is not. Ideal: a few atomic layers of Si–O(–N), a pseudo-binary oxide (the SiON/Ta₂O₅/TiN stack). ZrO₂ by ALD forms a 9 Å interfacial silicate.
  • ALD: one reactant at a time, chemisorbed to saturation, purged between pulses; < 400 °C, 100% step coverage, thickness by cycle count, 50–200 Å/min with < 1 s switching. Al₂O₃ from TMA + O₃ at 450 °C (ALD by Wolf's account); ZrO₂ from ZrCl₄ + H₂O. Growth per cycle is below a monolayer (steric hindrance; TMA/H₂O 1.1–1.2 Å), not set by the lattice constant.
  • Ta₂O₅ (k 25): 2 nm oxide nitrided in N₂ at 800 °C for 30 [min] + TiN gate; or NO at 800 °C, 20 s, then CVD Ta₂O₅ → EOT 2.3 nm and 1000× less leakage than with an SiO₂ interlayer; TiN or WN barrier against poly. Al₂O₃: k 9, Eg ≈ 8.3 eV (8.7 in the table), stable on Si. ZrO₂: k 20–25, Eg ≈ 5.1 eV (7.8 in the table), mismatch 2.1% in the notes (6.6% from lattice constants), sputtered or RTA-oxidised, HF-etchable, Al-doped, PDA 400–600 °C, Zr-Al-Si-O, YSZ.
  • HfO₂: k 24–40 (literature 20–25), Eg ≈ 5.7 eV, PVD or RTCVD; no layer against poly deposited below 600 °C with 1000 °C RTA — the gate side; a ≈ 6 Å layer still forms at the substrate; crystallises; 10⁴ less leakage than SiO₂ at 12 Å EOT. HfSiON: less leakage than SiO₂ at equal EOT; silicates trade k for stability. Pr₂O₃: 5 nA/cm² at 1 V, EOT 14 Å, k ≈ 31, no C–V hysteresis, MBE 625–725 °C at 0.1 Å/s, 600 °C N₂ or 1000 °C/15 s RTA. CeO₂ (lecture only): 0.37% lattice mismatch, epitaxial.
  • Gate: poly reacts with and depletes on high-k; a metal gate removes depletion (TaN on HfO₂: 8 Å EOT) but a midgap metal such as TiN (≈ 4.6 eV against 4.05 eV for n⁺ poly) raises VT by ≈ 0.6 V; counter-doping to recover it gives a buried channel. Production (2007, 45 nm): hafnium-based high-k with dual band-edge metal gates.
