Module 10

Source–Drain Engineering

The resistance between the metal contact and the end of the channel, term by term, measured against the resistance of the channel itself; the silicide contact and the silicon it consumes; the elevated source/drain; and the implant and anneal that make a junction a few tens of nanometres deep, with the dopant they lose and the position of the extension edge under the gate.

Prerequisites M02, M09 Read time ~55 min Sources Class notes pp. 77–88 (Chapter 5); Lectures 17–18 and the recorded class of 23 Oct; Wolf Vol. 4 §5.3; Taur & Ning 3/E §2.3, §8.3; Plummer, Deal & Griffin Table 11-5

The source and drain are the two heavily doped regions at the ends of the channel through which current enters and leaves the transistor (M01). M02 §6 traced how the drain was rebuilt twice: a lightly doped drain was added near 2 µm (the notes; Wolf: below 1.5 µm) to lower the peak field and with it hot-carrier injection, and at 0.18 µm that region was redoped far more heavily, to within a few times the contact's doping, and made shallow, as the source/drain extension. M09 shaped the doping of the channel between them. This module takes the rest of Chapter 5 of the notes (pp. 77–88), which asks what the source and drain cost the transistor and how that cost is held down as the gate shrinks. The cost is resistance. Every ampere of drain current passes through a metal-to-silicon contact, through the heavily doped region, around the corner into a layer a few nanometres thick at the surface, and only then into the channel; each stage adds a resistance in series, and the channel resistance it is compared with falls at every generation. The module builds the sheet-resistance arithmetic first, because every later term is expressed with it; then the four series terms and the yardstick they are measured against; then the silicide contact, the elevated source/drain, and the process that makes an ultra-shallow junction — the low-energy implant, the spike anneal and the dopant lost on the way. It closes on how far the extension should reach under the gate. Transient enhanced diffusion and channelling, developed on M09 §3–4, are applied here to the source and drain rather than explained again.

Assumes: the drain structures and the salicide process (M02 §3.3, §6)

The lightly doped drain (LDD) placed a region of about 10¹⁷ cm⁻³ between the channel and the 10²⁰ cm⁻³ drain, so that the drain voltage dropped over a wider depletion layer and the peak field fell. It is made by implanting after the gate is patterned, depositing a conformal oxide, etching it anisotropically with no mask so that a sidewall spacer remains against the gate, and implanting the heavy dose with the spacer as the mask (M02 §6.1). At 0.18 µm the supply fell to about 2 V, hot-carrier control by an LDD was no longer needed, and the near region was redoped at 5–10×10¹⁴ cm⁻², which the notes' Chapter 1 describes as as heavily as the contact, but shallower: the source/drain extension (SDE, M02 §6.2). A salicide (self-aligned silicide) is formed by sputtering a metal over the wafer and annealing it, so that it reacts only where it touches silicon, then stripping the unreacted metal (M02 §3.3).

1 · The two-region source/drain

A deep-submicron source or drain is two junctions in series along the surface, each with a different job. This section defines them as the notes do, states what each one must achieve, and shows the geometric link between them that drives most of the rest of the module: the depth of the second region sets the length of the first.

1.1 Extension and contact

The notes (p. 77, following Wolf §5.3) describe the source/drain contact structure as two regions: a shallow source/drain extension with junction depth Xj, which reaches from the channel out under the sidewall spacer, and a deeper contact region with junction depth Xjc, beyond the spacer, which carries the silicide and the metal contact. The extension is the part the channel sees. Its depth decides how far the drain's field and depletion region reach toward the source, so in a deep-submicron device the notes give it the role of reducing short-channel effects, the fall in threshold voltage and the loss of gate control that follow when the drain influences the channel. The contact region exists for two reasons the notes name: it must be thick enough to hold the salicide layer, which consumes silicon as it forms (§4.3), and deep enough that the junction under the silicide does not leak.

The two depths are not independent. The contact region is implanted after the spacer is formed, with the spacer as the mask, and its ions scatter sideways as they slow down; this lateral straggle grows with the implant's projected range (Wolf p. 204; lateral straggle values on M09 §6.2). A deeper contact junction therefore needs a higher-energy implant, whose dopant reaches further sideways, and so needs a wider spacer to keep that dopant away from the gate edge. A wider spacer makes the extension longer, and a longer extension has more resistance. The notes state the chain directly: the greater the contact junction depth, the wider the spacer must be, and the longer SDE region "increases the parasitic SDE resistance RSDE" (p. 77). Hence their rule that Xjc "should not be larger than that needed" to hold the salicide and keep leakage low, and their design goal: minimise both the parasitic series resistance and the silicide–silicon contact resistance.

Wolf quotes the depths that the 1999 and 2001 editions of the International Technology Roadmap for Semiconductors (ITRS) set against the lithographically drawn gate length LL: the contact junction at 0.8 LL in 1999, relaxed to 1.1 LL in 2001, and the extension at 0.4 LL, relaxed to 0.55 LL. At a 50 nm gate the extension is therefore 20–28 nm deep and the contact junction 40–55 nm. The notes use the 0.55 LL extension rule in §3.5.

1.2 What the extension is no longer for

Lecture 17 listed three advantages of the extension: lower parasitic capacitance, a lower field in the extension, and suppressed hot-electron injection. The second and third describe the lightly doped drain from which the extension descends. Wolf gives the history the notes compress into "First region had a lighter doping than the second region": the LDD was added as gate lengths went below 1.5 µm (the notes, M02 §6.1, put the hot-carrier problem at 2 µm) to reduce the peak field and hot-electron injection; as gate lengths entered the deep submicron the supply voltage fell, the peak field near the drain fell with it, and the LDD "was no longer needed to suppress hot-carrier-injection effects". The extension that replaced it is doped heavily, so it spreads the field far less than the LDD did. The hot-carrier mechanism, and the fact that its damage is concentrated near the drain, so that the threshold shift is smaller than a uniform charge sheet would give and larger with source and drain exchanged, are on M02 §6.1 and M05b. The resistance the light doping caused is the subject of this module.

Extension doping: "10¹⁷" in Lecture 17, 5.5×10¹⁹ cm⁻³ in the notes

Lecture 17 gave the extension doping as "may be 10¹⁷" with the contact at 10¹⁹–10²⁰ cm⁻³. That is the level M02 §6.1 gives for the lightly doped drain (M02 §6.1), not the extension's. The notes require the extension to reach 5.5×10¹⁹ cm⁻³ at the 50 nm node to keep its sheet resistance (p. 82, §3.5 below), Lecture 18 used the same 5.5×10¹⁹ cm⁻³ and said the structure was no longer an LDD, and Wolf states that the peak extension doping must exceed 10¹⁹ cm⁻³. This page uses the notes' value.

A designer proposes to make the contact junction twice as deep "for margin" against silicide spiking. Using the chain of §1.1, state what happens to the extension resistance, and why.

It rises. A deeper contact junction needs a higher-energy contact implant; its lateral straggle grows with the projected range, so the dopant reaches further sideways under the spacer toward the gate. To keep it from reaching the gate edge the spacer must be widened. The extension runs under the whole spacer, so its length ℓSDE grows with the spacer width, and RSDE is proportional to ℓSDE (eq. (10.5)). That is why the notes say Xjc should be no deeper than the silicide and the leakage require. The elevated source/drain of §5 removes the conflict by putting the extra thickness above the surface.

2 · Sheet resistance and counting squares

Every resistance in the source/drain is the resistance of a thin conducting layer: a diffused or implanted region a few tens of nanometres deep, a silicide film, an accumulation layer. For such layers the thickness is fixed by the process and only the outline is drawn, so resistance is expressed per unit of outline. Lecture 17 ended on this arithmetic and worked it at the board; the notes use it without defining it (p. 82, "650 Ω/sq").

2.1 Ohms per square

A bar of resistivity ρ, length L in the direction of current, width W and thickness t has resistance R = ρL/(Wt), because the current crosses a face of area Wt. When the layer is thin compared with its length and width — the thin-film case Lecture 17 described — ρ and t are properties of the process, and the drawn outline supplies only L/W. Grouping the first two defines the sheet resistance.

ρs = ρt  R = ρs LW
(10.1)
ρs sheet resistance (Ω/□, read "ohms per square") · ρ resistivity of the layer (Ω·cm) · t layer thickness, for a junction its depth Xj (cm) · R resistance (Ω) · L length along the current and W width across it (any one unit, since only the ratio enters). Uniform layer, current parallel to the surface, t ≪ L, W. Taur & Ning write ρsh; the notes write "Ω/sq".

The ratio L/W is the number of squares of side W that fit along the strip. Every square has the same resistance, ρs, whatever its size: enlarging a square lengthens the current path and widens the face it crosses by the same factor, and the two cancel. The unit is dimensionally the ohm — Ω·cm divided by cm — and "per square" records only that the number is to be multiplied by a count of squares (Taur & Ning §2.3.1; Lecture 17's "square gets cancelled"). For a layer whose doping varies with depth, as every implanted junction does, ρ/t is replaced by the reciprocal of the conductance per square summed over the depth, but the counting of squares is unchanged. ρs is measured directly with a four-point probe: a current is passed between the outer two of four collinear probes and the voltage read between the inner two; on a large thin film V/I = ρs ln 2/π ≈ 0.22 ρs (Taur & Ning eq. (2.41) and text).

3 · The series resistance and its yardstick

This section follows the current from the metal contact to the end of the channel, names the four resistances it meets (notes pp. 78–81), and sets them against the resistance of the channel itself, which is the only measure of whether a given source/drain resistance is acceptable. The arithmetic is the notes' own, and the two board formulas of Lectures 17 and 18, Rsp and RSDE, are worked with the notes' numbers.

3.1 Where the current goes

Current from the metal interconnect enters the silicon perpendicular to the wafer surface at the contact, then turns and travels parallel to the surface to the channel (Wolf p. 197). Along that path it meets four resistances in series, which the notes list for a single-region drain (p. 78) and then for a drain with an extension (p. 79):

  • Rco, the contact resistance between the metal (or silicide) and the source/drain silicon (§4.1);
  • Rsh, the resistance of the body of the source/drain region, computed from its sheet resistance and its length in squares; with an extension this is RSDE, the resistance of the extension (§3.5);
  • Rsp, the spreading resistance of the current lines crowding into the thin surface layer at the channel end (§3.4);
  • Rac, the resistance of the accumulation layer where the source/drain doping reaches under the gate (§3.3).
Rs = Rco + RSDE + Rsp + Rac
(10.2)
Rs total series resistance of one source or one drain (Ω) · terms as listed above (Ω). Without an extension, RSDE is replaced by Rsh (notes p. 78; Wolf eqs. 5.1–5.2). The source and the drain each contribute one Rs. Each term varies as 1/W, so the products R·W (Ω·cm or Ω·µm) are the quantities compared.

3.2 The yardstick: the channel's own resistance

A source/drain resistance is large or small only relative to the resistance of the channel when the transistor is on. The notes define that resistance as Rsat = VDD/IDsat, the supply voltage divided by the saturated drain current (p. 79), and set the design goal: the series resistance of the source and drain may reduce IDsat by no more than 10 %. Wolf adds the relation that turns this into a budget: an external source-plus-drain resistance of about 10 % of Rsat reduces IDsat by about 8 %. The two halves do not cost the same. Taur & Ning write the intrinsic transistor's voltages as V′gs = Vgs − RsIds and V′ds = Vds − (Rs + Rd)Ids (their eqs. (8.13)–(8.14)): the drop across the source resistance is subtracted from the gate drive as well as from the drain voltage, and they call resistance on the source side "particularly troublesome", "as it degrades the gate drive as well".

RsatW = VDD tox0.47 vsat εox (VDD − VT) ≈ tox0.376 vsat εox
(10.3)
RsatW channel on-resistance times width (Ω·cm) · VDD supply voltage (V) · tox gate oxide thickness (cm) · vsat saturation velocity, ≈ 10⁷ cm/s · εox = 3.9ε0 = 3.45×10⁻¹³ F/cm · VT threshold voltage (V). Notes p. 79, Wolf eq. 5.4. The approximate form is the exact one with VT = 0.2 VDD, since 0.47 × 0.8 = 0.376; neither source states that assumption. Velocity-saturated drain current.

The first form is VDD divided by a saturated drain current per unit width of 0.47 vsatCox(VDD − VT), the saturated drain current Wolf's model assumes; it is about half of the full velocity-saturation limit, vsatCox(Vgs − VT) per unit width (Taur & Ning eq. (6.51)); the gate capacitance per unit area Cox = εox/tox is where tox enters. The consequence is the one the notes draw: since tox falls at every generation, RsatW falls with it, and the source/drain resistance RsW must fall at every node just to hold its share.

3.3 The accumulation layer, Rac

The notes (p. 80) define the end of the channel as the point at the surface where the source/drain doping equals the body doping, ND ≈ NA: the metallurgical junction. Beyond that point, toward the source or drain, the silicon under the gate edge is n-type. The positive gate voltage that inverts the p-type channel acts on this n-type silicon too, because the gate is a single equipotential; on n-type silicon it attracts more of the majority carriers to the surface, and the result is an accumulation layer rather than an inversion layer. The accumulation layer ends, toward the source or drain, where the carrier concentration it induces equals the local doping: Wolf gives about 10¹⁹ cm⁻³ induced against a source/drain peak doping of 10²⁰–10²¹ cm⁻³, so the layer matters only in the graded edge of the junction nearest the channel. Its resistance Rac is in series with the channel. In a deep-submicron transistor the overlap, and with it the accumulation layer, is short, and the notes conclude that Rac "does not play a significant role" as the device is scaled. The accumulation and inversion layers have about the same thickness, Xc (Wolf), and that thickness is the starting width of the spreading problem.

3.4 Spreading from the surface layer, Rsp

Leaving the accumulation layer, the current is confined to a sheet of thickness Xc at the surface; a little further on it occupies the full depth Xj of the extension. Between the two the current lines fan out, and the crowding near the narrow end adds resistance beyond what the same length of uniformly filled extension would have. That excess is the spreading resistance (notes p. 81; Wolf eq. 5.5). Taur & Ning attribute the analytical expression to Baccarani and Sai-Halasz (1983).

Rsp ≈ 2ρπW ln (β XjXc)
(10.4)
Rsp spreading resistance (Ω) · ρ resistivity of the extension (Ω·cm) · W device width (cm) · Xj extension junction depth · Xc inversion/accumulation layer thickness (same unit as Xj) · β constant, 0.37–0.9 depending on the derivation (notes; Wolf uses 0.58, Taur & Ning's eq. (8.16) has 0.75). Uniformly doped extension with an abrupt edge; it ignores the lateral doping gradient (§7.3).

Two features of eq. (10.4) carry its physics. The resistance depends on the geometry only through the ratio Xj/Xc, the factor by which the current must spread; and it depends on that ratio logarithmically, as the resistance of any radially spreading current does, so halving the junction depth removes a fixed amount, not a fixed fraction, of Rsp. The same logarithm makes the uncertain constant β matter little.

Which ratio is inside the logarithm

The argument is βXj/Xc, junction depth over layer thickness, in the notes (p. 81), in Wolf and in Taur & Ning, and Lecture 18 dictated it the same way. Since Xj ≫ Xc, the logarithm is positive; with the ratio inverted it would be negative, and so would the resistance.

3.5 The extension itself, RSDE, and the sum

Beyond the spreading region the current fills the extension uniformly, and the extension is a resistor of length ℓSDE, width W and depth Xj: R = ρl/A (notes p. 81; Wolf eq. 5.6).

RSDE ≈ ρ ℓSDEW Xj = ρs ℓSDEW
(10.5)
RSDE extension resistance (Ω) · ℓSDE extension length, ≈ LL/2 (notes p. 81) · Xj extension depth, ≈ 0.55 LL · ρs = ρ/Xj the extension's sheet resistance (Ω/□), eq. (10.1). The second form is ℓSDE/W squares of the extension's sheet resistance.

Adding eqs. (10.4) and (10.5) and multiplying by W gives the notes' expression for the parasitic resistance of the extension region, with RSDE factored out (p. 81; Wolf eq. 5.7):

(Rsp + RSDE) W ≈ ρ ℓSDEXj [1 + 2Xjπ ℓSDE ln (β XjXc)]
(10.6)
Symbols as in eqs. (10.4)–(10.5); the left side in Ω·cm. ρℓSDE/Xj = ρsℓSDE is RSDEW, and the bracket is the factor by which spreading multiplies it. Wolf evaluates the bracket at 2.09 for the 250 nm generation and about 1.48 for 50 nm.

The bracket says how much of the extension's parasitic resistance is spreading. At 2.09, spreading is (2.09 − 1)/2.09 = 52 % of the sum; at 1.48 it is 32 %. That is the basis of the notes' statement that Rsp "plays a significant role". The bracket falls with scaling because Xj shrinks toward Xc, which cannot shrink with it: the inversion layer stays a few nanometres thick (notes p. 76 put it under 7 nm), so there is less depth for the current to spread into. ℓSDE/Xj ≈ 1.2 does not follow from the two rules beside it

The notes (p. 81) take ℓSDE ≈ LL/2 and Xj ≈ 0.55 LL and write ℓSDE/Xj ≈ 1.2. The two rules give 0.5/0.55 = 0.91. The 1.2 is copied from Wolf, whose sentence is the same; it matches the earlier ITRS rule Xj = 0.4 LL (0.5/0.4 = 1.25), which the 2001 roadmap relaxed to 0.55 LL (§1.1). Use the notes' 1.2 where a problem gives it; the ratio from the stated rules is 0.91.

4 · The contact region and its silicide

The deep contact region contributes the fourth resistance, Rco, at the interface between the silicide and the silicon (notes p. 83). This section quantifies it, gives the silicides' own resistivities against aluminium's, and works out how much silicon the silicide consumes, which is what fixes the depth of the contact junction.

4.1 Contact resistance, Rco

The quality of a metal–silicon contact is stated as its specific contact resistivity ρc (Ω·cm²): the resistance of a unit area of interface. A contact of area A with current spread evenly over it has resistance ρc/A. For heavily doped silicon the current crosses the metal–semiconductor barrier by tunnelling, and Taur & Ning give the dependence (their eq. (8.21)): ρc ∝ exp[(4πφB/h)(m*εSi/Nd)1/2], with φB the barrier height and Nd the surface doping. The doping enters through an exponent, so a modest fall in surface doping raises ρc by a large factor. The notes' model for the contact term is a bound:

RcoW ≥ ρc2 LL

4.2 The silicide

The contact region is covered with a silicide, TiSi₂ or CoSi₂ in the notes (p. 83), formed by sputtering titanium or cobalt and annealing so that the metal reacts with the silicon; the self-aligned process and its two anneals are on M02 §3.3. The silicide does two things for the series resistance. Its sheet resistance, about 2–10 Ω/□ (Taur & Ning), is one to two orders of magnitude below that of the source/drain silicon, so it shunts the whole contact region and leaves only the unsilicided strip under the spacer as Rsh; and the contact area becomes the whole silicided surface instead of a contact window. Taur & Ning add that a silicide with a work function near the middle of the gap, such as TiSi₂, CoSi₂ or NiSi, is needed in CMOS so that the barriers to n⁺ and to p⁺ silicon are about equal.

4.3 The silicon a silicide consumes

A silicide forms by reaction with the silicon beneath the metal, so its lower interface lies below the original silicon surface. The notes (p. 83) state the consequence: "During silicide formation nearly 75% of Si is consumed. For this deep contact region is required." Wolf, whom the notes follow, says that for TiSi₂ "the silicon consumed is about 75% of that of the thickness of the final silicide film", and for CoSi₂ about equal to it. Plummer's table gives the ratio for TiSi₂ as 2.27/2.51 = 0.90, and for CoSi₂ 3.64/3.52 = 1.03 (Table 1). Wolf gives two consequences:

  • The contact junction must be deep enough that the silicide does not consume it, or reach so close to it that the junction leaks. That forces the wide spacer and long extension of §1.1.
  • The silicon consumed is the top layer, where an implanted profile is most heavily doped. The silicide–silicon interface therefore sits in silicon of lower doping, which raises ρc through the exponential of §4.1; and boron in particular segregates into the silicide, depleting the interface further.

5 · The elevated source/drain

Sections 3 and 4 reached a deadlock. The extension resistance falls if the spacer is narrowed, but the contact implant then straggles under the gate; the contact junction must be deep to hold the silicide, and a deep implant straggles further. The elevated source/drain removes the deadlock by moving the extra thickness the silicide needs above the original surface (notes pp. 83–84; Wolf §5.3.2).

In an elevated (or raised) source/drain, the heavily doped contact layer is grown on top of the source and drain by selective epitaxy: single-crystal silicon, or perhaps silicon–germanium, deposited so that it continues the crystal lattice of the silicon beneath, and grown only on the exposed silicon of the source and drain, not on the oxide of the spacers and the isolation. The silicide is then formed in the grown layer. The notes list three advantages:

  1. The spacer can be made much narrower. The contact region no longer needs a deep implant, so the lateral-straggle argument of §1.1 no longer sets the spacer width; a narrower spacer shortens ℓSDE, and RSDE falls in proportion (eq. (10.5)).
  2. The contact layer can be thick enough to contain the silicide without junction leakage. The silicon the silicide consumes (§4.3) now comes from the grown layer, above the junction.
  3. The grown layer can be heavily doped during growth, which lowers ρc through the exponential dependence of §4.1.

Two constraints come with it. Because n-channel and p-channel transistors need n⁺ and p⁺ layers, two separate selective epitaxy processes are needed (notes p. 84); Lecture 18 tied the word "selective" to growing the two types separately, while the elevated structure Wolf describes needs the growth confined to the exposed silicon of the source and drain. And the growth comes after the extension implant, so its thermal budget acts on the extension: the notes require both growths below 850 °C "to prevent the SDE implant from diffusing too far under the gate edge" (Wolf: "probably below 850°C"). The extension must already sit where §7 wants it, and the epitaxy must not move it.

Explain, as a chain of three causes, why the elevated source/drain lowers RSDE even though no part of it touches the extension.

(1) The silicide is formed in the grown layer above the original surface, so the contact region no longer needs a deep junction to hold it. (2) Without a deep contact implant there is no high-energy implant whose lateral straggle must be kept away from the gate edge, so the spacer that masks it can be made narrow. (3) The extension runs under the spacer, so its length ℓSDE shrinks with the spacer, and RSDE = ρsℓSDE/W falls in proportion. The extension's depth and doping are unchanged; only its length is.

6 · Making an ultra-shallow junction

The extension must be a few tens of nanometres deep, doped above 10¹⁹ cm⁻³, with its edge in a precise place. This section follows the notes (pp. 84–88) and the recorded class of 23 October through the three steps that decide whether that is achieved: the implant energy, the anneal, and the dopant lost between implanting and finishing. The physics of channelling and of transient enhanced diffusion is on M09 §3–4; here it is applied.

6.1 Lower energy, shallower junction

The main lever on junction depth is the implant energy. Wolf expects single-digit-keV implants for the contact regions and sub-keV implants for the extensions at the 0.13 and 0.1 µm nodes; Lecture 18 and the 23 October class spoke of 0.2–0.3 keV for the extension. The notes give three reasons a lower energy gives a shallower junction (pp. 84–85):

  1. The projected range Rp becomes smaller, so the as-implanted peak is nearer the surface.
  2. The straggle and the channelling tail scale with Rp (Wolf adds: "especially important for B implants"), so the whole profile contracts with it.
  3. Transient enhanced diffusion is reduced. The excess interstitials sit near Rp, and a shallow population reaches the surface, where it is annihilated, sooner; Wolf's eq. 5.9 makes the extra depth grow as the square root of Rp (M09 §4.3). The notes word this as TED "occuring during implantation"; it occurs during the anneal that follows, as M09 §4.3 records.

At sub-keV energies the balance between the last two shifts. Wolf states that for sub-keV boron, channelling becomes unimportant next to the broadening TED causes during the anneal, so pre-amorphising the surface to suppress channelling, which Wolf calls an "elaborate" measure, is not expected to be used for gate lengths of 0.1 µm and below.

6.2 The cost: beam current

A low-energy implant is slow. The notes (p. 85) give the comparison: above 10 keV, an implanter delivering 10–25 mA can implant 230 wafers per hour at a dose of 4×10¹⁵ cm⁻²; at sub-keV energies the beam current is 1–2 mA. Wolf dates the 1–2 mA as the maximum available on commercial implanters in the mid-1990s, and gives the cause, which the notes omit: Child's law, the space-charge limit on the ion current that can be extracted from an ion source.

Imax ∝ V3/2
(10.8)

Implanters work around the limit by extracting at a higher voltage and decelerating the beam to the final energy ("decel"). The cost is energy contamination: an ion neutralised in the beamline before or inside the decelerating field is not counted in the measured dose and is not decelerated, or not fully, so it arrives at a higher energy than intended, which puts dopant deeper and defeats the purpose of the low-energy implant (Wolf).

6.3 The anneal: hot and short

An implanted layer must be annealed to place the dopant on lattice sites, where it is electrically active, and to repair the implant damage. Every second at temperature also diffuses the dopant and deepens the junction. The notes (p. 86) state why the source/drain anneal is done by rapid thermal annealing (RTA) rather than in a furnace: in the ideal case the as-implanted dose would spend "zero" time at the temperature that activates the dopant, and a short exposure to a high temperature favours activation over diffusion because the activation energy for dopant activation is higher than that for diffusion. Wolf gives the values for boron, 5.4 eV for activation and 3.4 eV for diffusion.

Check: the factor exceeds 1 because 5.4 eV > 3.4 eV; were the energies equal, temperature would buy nothing.

A second reason for RTA comes from transient enhanced diffusion. TED has a negative effective activation energy (Wolf eq. 5.9, M09 §4.3): the interstitial supersaturation that drives it is larger at lower temperature, so defects annealed out slowly at a low temperature leave a deeper junction than the same defects annealed out quickly at a high one. A furnace ramps at a few degrees per minute and spends minutes in exactly that low-temperature range; an RTA chamber passes through it in seconds. The notes compress this to "Less time is spent during annealing"; Wolf's point is less time during the ramp-up, at the low temperatures where the enhancement is largest.

The limit of the idea is the spike anneal: ramp up fast, ramp down fast, and spend zero time at the peak. The notes give 100–200 °C/s up and 50–80 °C/s down; the 23 October class gave the same. Wolf separates them: ramp-up rates of about 200 °C/s are achievable (his experiments go to 200–400 °C/s), but going beyond a moderately fast 100 °C/s gains little, because TED is already minimised at 100 °C/s and the part of the thermal budget that cannot be cut, the finite time near the peak and the slower ramp-down, dominates. He also gives the better figure of merit: about 90 % of dopant activation occurs within roughly 50 °C of the peak, so the time spent in that band specifies a spike anneal better than the ramp rate does.

The notes list five kinds of RTA tool (p. 86, Wolf p. 212): linear lamps heating both sides of the wafer, axisymmetric lamps heating one side, an arc lamp, a hot plate, and a hot-wall RTP chamber; the 23 October class described four. Three further routes raise the fraction of the dopant that is electrically active in a shallow junction (notes p. 87): laser annealing; strain-compensation implants, in which germanium, larger than silicon, offsets the lattice strain caused by the smaller boron atoms, which raises boron's solubility (Wolf); and RTP tools whose lamps respond quickly, which raises the ramp-down rate and so shortens the slowest leg of the spike.

Wolf reports where these methods stood in 2001. A 0.5 keV boron implant with a spike anneal gave a junction about 35 nm deep with a sheet resistance of about 350 Ω/□ (the junction the second example of §2 compares with), and that appeared to be the practical limit: reducing either quantity required increasing the other, and implants below 0.5 keV were less productive with no better result. The 50 nm node needs extension junctions of about 20–25 nm, and Wolf judged it unclear whether ion implantation and conventional RTA could meet that.

6.4 Dopant that never reaches the junction

At sub-keV energies part of the implanted dose is lost. The notes (pp. 87–88) name three mechanisms, and Wolf quantifies them for boron:

  • Self-sputtering. Every ion that strikes a surface knocks some surface atoms out of it, and the number sputtered per ion rises as the ion energy falls. At 0.5 keV about one silicon atom is sputtered for every four boron atoms implanted. Because a low-energy ion also comes to rest near the surface, the surface layer becomes progressively richer in boron as the dose builds, and more of what is sputtered is the boron already implanted; the loss therefore depends on how much has been implanted. For a nominal 1×10¹⁵ cm⁻², the retained dose is about 10 % low at 0.5 keV and 20 % low at 0.2 keV; at 2 keV essentially all of it is retained.
  • Out-diffusion. During the anneal, dopant near the surface diffuses out of the silicon, and a smaller Rp puts more of it near the surface. After a 1000 °C, 15 s anneal the retained dose falls by about 10 % at 0.5 keV and 15 % at 0.2 keV.
  • The protective oxide. Implants are normally made through a thin oxide that protects the surface from metal contamination, and the oxide is stripped afterwards. At sub-keV energy a large fraction of the dose stops in it: Wolf gives about 25 % of a 0.5 keV boron implant lost with a 15 nm oxide, and more than 40 % at 0.2 keV. Some ultra-low-energy implants are therefore made without the oxide. The thickness is printed as 15 nm, but sub-keV boron stops within a few nanometres (M09 Table 1 gives Rp = 9.7 nm at 2 keV), so an oxide that thick would hold nearly the whole dose; the percentages are kept as Wolf gives them and the oxide was probably much thinner.

Wolf's summary is that, for the doses an extension needs at 0.2 keV, self-sputtering, out-diffusion and the removal of the native oxide together can remove as much as 40 % of the boron; his summary names the native oxide, not a deliberately grown one. Because each loss depends on energy and dose, the notes add that a correction for the retained dose is needed before junctions made with different implants are compared (p. 87). Self-sputtering rises (about 10 % → 20 % at 10¹⁵ cm⁻²), because the sputtering yield per ion rises as ion energy falls and the shallower boron is itself sputtered. Out-diffusion rises (about 10 % → 15 % after 1000 °C, 15 s), because a smaller Rp puts more boron near the surface. Loss in the protective oxide rises (about 25 % → more than 40 % with 15 nm), because more of the shallower profile lies inside the oxide that is stripped. Together, up to about 40 % of the dose can go (Wolf). To keep the retained dose the nominal dose must rise, or the oxide must be omitted; a larger dose at a lower beam current (§6.2) costs further throughput, and a larger dose raises the sputtering loss again, since that loss grows with dose.

7 · Where the extension ends under the gate

The extension is implanted with the gate as its mask, and then the anneal and every later thermal step move its edge sideways under the gate. The final position of that edge relative to the gate edge is the overlap, and the notes (pp. 88–89) and Wolf (§5.3.4) treat it as critical on two sides. This section states both, quantifies the overlap capacitance, and works the experiment by which the right overlap is found.

7.1 Too much and too little

  • Too much overlap. The two extensions reach further under the gate, so the electrical channel length is shorter than the gate, and short-channel effects grow: the device suffers excessive leakage, meaning an off-state current above its design maximum, of the order of 1 nA/µm (Wolf). The gate-to-source and gate-to-drain overlap capacitance also grows, and that slows switching.
  • Too little overlap. The end of the channel and the extension are "not electrically well coupled" (notes), which the notes call "causing excessive resistance", and IDsat falls; Wolf's study shows the fall setting in below about 20 nm of overlap, with no further mechanism given. Taur & Ning add a reliability reason for keeping some overlap: with less than about two to three oxide thicknesses the extension risks ending outside the gate, and that ungated region is exposed to hot-carrier injection, the drain-end damage of M02 §6.1.

The overlap capacitance has a direct part, the parallel plate formed by the gate over the overlapped extension, and two fringing parts, from the side of the gate to the source/drain outside it and from the gate to the inner edge of the junction below it. 7.2 Finding the right overlap

Wolf describes how the overlap is set in practice. The implant and anneal for the extension and contact are fixed first. Then an offset spacer, a thin spacer formed on the gate after the polysilicon is etched and before the extension implant, is varied in width from zero up to some limit (50 nm in his example). The offset spacer moves the extension implant away from the gate edge by its own width, so each width gives a different overlap; the width that gives the best combination of off-current and IDsat is kept. In the study he reports, on 0.25 µm n-channel transistors with arsenic extensions 50 nm deep, the lateral diffusion under the gate edge was estimated at 0.6–0.7 times the junction depth, and IDsat fell rapidly once the overlap was below 20 nm: a minimum overlap of about 20 nm was recommended.

7.3 The steepness of the extension's edge

Beyond the notes: lateral abruptness (Wolf §5.3.5)

The notes skip Wolf's §5.3.5, which adds a fourth requirement to depth, doping and position: the extension's doping must fall off steeply along the surface where it meets the channel. An abrupt lateral edge limits how far the extension's depletion region encroaches on the channel and, more importantly, minimises Rsp, which eq. (10.4) treats as if the edge were abrupt and which, below 100 nm, becomes dominated by the edge's gradient. The design rule Wolf quotes is a fall of at least one decade in concentration within about 3 % of the gate length, for a spreading resistance under 5 % of Rsat: about 3 nm per decade at 100 nm, roughly the slope of as-implanted 0.2 keV boron, so the final profile must be as steep as the implant before any anneal. A 0° implant gives the most abrupt edge, because any tilt shadows the region beside the gate (the trade-off against channelling of M09 §3); a parallel-beam single-wafer implanter holds the angle, while the conical disk of a batch implanter can vary it by up to 1.5° across a wafer.

A process change makes the arsenic extension 40 nm deep instead of 50 nm, with the same lateral-diffusion ratio. With no offset spacer, does the overlap still meet the 20 nm minimum? With a 10 nm offset spacer? What happens to IDsat and to the overlap capacitance in each case?

Lateral diffusion is 0.6–0.7 × 40 = 24–28 nm. With no offset spacer the overlap is 24–28 nm, above 20 nm, so IDsat holds, and the direct overlap capacitance (εoxlov/tox per unit width) is 20–40 % above the 20 nm value. With a 10 nm offset spacer the overlap is 14–18 nm, below 20 nm: the extension and the channel end are poorly coupled, and by the study's result IDsat falls rapidly. The capacitance falls further, in proportion to the overlap, but that gain does not compensate the lost drive current. The shallower junction therefore needs a narrower offset spacer, at most 4–8 nm.

Read down the table, the principle is that every term of the series resistance has to fall at least as fast as the channel resistance it is measured against, and each falls by a different lever: RSDE by doping and length, Rco by interface doping, Rsp by abruptness. The levers interfere. The doping that lowers RSDE must sit in a shallower layer, which the implant-and-anneal limit of §6 caps; the length that lowers RSDE needs a narrower spacer, which the contact junction depth forbids unless the contact is elevated; and the overlap that lowers Rac costs leakage and capacitance. The elevated source/drain is the one structural change that relieves two of these conflicts at once.

Write the half-page answer: "the four series resistances of a deep-submicron source/drain — what each is — which one the notes call insignificant and why — what the 10 % criterion is measured against — why the extension's sheet resistance gets harder to meet at each node — the structure that helps". Give one number at each stage.

The current from the contact meets Rco, the silicide–silicon contact resistance, set by ρc (10⁻⁷ Ω·cm² at 0.25 µm, 4×10⁻⁸ at 50 nm, needing interface doping above 10²⁰ cm⁻³); RSDE, the extension as a resistor, ρsℓSDE/W; Rsp, the spreading from the inversion/accumulation layer into the extension's depth, (2ρ/πW) ln(βXj/Xc), which makes the sum 2.09 × RSDE at 250 nm; and Rac, the accumulation layer under the gate edge, which the notes call insignificant in deep-submicron devices because the overlap is short. The yardstick is Rsat = VDD/IDsat, with RsatW ≈ tox/(0.376vsatεox) = 0.347 Ω·cm at 4.5 nm; the series resistance may cut IDsat by no more than 10 %. Since tox falls at every node, so does the allowance, while the extension gets shallower: at 250 nm the 10 %-of-Rsat criterion is reached at 663 Ω/□, which the notes' 650 Ω/□ just meets, and holding the sheet resistance at 50 nm needs 5.5×10¹⁹ cm⁻³ in a 20–28 nm layer, shallower than the 35 nm (at ≈ 350 Ω/□) that a 0.5 keV implant and spike anneal reached in 2001; Wolf judged it unclear whether that could be done. The structure that helps is the elevated source/drain: the silicide sits in a selectively grown, heavily doped layer, so the contact junction need not be deep, the spacer can be narrow, ℓSDE shrinks, and the heavy epitaxial doping lowers ρc.

Summary

  • Two regions: a shallow extension (depth Xj, ≈ 0.4–0.55 LL) for short-channel control, and a deeper contact (Xjc, ≈ 0.8–1.1 LL) to hold the silicide without leakage. Deeper contact → wider spacer → longer extension → more RSDE.
  • Sheet resistance ρs = ρ/t (Ω/□); R = ρsL/W, by counting squares, independent of size (eq. (10.1)).
  • Rs = Rco + RSDE + Rsp + Rac, measured against RsatW ≈ tox/(0.376vsatεox); source and drain together may cut IDsat by ≤ 10 % (≈ 10 % of Rsat costs ≈ 8 % of current).
  • Rsp = (2ρ/πW) ln(βXj/Xc), β 0.37–0.9; RSDE = ρℓSDE/(WXj); their sum is RSDE times a bracket of 2.09 (250 nm) to 1.48 (50 nm). Extension sheet resistance ≈ 650 Ω/□ at 250 nm; 5.5×10¹⁹ cm⁻³ at 50 nm.
  • RcoW ≥ ρc/(2LL); ρc = 10⁻⁷ (0.25 µm) → 4×10⁻⁸ Ω·cm² (50 nm), interface doping > 10²⁰ cm⁻³ for a 0.5 eV barrier.
  • Silicides: TiSi₂ 13–16, CoSi₂ and NiSi 14–20 µΩ·cm, 4–7 × aluminium's 2.7–3.0. Silicon consumed per nm of silicide: 0.90 TiSi₂, 1.03 CoSi₂, 0.78 NiSi (Plummer); the notes' "75 %" is Wolf's TiSi₂ ratio.
  • Elevated source/drain: selective n⁺/p⁺ epitaxy below 850 °C; narrow spacer, short ℓSDE, silicide above the junction, heavy doping for low ρc.
  • Ultra-shallow junctions: sub-keV implants (smaller Rp, straggle and channelling; less TED), limited by Child's law, I ∝ V3/2 (1–2 mA against 10–25 mA); spike RTA, 100–200 °C/s up, 50–80 °C/s down, because activation (5.4 eV) outruns diffusion (3.4 eV) at high temperature and TED is worst at low; limit ≈ 35 nm and 350 Ω/□ with 0.5 keV boron.
  • Dose loss below 1 keV: self-sputtering, out-diffusion and the stripped protective oxide, up to ≈ 40 % at 0.2 keV.
  • Overlap: too much → leakage and overlap capacitance (Cov ≈ εoxW(lov/tox + 7)); too little → IDsat falls. ≈ 20 nm minimum at 0.25 µm, set with an offset spacer; lateral diffusion 0.6–0.7 Xj.
