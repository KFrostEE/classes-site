Module 08

Well Engineering and Retrograde Wells

How the two wells of a CMOS process are made. The diffused well, implanted at moderate energy and driven in for many hours, and the reasons it stopped serving below 0.5 µm; then the retrograde well, implanted at high energy with its doping placed depth by depth, the chained-implant recipe that builds it, the field isolation it has to serve, and what its heavily doped bottom does against punch-through, latch-up and soft errors.

Prerequisites M02, M03 Read time ~55 min Sources Class notes pp. 68–74 (Chapter 5) and the Fig. 1-7 insert; Lectures 15–16; Wolf Vol. 4 ch. 5; Plummer, Deal & Griffin

Chapter 5 of the notes is titled "The structure of deep submicron MOSFETs" and names four subjects: retrograde wells, super-steep retrograde channel doping, drain engineering and punch-through control structures. This module takes the first, notes pp. 68–74. The second, with the pocket implants, is M09 (pp. 75–76 and 89–90), and the drain is M10 (pp. 77–88). A well is a region of the substrate doped to one type so that transistors of the opposite channel type can be built in it (M02 §5.1): an n-channel transistor needs a p-type body and a p-channel transistor an n-type body. The well's doping is the transistor's body doping. It enters the threshold voltage through the depletion-charge term (M03 §4), it fixes the depletion widths that decide punch-through (M02 §5.2), and it sets the capacitance of every source and drain junction. Making a well is therefore choosing a doping profile from the surface down to a micrometre or more, and this chapter of the notes is an account of how much control over that profile each way of making wells gives.

§1 sets out the twin-well CMOS inverter that every later section refers to. §2 is the diffused well: what its long drive-in does to the implanted dopant, why the well had to be deep, and the five problems the notes list for it below 0.5 µm. §3 treats the isolation between devices — the parasitic field transistor, the boron the field oxide takes from the p-well, and the channel-stop implant that restores it. §4 introduces the retrograde well, made by high-energy implantation, and §5 the protection its doped bottom gives against latch-up. §6 is the chained-implant recipe of the notes, §7 the modulated well on shallow trench isolation, and §8 lays out the complete sequence of each well process side by side and separates the three different implants that are all called punch-through implants. Physics the notes do not state but that is needed to reason about their statements is marked beyond the notes. Where a lecture stated something that the notes or the literature contradict, a flagged box says so beside the notes' version.

1 · The twin-well CMOS inverter

The notes open the chapter with the structure: in twin-well CMOS technology "each well is formed separately by implanting the well dopants into a lightly doped substrate", which "allows performance adjustments for both PMOS and NMOS tailored separately" (p. 68). Wolf, whose chapter the notes condense, calls twin-well CMOS the dominant CMOS technology in production. The circuit is the CMOS inverter of M01 §6: an n-channel and a p-channel transistor in series between the supply and ground, their gates joined as the input and their drains joined as the output. Figure 1 is that circuit built in silicon. It redraws, as a section, the perspective drawing Wolf gives as his Fig. 1-7, which the notes carry as an insert after p. 68.

Read from left to right, the n-channel transistor in the p-well has an n⁺ source and an n⁺ drain on either side of its gate, and the p-channel transistor in the n-well a p⁺ drain and a p⁺ source. The ground line contacts the n-channel source and a p⁺ diffusion in the p-well, the p-well contact; the power line contacts the p-channel source and an n⁺ diffusion in the n-well, the n-well contact. The contacts hold each well at the potential of its transistor's source, the p-well at ground and the n-well at VDD, so that every source and drain junction stays at zero or reverse bias whatever the output does. The output line joins the two drains and crosses the central field oxide, the thick isolation oxide that separates the two transistors from each other and the inverter from its neighbours. Lecture 16 walked through the same figure. Beneath the field oxide where it lies on the p-well, the left island and the p-well end of the central one, lies a p-type channel stop (dashed); §3 explains both the oxide and the channel stop.

"Twin" means that both transistor types have a well of their own. A process with a single well builds the other transistor type directly in the substrate, whose doping must then serve as that transistor's body doping as well as everything else asked of the wafer. With two wells, each body doping is set by its own implant, so the threshold voltage, the punch-through margin and the junction capacitance of each transistor type can be adjusted without disturbing the other — the advantage the notes state and Lecture 15 repeated.

The notes specify the starting wafer in Chapter 1 (p. 10): a p⁺ substrate carrying a lightly doped p-type epitaxial layer, or the n-type equivalent (M02 §7). An epitaxial layer is single-crystal silicon grown on the wafer and continuing its lattice, so its doping can be chosen independently of the substrate's. The wells are formed in that layer, and the heavily doped substrate below provides a low-resistance path that §5 shows to be a defence against latch-up. The notes give no numbers for any of these layers. Lectures 15 and 16 did:

2 · The diffused well, and why it stopped scaling

The first twin wells were made in two steps. The well dopant was implanted at moderate energy, which leaves it within a few tenths of a micrometre of the surface, and a long high-temperature anneal, the drive-in, then diffused it down to the depth the well needed. The notes call the result a diffused well structure. This section takes what the drive-in does to the dopant (§2.1), why the well had to be deep (§2.2), and the five problems the notes list for diffused wells once CMOS was scaled below 0.5 µm (§2.3).

2.1 The recipe, and what the drive-in does

The notes' recipe is an implant at moderate energy — 50–100 keV (p. 70) — after which "the impurities were driven into the substrate by long 12–24 hr, high temperature (>1100 °C) diffusion" (p. 68). Lecture 15 gave the same figures. The notes name no species. In Plummer's flow the p-well is boron and the n-well phosphorus, because the two have nearly equal diffusion coefficients and one drive-in takes both wells to about the same depth (Plummer p. 62): at 1100 °C their intrinsic diffusivities are 1.4×10⁻¹³ and 1.5×10⁻¹³ cm²/s (Plummer Table 7-3). Lecture 15 also named arsenic for the n-well. Arsenic diffuses about seven times more slowly than phosphorus at that temperature, 2.1×10⁻¹⁴ cm²/s, and as the heavier ion it needs a higher energy to reach the same implanted depth, which is why Plummer passes it over for the well.

With an oxide over the surface to keep the dopant from escaping (Plummer p. 392), the drive-in neither adds dopant nor removes it. The dose Q — the number of dopant atoms beneath unit area of surface, the integral of the concentration over depth — stays fixed while the profile spreads, so a thin, concentrated implanted layer becomes a deep, dilute well. On a linear concentration axis the dose is the area under the profile, and the two areas are equal (Figure 2(a)). On the logarithmic axis the notes draw profiles on, areas cannot be compared; that axis shows instead the shape the drive-in leaves, highest at the surface and falling monotonically with depth to the junction (Figure 2(b)). The notes write this beside their sketch of a conventional well: "[the doping] after drive-in is highest at the surface and monotonically decreas[es] with depth" (p. 70; the handwriting reads "Drive in after drive-in").

Mean concentration over the well. The drive-in conserves Q, so 10¹⁴ cm⁻² ÷ 2×10⁻⁴ cm = 5×10¹⁷ cm⁻³.

What dose makes a well 2 µm deep that averages 10¹⁶ cm⁻³, the low end of the lectures' well doping?

Q = 10¹⁶ cm⁻³ × 2×10⁻⁴ cm = 2×10¹² cm⁻², fifty times less than the layer of the example. Implanted into the top 0.1 µm, that dose is 2×10¹⁷ cm⁻³, not 10¹⁹ cm⁻³.

Lecture 15's drive-in numbers

Lecture 15 described the drive-in as taking a 0.1 µm layer at 10¹⁹ cm⁻³ down to about 10¹⁵ cm⁻³ in a well about 2 µm deep, and gave 12 h for a 1 µm well against 24 h for a 2 µm one. These figures do not hold together. The layer averages 5×10¹⁷ cm⁻³ over 2 µm, five hundred times 10¹⁵ cm⁻³, and a well that ended at the 10¹⁵ cm⁻³ of the epitaxial layer it sits in would be no well at all: an n-well compensated to zero net doping, a p-well indistinguishable from the epitaxial layer. Depth grows as the square root of time, equation (8.2) below, so doubling the time multiplies the depth by about 1.4, not 2; and 12–24 h at 1100 °C gives wells 3–4 µm deep or more, the notes' figure (p. 69), while 1 µm takes less than an hour. The lecture placed this arithmetic in the fabrication course. It is worked below.

2.2 Why the well had to be deep

The notes give the reason for the long drive-in in one sentence: "Deep drive-in step was needed to prevent punch through from the MOSFET drain regions at the surface to the substrate under well" (p. 68). Take the p-channel transistor of Figure 1. Its p⁺ drain sits in the n-well, and beneath the n-well lies the p-type epitaxial layer, so drain, well and substrate form a p–n–p stack, and so do the p⁺ source, the well and the substrate. The well is held at VDD by its contact and the substrate at ground, so the well–substrate junction always carries VDD. Each p⁺ junction's depletion region spreads down into the well, and the well–substrate junction's spreads up. If the two meet, no neutral n-type silicon is left between the p⁺ region and the substrate to hold a potential barrier, and holes can pass from one to the other through the depleted well. A current needs a p⁺ region at a potential above the substrate's: the source, always at VDD, or the drain while the output is high. The deepest depletion comes with the output low, when the drain is at ground and its junction with the well carries VDD as well; condition (8.3) below takes that bias on both junctions, which makes it a conservative criterion that covers every bias. This is vertical punch-through: the source-to-drain punch-through of M02 §5.2, L > 2W, turned on its side, with the depth of the well in place of the channel length.

Depth or doping

In Lecture 15 a student asked whether it is the depth of the well or its doping that prevents this punch-through, and the answer given was the depth. Both decide it. Condition (8.3) sets a minimum depth, and the doping sets that minimum: 1.14 µm at 10¹⁶ cm⁻³ against 0.31 µm at 10¹⁷ cm⁻³, because both depletion widths shrink as the doping rises, W ∝ N−1/2 (M02 §5.2). A diffused well is most lightly doped near its bottom, where its doping falls toward the junction, so it had to be deep. A well whose doping is highest near its bottom can be shallow, which is one of the advantages the notes give the retrograde well (§5).

2.3 Five problems below 0.5 µm

"Diffused well structures give rise to problems when the CMOS is scaled below 0.5 µm" (p. 68). The notes list five, reproduced here in their order and wording; the mechanism behind each follows the list.

  1. "Lateral (as well as vertical) diffusion occurs during the diffusion steps which has reduced packing densities."
  2. "If LOCOS isolation is used, boron from the p-well dopant implant-step segregates into the oxide during the long, high temperature field oxide growth step. Compensating for this effect requires a separate field threshold-adjust implant (channel stop implant) prior to the field oxidation step. This additional step adds process complexity."
  3. "Diffusion of the channel-stop implant dopant into the active regions during the field oxidation adversely affects both junction capacitance and turn off characteristics."
  4. "High temperature drive in step increases process cycle time, and increase in risk of Si wafer warpage, bow, flatness degradation."
  5. "The diffused well profile for a particular technology node is the net result of compromises between the need to control threshold-voltage, to suppress hot-carrier effects, to control punch through and soft error effects, and to provide latch up immunity."

Problem 1, lateral diffusion. Diffusion carries dopant in every direction down the concentration gradient. Dopant that enters through a window in a mask therefore spreads sideways beneath the mask edge as well as downward (M01 §7.2), and a well driven microns deep spreads microns sideways. The notes quantify it in an example (p. 69): "Lateral diffusion is ~0.7 times the junction depth. A vertical junction depth of 3–4 µm results in a lateral spread of 2–3 µm. The maximum aspect ratio of a diffused well is limited to about 1.6:1. This would intermingle with the dopant of adjacent wells." Wolf's text, which the notes condense, supplies the condition the last sentence refers to: wells narrower than about 1.6 times their depth would intermingle with the dopant of adjacent wells. The aspect ratio is the well's width to its depth. In a twin-well process the n-well and p-well windows abut, so every well has neighbours of the opposite type whose dopant spreads into it (Figure 4).

Use 0.7 for the spread and 1.6:1 for the aspect ratio, as the notes do, without deriving one from the other.

Problems 2 and 3, the field oxide. Both concern LOCOS, the isolation process of M02 §9, and §3 takes them in full: why the boron that the field oxide takes from the p-well matters, and what the channel-stop implant that replaces it does. Problem 3 follows from where the channel stop sits. It is boron placed under the field oxide at the edge of every n-channel active area, beside the n⁺ sources and drains, and during the long field oxidation it diffuses sideways into the active area. An n⁺ junction that faces more boron has a narrower depletion region and therefore a larger capacitance (equation (8.12), §5). The notes give the second effect, on the turn-off characteristics — of the n-channel transistors, Wolf adds — without its mechanism, which Wolf refers to his Vol. 3.

Problem 4, the thermal budget. Twelve to twenty-four hours in a furnace is that much of every wafer's cycle time, the time it spends in the production line. Near 1100 °C silicon is plastically soft (M04 §3), and the temperature differences across a wafer while it is loaded and ramped set up stresses that can make it slip and warp, which is why furnaces load and ramp slowly (Plummer pp. 392–393). Warpage and bow are departures of the whole wafer from a plane; flatness is the variation of its surface height. The notes' own wafer specification for the ULSI era (M04 §5) allows less than 5 µm of bow and less than 1 µm of flatness variation. Flatness matters because the lithography tool must hold a whole exposure field in focus at once (M02 §9). Wolf adds a risk the notes leave out: metal contamination from the furnace's heating elements.

Problem 5, the compromise. Each requirement in the notes' list wants its own doping at its own depth. Threshold control is set by the doping at the surface (M03 §4). Hot-carrier suppression wants a lower field at the drain junction, and so lighter doping there (M02 §6.1). Punch-through control wants heavier doping below the surface, where the source and drain depletion regions approach each other (M02 §5.2). Soft-error control wants heavier doping below the surface too, because it narrows the depletion regions that collect the charge a particle strike leaves in the silicon (Wolf p. 185; M07b §2 describes soft errors). Latch-up immunity wants heavy doping deep in the well (§5). A diffused well has two adjustable quantities, the dose and the drive-in, and they fix one profile that falls monotonically from the surface, so it can only compromise. Wolf gives the example the notes drop: high doping deep in the well strengthens latch-up immunity, and a diffused well cannot have it, because its doping at the bottom must be lower than at the surface, and the surface doping is capped by the limits on junction breakdown voltage and junction capacitance. A well whose doping can be placed at each depth independently removes the compromise. That is the retrograde well of §4.

3 · Field isolation: the field transistor, boron segregation and the channel stop

Problems 2 and 3 of the notes' list concern the isolation between transistors. Neighbouring devices must not conduct into one another along the silicon surface between them, and in the processes of this chapter that surface lies under the field oxide grown by LOCOS. This section first sets out the parasitic transistor that every field region forms (§3.1), then the boron the field oxide takes from the p-well as it grows (§3.2), and then the channel-stop implant that restores it (§3.3).

3.1 The parasitic field transistor

LOCOS, local oxidation of silicon, grows the field oxide wherever a patterned silicon nitride film, laid over a thin pad oxide, leaves the silicon exposed (M02 §9 gives the process and its bird's beak). The lectures put its thickness at about a micrometre, as M02 §9 does; the notes' own thickness table in Chapter 3 gives 200–400 nm for a LOCOS field oxide (M05 §1), and Plummer's flow uses about 0.5 µm. Whatever its thickness, the oxide has one job, and Lecture 15 put it in terms of the output line of Figure 1: if the oxide under that line is not thick enough, a MOSFET forms there.

Any conductor that crosses a field region — the metal output line of Figure 1, or a polysilicon line — is the gate of a parasitic MOS transistor. The field oxide is its gate insulator and the p-type surface beneath is its channel. Between two n-channel transistors the would-be channel runs from the n⁺ drain of one to the n⁺ source of the next, which serve as its source and drain (Figure 5). Under the output line of Figure 1 it runs from the n-channel transistor's n⁺ drain across the p-well surface to the n-well: in Lecture 15's words, the two wells would be shorted. Either way the surface that inverts is the p-type one. Plummer calls these parasitic MOS devices field oxide transistors (p. 61). If the line's voltage exceeds the threshold voltage of this field transistor, an inversion layer forms under the field oxide and joins the two diffusions. 3.2 Boron lost to the field oxide

In the diffused-well process the wells are implanted and driven in first and the field oxide is grown afterwards: the well dopants "must be implanted before the field-oxide, and driven-in either before — or during — the field-oxidation step" (Wolf p. 184), and Lecture 15 gave the same order. The p-well's boron is therefore in the silicon while the field oxide grows, and the notes state what happens to it: "boron from the p-well dopant implant-step segregates into the oxide during the long, high temperature field oxide growth step" (p. 69).

Segregation is the redistribution of a dopant between two materials in contact. At the interface the dopant settles at a fixed ratio of its concentrations on the two sides, the segregation coefficient:

m = CSiCSiO₂
(8.7)
m segregation coefficient at the Si/SiO₂ interface (dimensionless) · CSi, CSiO₂ dopant concentrations on the silicon and the oxide side of the interface (cm⁻³). Plummer §7.5.4 gives typical values of 0.3 for boron and about 10 for phosphorus, arsenic and antimony; they depend on temperature, and for boron on the moisture in the oxidising ambient.

Thermal oxidation consumes silicon, so the interface moves into the silicon and sweeps through the dopant already there (M05a §4). Boron, with m below one, is taken up into the growing oxide, and the silicon just beneath the oxide is depleted of it. Phosphorus, with m above one, is rejected by the oxide and piles up in the silicon ahead of the moving interface (Plummer pp. 414–416; Figure 6).

The consequence for isolation runs through the field threshold. Less boron in the silicon under the field oxide is a smaller depletion charge, and in equation (8.6) that is a lower VT,field: in the example, halving the surface boron takes the field threshold from 4.4 V to 2.3 V. Only the p-well side suffers. Under the n-well's field oxide the phosphorus piles up instead, making the surface more strongly n-type, and the positive interface charge, which pushes a p-type surface toward inversion, pushes an n-type surface away from it; both raise the magnitude of the threshold of the p-channel field transistors there. That is why Figure 1 has channel stops only beneath the field oxide that lies on the p-well.

"Boron penetration" is the gate's problem, not the field oxide's

Lecture 16 called this effect boron penetration, and said that the boron changes the electrical properties of the field oxide. The notes' word is "segregates". In this course boron penetration is the name for boron from a p⁺ polysilicon gate crossing the thin gate oxide into the channel (M02 §8, M06 §9.1). What lowers the field threshold, in the notes' account and in Plummer's, is the boron removed from the silicon beneath the oxide, through the depletion-charge term of (8.6). Lecture 16 drew the same conclusion when it came to the cure: more boron restores the threshold "because the QD".

3.3 The channel-stop implant

The notes' remedy: "Compensating for this effect requires a separate field threshold-adjust implant (channel stop implant) prior to the field oxidation step. This additional step adds process complexity" (p. 69). The channel-stop implant is boron placed in the p-type field regions before the field oxide grows, enough that after segregation the surface beneath the oxide still holds the field threshold above the supply; in the example a surface doped 5×10¹⁶ cm⁻³ gives 14 V under 0.5 µm of oxide. Plummer describes the usual way of placing it: a low-energy boron implant, about 10¹³ cm⁻² at 50 keV, through the openings in the LOCOS nitride before oxidation, with the resist, nitride and pad-oxide stack masking the active areas, so the boron lands only where field oxide will grow. As the oxide grows most of that boron diffuses ahead of it into the silicon, and some is incorporated into the oxide and lost (Plummer p. 64).

The channel stop then causes problem 3 of the notes' list. It sits at the edge of every n-channel active area, and the long field oxidation that follows its implant drives it sideways into the active area, against the n⁺ sources and drains. The problem belongs to the diffused-well flow because there the wells come before the field oxide; §4 shows that a well implanted after the field oxide does not lose its boron to it, and §8 sets the two sequences side by side.

4 · Retrograde wells

The notes introduce the replacement in three sentences (p. 70): "Medium energy (50–100 keV) is replaced with high energy (300 keV – 1 MeV) implant. This leaves the peak of the implant location deeper in Si substrate. This is retrograde." A profile is retrograde when its concentration rises with depth below the surface instead of falling (the term M02 §5.3 uses for the channel profile). This section takes how such a well is implanted (§4.1), how far it spreads sideways (§4.2), what the anneal after it does (§4.3), where it sits in the process relative to the field oxide (§4.4), what it does to the spacing between devices (§4.5), and the control over the doping at every depth that Wolf calls well engineering (§4.6).

4.1 High-energy implantation

An ion implanter makes ions from a gas, selects one species by bending the beam through a magnetic field (M03 §9.3), and accelerates the selected ions to their final energy, in Plummer's description in a small linear accelerator (p. 466). Most implanters cover 30–200 keV for singly charged ions. Doubly charged ions gain twice the energy across the same voltage, at a lower beam current, and energies in the MeV range serve, in Plummer's words, applications such as the deep wells of a CMOS technology (§8.3.1). Lecture 16 named the linear accelerator when a student asked how such energies are reached.

The energy sets the depth of the peak, the projected range Rp of M03 §9.2. Wolf puts the peak "at roughly 1-µm for the energies most frequently used", and the notes' sketch of a retrograde well on p. 70 puts it a little shallower. SRIM range tables give boron Rp = 0.79 µm at 300 keV and 1.13 µm at 500 keV, and phosphorus 0.73 µm at 600 keV; at the 50–100 keV of the diffused well's implant they give 0.17–0.32 µm for boron. Phosphorus needs about twice boron's energy for the same depth, because a heavier ion has a shorter range at a given energy (Plummer p. 62). Lecture 16's figures — a range of about 0.8 µm for the high-energy implant against about 0.1 µm at 50–100 keV — are of the right order: about right for phosphorus, whose range at 50–100 keV is 0.07–0.13 µm, and short for boron. With its peak this deep, the implant's own concentration at the surface lies far below its peak (Figure 7). How far, the Gaussian of equation (3.9) cannot say: it fits an implanted profile only near the peak, and boron profiles are skewed toward the surface (Plummer p. 459). Wolf's example of a 400 keV boron p-well has a few times 10¹⁶ cm⁻³ at the surface, about a decade below its peak (his Fig. 5-3). In a finished well the surface doping is set by later, shallower implants, and Wolf adds that the retrograde implant's own contribution at the surface should be at least five times below that final value.

4.2 Lateral straggle

The notes' first advantage of the retrograde well is "Much less lateral-dopant spread ... for a retrograde well having the same junction depth as a diffused well" (p. 70). An implanted ion is scattered sideways as well as slowed by its collisions, so ions that enter the silicon at one point come to rest spread about it laterally as well as in depth. The standard deviation of that lateral spread is the lateral straggle, ΔR⊥, the sideways counterpart of the straggle ΔRp in depth. As a standard deviation, the lateral straggle of boron at 500 keV is 0.18 µm in SRIM's range tables (0.15 µm at 300 keV; 0.14 µm for phosphorus at 600 keV), about a quarter of 0.7 µm; Plummer notes that simulators take the lateral straggle as some fraction of the vertical straggle, and SRIM gives the two as comparable. Neither source says what quantity the 0.7 µm measures. One reading reconciles the two, and it is this page's, not Wolf's: the sideways reach of the doping beyond the mask edge before it falls to the background. By equation (8.8), a 3×10¹³ cm⁻² boron implant at 500 keV reaches 0.53 µm under the mask at 10¹⁵ cm⁻³ and 0.65 µm at 10¹⁴ cm⁻³, three to four lateral straggles, and the anneals that follow carry it toward the ~1 µm the notes give next. Unlike a straggle, that reach depends on the dose and the background; another fit is the full width of ±2 standard deviations, 4ΔR⊥ = 0.71 µm. For the course, the lateral straggle at 500 keV is ~0.7 µm (notes, Wolf), and the lateral spread after all processing ~1 µm.

4.3 The activation anneal, and no long drive-in

The same advantage in the notes continues: "Very little additional diffusion occurs after implantation as a short anneal is performed at a relatively low temp ... to activate the implant" (p. 70). An implant leaves the lattice damaged and much of its dopant off the lattice sites, where it gives no carrier; an anneal restores the crystal and moves the dopant onto lattice sites, where it is electrically active (M03 §9.2). What the anneal does not do

Lecture 16 said that the peak moves deeper during the anneal, from about 0.8 µm to about 1.0 µm, and a student asked whether gravity pulls the atoms down. The peak does not move. Diffusion carries dopant down its concentration gradient, which on the two sides of a peak points in opposite directions, so far from the surface the profile spreads symmetrically about Rp: in the example the peak stays at 0.727 µm while the deep-side junction moves 0.05 µm. Gravity has no measurable effect: lifting a phosphorus atom one micrometre takes about 3×10⁻¹² eV, ten orders of magnitude below kT at the anneal temperature. Lecture 16 also said that the implanted top layer becomes amorphous. An implant amorphises silicon only above a threshold dose, about 10¹⁵ cm⁻² in Plummer's example (M03 §9.2), thirty times the well dose; and a high-energy ion does most of its displacing near the end of its path, deep, because at high energy it loses its energy mainly to electrons — for boron at 500 keV, electronic stopping is 65 times nuclear stopping in SRIM's tables — and the nuclear collisions that displace silicon atoms take over only as the ion slows near the end of its range. Wolf calls that deep damage end-of-range damage (§6).

The short anneal is the only heat the retrograde well's profile is meant to see. Wolf: the retrograde profile "is retained by minimizing the temperature-cycles of later process steps (e.g., by using RTP instead of furnace anneals)" (p. 183), and the early retrograde twin-well processes used well "drive-in" steps of only about 5 min by rapid thermal processing (p. 186). A furnace drive-in long enough to move the dopant microns would spread the buried peak back toward a monotonic profile, and the well would no longer be retrograde.

No long drive-in after the retrograde implant

Lecture 16 described a drive-in after the retrograde implant that takes the well to 2–3 µm, with a peak of 10¹⁹ cm⁻³ falling to about 10¹⁷ cm⁻³. The notes say the opposite: very little additional diffusion, a short anneal at a relatively low temperature (p. 70). The notes' 3×10¹³ cm⁻² well implants peak near 8–9×10¹⁷ cm⁻³ as implanted (§6), not 10¹⁹ cm⁻³, and the 30 min anneal at 1100 °C of the check above already halves the peak. A short activation anneal, and a low thermal budget afterwards, is what defines the method.

4.4 Wells after the field oxide

The notes: "High energy implanted wells are formed after the long, high temperature cycle needed to grow the LOCOS field oxide, lateral-spread of the well dopant is further reduced" (p. 71). A high-energy ion passes through the field oxide and comes to rest in the silicon beneath, so the wells can be implanted after isolation, whether LOCOS or shallow trench isolation (Wolf p. 184). The diffused well cannot be: its dopant is implanted before the field oxide and driven in before or during the field oxidation. Figure 10 sets the two sequences side by side. Implanting after the field oxide does two things. The wells never see the field oxidation's long high-temperature cycle, so they do not spread in it; and the p-well's boron is not in the silicon while the oxide grows, so none of it segregates into the oxide and the p-well's field threshold stays higher (Wolf p. 184, after Rung, Dell'Oca and Walker, 1981). That second effect removes the cause of problems 2 and 3.

Retrograde wells and LOCOS

Lecture 16 said that LOCOS is not performed for the retrograde structure. The notes say the opposite: the high-energy wells are formed after the cycle that grows the LOCOS field oxide (p. 71), and the notes' own sketch of the process on p. 74 implants them through LOCOS islands (Figure 10(b)). Lecture 16 ended by saying that LOCOS is not used for [sub-]micron technology (the captions are garbled there). The notes date its end to the 0.35 µm generation (p. 11), after which shallow trench isolation replaced it, for its own reasons: the bird's beak and the non-planar surface (M02 §9). The notes' p. 74 shows the retrograde well on trench isolation as well (§7).

4.6 Well engineering

The notes' second advantage is the larger one: "By combining the high-energy implant step with other lower energy MOSFET channel implant (VT adjust and punch through stopper) and with minimal thermal diffusion, control of dopant levels throughout the depth of the well becomes possible. This permits optimization of device characteristics: Device speed, carrier mobility and S/D capacitance, inter-device leakage currents, VT-control, punch through suppression, parasitic transistor effect, and soft error and latch up resistance" (p. 71). Wolf calls this well engineering. Each implant of the chain puts its dopant at the depth its energy selects, and with almost no diffusion afterwards each depth keeps the doping it was given (Figure 12). Wolf gives the mechanism in two sentences: low surface doping raises the carrier mobility and lowers the capacitance; heavier doping below the surface narrows the depletion regions and so suppresses the collection of minority carriers, which reduces soft errors and susceptibility to latch-up. That is the compromise of the diffused well's problem 5, removed.

5 · What the bottom of the well buys: latch-up and the other advantages

After well engineering the notes list five "other advantages of retrograde wells" (pp. 71–72). Two of them concern latch-up, which M02 §7 introduced in one paragraph and Lecture 16 named in passing. The mechanism comes first (§5.1), because the advantages are statements about the parts of it that the well's doping controls; the five advantages follow in the notes' order (§5.2).

5.1 Latch-up

A bipolar transistor is a pair of back-to-back junctions with a thin middle region: carriers injected across a forward-biased emitter–base junction diffuse through the base and are swept into the collector by the reverse-biased base–collector junction. The CMOS structure of Figure 1 contains two such transistors that nobody designed (Plummer p. 466). The vertical pnp has the p⁺ source of the p-channel transistor as its emitter, the n-well as its base and the p-type substrate as its collector. The lateral npn has the n⁺ source of the n-channel transistor as its emitter, the p-well and substrate as its base and the n-well as its collector. The two share regions: the pnp's collector is the npn's base, and the npn's collector is the pnp's base (Figure 13).

In normal operation both are off. Each source is tied to its own well — the p⁺ source and the n-well to VDD, the n⁺ source and the p-well to ground — so every emitter–base junction sits at zero bias. The wells and the substrate, however, have resistance. A transient current flowing in the well or the substrate develops a voltage across that resistance, Rwell or Rsub, and when it reaches about 0.7 V it forward-biases an emitter junction and turns that transistor on (Plummer p. 467). Its collector current then flows in the other transistor's base region, through the other resistance, and turns the second transistor on, whose collector current in turn feeds the first. The pair is cross-coupled into a pnpn structure, a thyristor (Figure 14). Once triggered, the structure can carry a large current from VDD to ground and destroy the chip (Plummer p. 467), and the latched state persists until the supply is removed (EEE5416 M11 §10). That is latch-up. With no shunting resistance (each base open to its emitter), the loop sustains itself once the current gains of the two transistors satisfy the switching condition of a thyristor (Sze & Ng §11.2):

αnpn + αpnp ≥ 1
⇔  βnpnβpnp ≥ 1
(8.10)
α common-base current gain of each parasitic transistor, the fraction of its emitter current that reaches its collector (dimensionless) · β = α/(1 − α), its common-emitter current gain, collector current per unit base current (dimensionless). The two forms are equivalent. The well and substrate resistances carry part of each base current around the emitter junction, so a structure with low resistances needs a larger gain product to latch; a first-order condition.

Every remedy lowers a gain or a resistance. Plummer's: build the transistors in a lightly doped epitaxial layer on a heavily doped substrate, whose high conductivity shunts the parasitic pnpn devices with low-value resistances (pp. 65, 467) — the wafer of Figure 1 and of M02 §7 — or implant a heavily doped buried layer at MeV energies into a bulk wafer to the same end. The retrograde well acts on both quantities at once, as advantages 1 and 3 below state. A particle strike that leaves charge in the well or the substrate is one such trigger; EEE5416 M11 §10 treats single-event latch-up, and its §3 the same retrograde and buried layers as barriers to the collection of strike charge.

5.2 The other advantages, in the notes' order

  1. "A retarded electric field is created in the parasitic-vertical bipolar transistor, thereby reducing its current gain. This affords extra protection against latch up."
  2. "Susceptibility to vertical punch-through is reduced without having to significantly raise the surface doping concentration."
  3. "The conductivity in the bottom of the well is increased, which also provides additional resistance against latch up."
  4. "The shallower well depth should also make the drain of the parasitic n⁺-to n well (or p⁺-to p-well) parasitic FETs shallower. This contributes to improvements in both the short channel threshold-roll off effect" [and the punch-through characteristics of these parasitic field-region devices — the notes' sentence stops after its first item; the second is Wolf's, p. 185].
  5. "Retrograde wells with high energy implants results in a reduced doping concentration under the bottom of the source and drain regions, which reduces the S/D-to body capacitance."

Advantage 1, the retarding field. A doping gradient in a transistor's base creates a built-in electric field there. In equilibrium the majority carriers' tendency to diffuse from the more heavily doped side toward the more lightly doped side must be balanced by a field that pushes them back, and that field acts on the minority carriers crossing the base as well (Sze & Ng p. 248, eqs. 14–15):

|E| = kTq · 1N dNdx
(8.11)
E built-in field in a non-uniformly doped region (V/cm) · kT/q = 0.0259 V at 300 K · N(x) the doping (cm⁻³) at position x (cm). In an n-type region the field points from the more heavily toward the more lightly doped side, and it pushes holes the same way. Equilibrium, non-degenerate doping. Sze & Ng use a base whose doping falls from emitter to collector so that this field speeds carriers across it, the drift transistor.

In the vertical pnp the emitter is the p⁺ source at the surface and the base is the n-well. In a retrograde well the donor concentration rises with depth from the surface toward the buried peak, so the field between the emitter and the peak points up, toward the surface, and pushes the holes injected by the emitter back toward it. Fewer reach the collector, and the transistor's current gain falls: the "retarded electric field" of the notes. One straggle above the peak of the 600 keV n-well implant, where the doping changes by a factor e in about 0.14 µm, (8.11) gives 0.0259 V/1.44×10⁻⁵ cm ≈ 1.8 kV/cm (beyond the notes). A diffused well, highest at the surface, has the gradient the other way, and the same field helps the holes across, as in Sze & Ng's drift transistor.

Advantage 2, vertical punch-through. This is the §2.2 condition again. A well that is heavily doped at its bottom narrows both depletion regions where they would meet — the clearance falls from 1.14 µm at 10¹⁶ cm⁻³ to 0.31 µm at 10¹⁷ cm⁻³ in the §2.2 example — and the retrograde well puts that doping at the bottom without adding any at the surface, where it would raise the threshold and the junction capacitance and lower the mobility.

Advantage 3, the conductive bottom. The npn's collector current, and any transient current that triggers the loop, flows sideways through the n-well to the n-well contact, and most of it flows where the well conducts best. A heavily doped layer at the bottom of the well lowers Rwell, so a larger current is needed to drop 0.7 V across it, and it shunts the pnp's emitter junction more strongly — the effect Plummer obtains from a buried layer.

Advantage 4, the parasitic field-region FETs. The field transistor of §3 need not join two n⁺ diffusions. An n⁺ diffusion in the p-well and the neighbouring n-well are also a source and a drain, with the p-type surface between them as the channel and any line crossing the field oxide as the gate; a p⁺ diffusion in the n-well and the neighbouring p-well form the p-channel counterpart. In these devices the well is the drain, and a shallower well is a shallower drain. As in any short MOSFET, the source and drain junctions balance part of the depletion charge under the gate, so the gate needs less voltage to reach threshold, and that share, and with it the fall of the threshold as the spacing shrinks, grows with the junction depth (Sze & Ng §6.4.2, eqs. 98–99; the threshold roll-off of M07 §5). A shallower drain therefore keeps the parasitic device's threshold up and delays its punch-through, which also helps the n⁺-to-p⁺ spacing of §4.5 shrink (this page's inference; Wolf credits that reduction to the smaller lateral spread).

Advantage 5, the junction capacitance. A reverse-biased junction stores charge in its depletion region like a parallel-plate capacitor whose plate separation is the depletion width:

Cj = εSiW
(8.12)
Cj junction capacitance per unit area (F/cm²) · εSi = 1.044×10⁻¹² F/cm · W depletion width (cm), from (2.4) of M02 for a one-sided junction. Since W ∝ N−1/2, lighter doping on the lightly doped side gives a wider depletion region and a smaller capacitance. Depletion approximation.

A source or drain junction's capacitance to the body is charged and discharged at every switching event, so it slows the circuit. The retrograde well leaves the doping under the source and drain bottoms low, because its peak is far deeper, and lighter doping there means a wider depletion region and a smaller capacitance; Sze & Ng say the same of retrograde channel profiles (§6.5.1).

6 · Process integration: the chained implant

The notes close the subject with "Process Integration issues involving the use of Retrograde wells" (pp. 72–74): the resist that masks a high-energy implant (§6.1), the doses DRAM wells need and the order in which the implants are done (§6.2), the recipe itself (§6.3), and where each implant's dopant ends up, in the active areas and beneath the field oxide (§6.4).

6.1 Thick resist

"The process requires a thick photo-resist as mask material 2.0–2.5 µm and special exposure and development techniques are required" (p. 72); on p. 74, "Thick PR (2.0–2.5 µm) must be used as mask for high energy implant." Any layer thick enough to stop the implanted ions can mask an implant (Plummer p. 454), and photoresist can because implantation is done at room temperature. But the resist must hold the ions over their whole range and straggle, and the high-energy implants travel a long way in it: a polymer of about half silicon's density stops ions over a longer distance than silicon does. A common design rule, used in Coherent's SRIM-based charts, makes the resist at least the projected range plus five straggles in the resist (beyond the notes):

tresist ≥ Rp,resist + 5 ΔRp,resist
(8.13)
tresist minimum resist thickness (µm) · Rp,resist, ΔRp,resist projected range and straggle of the ion in the resist (µm), which exceed their values in silicon. At five straggles beyond the range the Gaussian has fallen to e−12.5 ≈ 4×10⁻⁶ of its peak, so the dopant that reaches the silicon beneath is negligible against any background. A rule of thumb; thicker resist may be needed at high doses, which shrink the resist.

The "special exposure and development techniques" follow from the thickness: the whole depth of the resist must be exposed and developed with straight walls, and Plummer notes that such resist is often not hard-baked, to avoid flow and loss of the sidewall angle (p. 468). 6.2 DRAM wells, and the order of the chain

"Retrograde well implants used for DRAM devices require higher doses than those used in logic devices" (p. 73). Wolf gives the reason: better suppression of minority carriers, which lowers the soft-error rate (a DRAM cell holds its datum as a small stored charge, M07b §2). The higher dose in turn needs a higher energy, to keep its tail out of the channel near the surface.

The notes continue: "The high energy implant step used to form retrograde well in deep sub-micron CMOS is performed together with VT adjust, punchthrough and channel stop isolation implants. All the implants are done in the same implanter. Highest energy implantation is done first and low energy implantation is done last." A chained implant is that sequence: one resist mask per well, and every implant for that well done through it in one load of the implanter, so the well-formation sequence looks to the factory like a single implant step, which Wolf reports a SEMATECH comparison found to cut cycle time and cost. Such an implanter, Wolf adds, must cover, for example, up to about 1 MeV for an n-well down to about 10 keV for an n-channel threshold implant (p. 187); the recipe of §6.3 spans 600 to 30 keV.

The order has a reason, which Wolf gives and the notes do not: doing the highest-energy implant first "ensures that no ions traverse the end-of-range (EOR) damage of a previous implant" (p. 186). End-of-range damage is the lattice disorder an implant leaves near the end of its ions' paths, where they have slowed enough to displace silicon atoms (§4.3). A shallower implant done afterwards stops above that damage and never has to cross it.

6.4 Where each implant's dopant ends up

Each implant of the chain is meant to put its peak at a particular depth, and the recipe's energies place them there: the well implants at 0.73–0.79 µm, the punch-through implants at 0.20 µm (phosphorus) and 0.45 µm (boron), below the channel, at and beneath the depth of the source and drain junctions, and the threshold implants at about 0.1 µm, within reach of the gate. Summed, they give a profile with maxima and minima at different depths (Figure 15). The notes define the term with the p. 74 sketch: "Modulated characteristics means that the doping concentration profile has several maxima and minima."

The same implants also pass through the field oxide where it covers the well, and they lose part of their range in it. To first order the oxide stops ions as silicon does, so beneath an oxide t thick each profile sits about t shallower in the silicon (M03 §9.2). SRIM-2013's ranges in oxide refine this: boron goes about 10% farther in SiO₂ than in silicon, and phosphorus up to about 6% farther, so 0.3 µm of field oxide does the work of 0.27–0.28 µm of silicon for the boron implants and 0.28–0.30 µm for the phosphorus. Under 0.3 µm of field oxide, inside the notes' 200–400 nm, the threshold implants stop in the oxide; the 150 keV boron peaks 0.18 µm below the silicon surface and brings it to about 4×10¹⁶ cm⁻³; the 150 keV phosphorus peaks inside the oxide, and its deeper tail, with the well implant's, brings the n-well's surface beneath to about 8×10¹⁶ cm⁻³; and the well implants peak 0.44–0.51 µm below the silicon surface there (Figure 15). That is how one implant serves "punch-through prevention and intra field isolation (channel stop)": under the active areas it lies below the channel, and under the field oxide the same dose lies at the surface and raises the field threshold. By the §3.1 calculation, a p-type surface at 4×10¹⁶ cm⁻³ under 0.3 µm of oxide has a field threshold of about 7 V. These are estimates: the surface sits on the steep side of the 150 keV profile, 1.7 straggles above its peak, where small changes in range matter — taking the oxide to stop boron exactly as silicon does gives 5.5×10¹⁶ cm⁻³ and 8.8 V — and boron's real shallow-side tail, heavier than the Gaussian's (§4.1), raises the surface value. Either way the field threshold lies above the 5 V supply. Plummer makes the same point for well implants made through the field oxide (p. 61), and it is why the notes' sketch on p. 74 draws the well bottom stepped up under each field-oxide island (Figure 10(b)).

"Implant through the oxide so the peak is at the interface" belongs to the threshold implant

In the middle of the recipe Lecture 16 said that the implant is done through the oxide so that its peak sits at the interface, avoiding two junctions. That is Lecture 3's reason for the screen oxide under a shallow threshold implant (M03 §9.2), and it is not the purpose of the well implant, whose peak is meant to be deep. The two-junction question does arise for the n-well, in a different way. A 600 keV phosphorus implant alone, into the 10¹⁵ cm⁻³ p-type epitaxial layer, meets M03's condition (3.10) for a second junction: Rp = 0.73 µm exceeds ΔRp√(2 ln(Cp/NB)) = 0.144 × 3.67 = 0.53 µm, so on the Gaussian it would leave p-type silicon above a buried n layer. That conclusion rests on the Gaussian's shallow tail, which a real profile does not follow (§4.1), so whether such a layer would survive is not settled by this arithmetic. In the chain, the 150 keV and 70 keV phosphorus implants fill the top of the well with donors (Figure 15(a)), and the well is n-type from the surface down to one junction at its bottom.

7 · The modulated well on shallow trench isolation

Beside the LOCOS flow of Figure 10(b) the notes draw a second sequence, the "Modulated n-well Structure for advanced Devices" (p. 74, right column; Wolf Fig. 5-5). Its isolation is shallow trench isolation: trenches etched into the silicon, filled with deposited oxide and polished flat, so there is no bird's beak and no step in the surface (M02 §9; the full process is M15). The sequence has three steps, in Wolf's words: shallow trench formation; modulated n-well implants and VTp adjust, through a resist mask over the p-well area ("n-well photo" in the notes' sketch); and modulated p-well implants and VTn adjust, through a resist mask over the n-well area. VTp and VTn are the threshold voltages of the p-channel and n-channel transistors.

The principle is that of §4.4: the isolation comes first, and each well is then made by a chain of implants through one resist mask, ending with the implant that sets its transistors' threshold. The trench oxide, like the LOCOS oxide of §6.4, absorbs part of each implant's range where it covers the silicon, and Wolf notes that high-energy implanters place dopant beneath a trench isolation oxide as they do beneath a LOCOS one. "Modulated", in the notes' definition, describes the result: "the doping concentration profile has several maxima and minima" — the profiles of Figure 15. The p. 74 sketch ends with the condition that governs both sequences: "Thick PR (2.0–2.5 µm) must be used as mask for high energy implant" (§6.1).

Summary

  • Twin-well CMOS: each well is implanted separately into a lightly doped substrate — a lightly doped p-type epitaxial layer on a p⁺ substrate — so the body doping of each transistor type is tailored separately. The well contacts tie the p-well to ground and the n-well to VDD.
  • The diffused well: implant at moderate energy (50–100 keV), then drive in for 12–24 h above 1100 °C; the wells are 3–4 µm deep and their doping is highest at the surface, falling monotonically. The drive-in conserves the dose, and depth grows as √(Dt): xj = 2√(Dt ln(Cs/CB)), Cs = Q/√(πDt). Plummer's well: 3 µm in 6.8 h at 1100 °C from 4.3×10¹³ cm⁻².
  • The well was deep to prevent vertical punch-through from the drain to the substrate: the drain's and the well bottom's depletion regions must not meet. With 5 V on both junctions, a conservative bound (constructed example), the well junction must lie 1.14 µm below the drain junction at 10¹⁶ cm⁻³ and 0.31 µm at 10¹⁷ cm⁻³. Depth and doping both decide it.
  • The notes' five problems below 0.5 µm: lateral diffusion, about 0.7 times the depth (3–4 µm deep, 2–3 µm sideways; aspect ratio at most about 1.6:1); boron from the p-well segregating into the LOCOS field oxide, needing a separate channel-stop implant before field oxidation; that channel stop diffusing into the active regions (junction capacitance, turn-off); the long drive-in (cycle time, warpage, bow, flatness); and a profile that can only compromise between threshold, hot carriers, punch-through, soft errors and latch-up.
  • Any line over the field oxide is the gate of a field transistor, whose threshold (M03's equation with CFOX) must exceed the supply. Under 0.5 µm of oxide (constructed example): 4.4 V at 10¹⁶ cm⁻³, 2.3 V with half the boron gone, 14 V with a 5×10¹⁶ cm⁻³ channel stop. Segregation coefficient m = CSi/CSiO₂: about 0.3 for boron, which the oxide takes, about 10 for phosphorus, arsenic and antimony, which pile up.
  • The retrograde well: a high-energy implant (300 keV–1 MeV) puts the peak deep — about 1 µm (Wolf); 0.79 µm for boron at 300 keV and 0.73 µm for phosphorus at 600 keV. Its lateral spread after all processing is about 1 µm, against at least 2 µm for a diffused well (Wolf). The notes' lateral straggle at 500 keV is ~0.7 µm (Wolf's figure), about four times SRIM's standard deviation of 0.18 µm. Only a short, low-temperature activation anneal follows; σ² = ΔRp² + 2Dt, and the peak stays at Rp. No long drive-in.
  • High-energy wells are implanted after the LOCOS field oxide: no segregation, less spread. The n⁺-to-p⁺ spacing of a 1.2 µm technology falls from 7–9 µm to 5 µm (29–44%); the chained process reports 40%.
  • Well engineering: control of the doping at every depth, which optimises device speed, carrier mobility and S/D capacitance, inter-device leakage, threshold control, punch-through suppression, parasitic transistor effects, and soft-error and latch-up resistance.
  • Latch-up: a vertical pnp (p⁺ source, n-well, substrate) and a lateral npn (n⁺ source, p-well, n-well), cross-coupled into a thyristor; about 0.7 V across Rwell or Rsub triggers it; with no shunting resistance it holds once αnpn + αpnp ≥ 1, and low well and substrate resistances raise the gain needed. The other advantages: a retarding field that lowers the pnp's gain; less vertical punch-through without raising the surface doping; a conductive well bottom; shallower drains for the parasitic n⁺-to-n-well FETs; and lower S/D-to-body capacitance, Cj = εSi/W.
  • The chained recipe: n-well phosphorus 600 keV 3×10¹³ (well), 150 keV 4×10¹² (punch-through and channel stop), 70 keV 6×10¹² (threshold); p-well boron 300 keV 3×10¹³, 150 keV 4×10¹², 30 keV 5.5×10¹² cm⁻². All in one implanter, highest energy first, so no ion crosses an earlier implant's end-of-range damage; resist 2.0–2.5 µm thick; DRAM wells take higher doses. The summed profile is modulated: several maxima and minima.
  • Three implants called punch-through: Chapter 1's blanket implant (no mask, after the threshold implant), Chapter 5's chained 150 keV stopper (well mask, before the threshold implant), and the halo or pocket (after the gate, self-aligned to it).
