Module 05b

Defects and Charge at the Si/SiO₂ Interface

The four kinds of charge in a gate oxide and at its interface with silicon, each traced to the atomic configuration that holds it — the dangling bond, the stretched and broken bond, the oxygen vacancy, the bonded impurity and the mobile ion — together with the anneals that remove what can be removed.

Prerequisites M03, M05a Read time ~55 min Sources Class notes pp. 23–29 (Chapter 3); Lectures 6–8; Wolf Vol. 4 §3.2

The threshold-voltage equation of M03 carries every charge that lies between the gate and the channel in a single term, Qi/Cox. Physically that term is the sum of four populations, distinguished by where the charge sits, what produced it and whether it can move: fixed oxide charge, interface trapped charge, oxide trapped charge and mobile ionic charge. Each is a particular atom in a particular bonding state. M05a built the vocabulary this module needs — the covalent bond, the silicon crystal and its surface, the SiO₄ tetrahedron, bridging and non-bridging oxygen, the transition region at the interface — and this module uses it to draw each of those states and to follow what each one does to a transistor.

The order follows the charges. Section 1 places all four and says which two remain a problem. Section 2 treats fixed charge. Section 3 is the longest, because the dangling bond is the origin of interface traps and the subject of the drawings on pp. 24–26 of the notes: what a dangling bond is electronically, how many survive oxidation and how many are too many, the other interface defects and where each puts its energy level, and what interface traps do to a device. Section 4 is the remedy — hydrogen, and the alternatives to it — and the hot-carrier mechanism that undoes it. Section 5 turns to the oxide bulk: strained and broken bonds, the oxygen vacancy, the processes that create them and the anneal that removes them. Section 6 is mobile ions. Section 7 maps every configuration back onto its charge class and hands on to M06, which follows what current through the oxide does to these same sites.

1 · The four charges

This section names the four charges, places each one, and records which two still limit devices. The notes (p. 23) give each charge in two counting units: a charge per unit area, in C/cm², written Q, and a number per unit area, in cm⁻², written N. The two are related by the electronic charge, Q = qN with q = 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ C, which is the step M03 §3 applies when it multiplies the quoted interface-charge density by q. Interface traps carry a third unit, a number per unit area per unit energy, for a reason §3.5 explains.

Qi = Qf + Qit + Qot + Qm
(5b.1)
Qi total interface charge per unit area in the threshold equation (C/cm²) · Qf fixed oxide charge (C/cm²), counted as Nf (cm⁻²) · Qit interface trapped charge (C/cm²), counted as Nit (cm⁻²) or as Dit (cm⁻² eV⁻¹) · Qot oxide trapped charge (C/cm²), counted as Not (cm⁻²) · Qm mobile ionic charge (C/cm²), counted as Nm (cm⁻²). Each is an areal density, positive for positive charge. The sum enters the threshold voltage without weighting only for charge at the interface; charge deeper in the oxide counts for less, as equation (5b.2) states.

Position is the classification. The transition region the notes mark between the two materials is the layer, no more than one or two atomic distances thick (Plummer p. 353), in which silicon is incompletely oxidised: bonded to one, two or three oxygen atoms instead of four. Each oxygen is shared between two silicons, so those three states correspond to SiOx with x = 0.5, 1 and 1.5; Sze & Ng (p. 213) describe the layer as a monolayer of incompletely oxidised SiOx. M05a §5 draws those suboxide silicons; they are where fixed charge lives.

The four do not need the same attention. The notes pair them: interface traps and oxide traps are the ongoing reliability problem, mobile and fixed charge are not. The sentence that makes the pairing, however, does not say what its source says, and the difference reverses its meaning.

The notes' sentence on Qm and Qf reverses its source

The notes (p. 23) read: "Reliability involves Qit and Qot and are ongoing. Qm and Qf are not well controlled. Their impact does not get any more severe as oxides are scaled." The passage they condense, Wolf Vol. 4 pp. 82–83, says the problems caused by Qm and Qf "are not only well controlled; but their impact does not get any more severe as oxides are scaled", and calls those of Qit and Qot "ongoing, scaling-dependent problems". Dropping "only" turned "well controlled" into "not well controlled".

The physics is Wolf's. Mobile ions were brought under control by attention to cleanliness in wafer fabrication and had largely ceased to be a problem by the 1970s (Plummer p. 295), and fixed charge is brought to its minimum by a high-temperature anneal (§2). The amount of each is fixed during manufacture: mobile ions still drift under bias in service, which is the instability of §6, but their number does not grow. The threshold shift each can produce is its charge divided by Cox, so the shift shrinks in proportion to tox as the oxide thins (Plummer p. 295 makes the point for mobile ions). Interface and oxide traps are generated again in service, by hot carriers and by current through the oxide, and Wolf (p. 90) records that damage from high oxide fields grew more serious as oxides were thinned. The notes' wording is quoted above as written; Wolf's is the one the physics supports.

2 · Fixed oxide charge, Qf

Fixed oxide charge is the simplest of the four: positive, immobile, and set by the last high-temperature step, the end of oxidation or a later inert anneal, then frozen in as the wafer cools. The notes give it only a name, a unit and a place in the p. 23 drawing; its properties come from the lecture and the textbooks.

It is a sheet of positive charge in the oxide very close to the interface — within about 2 nm of it, and perhaps closer — associated with the transition from silicon to SiO₂ (Plummer p. 294). Its charge state does not change during normal device operation, which is what "fixed" means. Its density is not greatly affected by the oxide thickness or by the type or concentration of dopant in the silicon, but it depends on the oxidation and annealing conditions and on the orientation of the silicon surface (Sze & Ng p. 223).

Its origin is the oxidation reaction. The lecture (L6) puts it directly: as the surface oxidises, most of the silicon atoms become bonded to oxygen, some do not, and fixed charge comes from those silicon atoms left short of oxygen. Two atomic models are in the literature (Sze & Ng p. 223): excess silicon — a trivalent silicon atom near the interface that has lost the electron from its unbonded orbital — and an excess-oxygen centre, a non-bridging oxygen near the interface that has lost an electron. Plummer (Fig. 6-39) draws the first, as an excess Si⁺.

The trivalent silicon of the first model is also the defect §3 identifies with interface traps, so the two charges must differ in some structural detail that neither textbook settles. The two suggestions Plummer records (p. 353) are that the trivalent silicons responsible for fixed charge sit further from the interface, too far to exchange carriers with the silicon, or that their levels lie outside the silicon band gap, where the Fermi level never reaches them. A process that gives a high fixed charge nearly always gives a high interface-trap density too, which suggests a common origin (Plummer p. 354).

Because the fixed charge is set by the last moments at temperature, it is controlled at that point. The value is "frozen in" when the wafer is pulled from the furnace, and it depends on the final temperature and ambient; a high-temperature anneal in an inert ambient toward the end of the high-temperature processing brings it to its minimum (Plummer pp. 296, 354–355). Argon is preferred to nitrogen for that anneal, because in nitrogen the fixed charge falls to its minimum and then rises again with time. Values on (100) silicon are about a third of those on (111) (Plummer Fig. 6-40), a difference §3.6 returns to. The hydrogen anneal of §4 leaves fixed charge unchanged (Plummer p. 356).

3 · The dangling bond and interface trapped charge, Qit

Interface trapped charge, in the notes' definition (p. 23), is charge localised at sites at the silicon surface. Those sites arise from energy states in the forbidden gap of the silicon — extra allowed states present at the surface but not in the bulk. Nearly all of this section follows from one configuration: a silicon atom at the interface with one bond tied to nothing. The subsections take that atom from its electronic structure to its consequences for a transistor.

3.1 What a dangling bond is

Inside a silicon crystal each atom forms four sp3 hybrid orbitals pointing to the corners of a tetrahedron, and each hybrid shares an electron pair with one neighbour (M05a §1–2). Two overlapping hybrids combine into a bonding level, lower in energy, and an antibonding level, higher. In the crystal the bonding levels of all the atoms broaden into the valence band and the antibonding levels into the conduction band, and the 1.12 eV gap between them is the band gap.

A silicon atom that has lost one neighbour keeps three bonds and one hybrid that overlaps nothing. That orbital is the dangling bond. It holds one electron — each silicon hybrid brings one electron to its bond, and this one has no partner to pair it with — and because nothing overlaps it, it is neither bonding nor antibonding: its level lies between the two, in the gap. The atom is called a trivalent silicon atom, since it has three bonds instead of four (notes p. 24). The notes describe the result as the physics was first described, by Tamm in 1932 (Wolf p. 83): a crystal terminated at a surface acquires new electronic states, each belonging to a single surface atom, and an electron in one is localised — held in a small region centred on that atom. The notes' reason is that the field of the crystal at the surface is one-sided: the electrons there are bonded only from the side facing the bulk.

A level in the gap that can hold zero, one or two electrons gives the dangling bond three charge states. Empty, it is positive; with its own electron it is neutral; with a second electron it is negative. It is therefore amphoteric: with the Fermi level low in the gap it gives up its electron and acts as a donor, becoming positive; with the Fermi level high it accepts a second electron and acts as an acceptor, becoming negative; near midgap it stays neutral and keeps its single, unpaired electron (Lenahan & Conley 1998; the donor and acceptor definitions for interface traps are Sze & Ng p. 214). The two changes of charge happen at different Fermi-level positions although only one orbital is involved. A second electron entering an orbital that already holds one is repelled by it, so adding the second electron costs more energy than adding the first; the transition from 0 to − therefore lies above the transition from + to 0, separated by that extra repulsion, the correlation energy (Lenahan & Conley 1998). The dangling bond's charge therefore follows the Fermi level at the surface, and the gate voltage moves that Fermi level. That is the physical content of "interface trapped charge": a charge that exchanges carriers with the silicon and changes with bias (Sze & Ng p. 213).

3.2 From a bare surface to an oxidised one

On a bare silicon surface every surface atom carries at least one dangling bond. On an ideal (111) surface each surface atom has one and is trivalent; on an ideal (100) surface each has two, though a clean (100) surface reconstructs, neighbouring atoms pairing into dimers that leave one dangling bond apiece (Chadi 1979). Measurements on clean surfaces in ultra-high vacuum find interface-trap densities of the order of the surface atom density, about 10¹⁵ cm⁻² (Sze & Ng p. 214). Thermal oxidation removes almost all of them. Each surface silicon bonds to an oxygen, the oxygen bridges to a silicon of the growing oxide, and that silicon completes its own tetrahedron with three more oxygens. Along a perfect interface every surface bond would end on an oxygen in this way (notes p. 24, drawing b). But oxidation does not tie up all the bonds: a small fraction is left unsatisfied, and a significant number of surface states can remain (notes p. 25).

The lecture (L7) gives the reason as incomplete and abrupt oxidation. Oxidation is run for a set time and then stopped by withdrawing the wafer from the furnace, and in the lecture's account any surface silicon that has not yet bonded to oxygen at that moment keeps its dangling bond.

The literature traces the dangling bond to strain, not to the moment growth stops

The oxidation front moves through the silicon continuously, so the interface has the same structure whenever growth is stopped; there is no population of surface atoms that the oxygen has not yet reached. The literature treats the surviving dangling bond as a defect built into the interface by the mismatch between the silicon lattice and the oxide network and by the stress that mismatch leaves in the oxide. On oxidised (111) silicon the measured density of these dangling bonds is about 10¹³ cm⁻², roughly constant for oxidation between 300 and 800 °C and falling above that range as the interface relaxes structurally, and it tracks the stress in the oxide (Stesmans 1993). The count is therefore set by the oxidation conditions, temperature above all. The lecture's account is given above as it was taught; the strain account is the one §3.6 relies on.

The surviving interfacial dangling bond has a name, because it has been identified directly. An unpaired electron has a magnetic moment, and in a magnetic field its two spin orientations have different energies; microwaves are absorbed when their photon energy matches that splitting, at a field that depends on the atom the electron sits on and on that atom's neighbours. This is electron spin resonance (ESR, also called electron paramagnetic resonance): the spectrum identifies a defect's structure and its intensity counts it. The neutral dangling bond, with one unpaired electron, shows up in it; the empty and doubly occupied states, having no unpaired electron, do not. ESR identifies the interface trap as the Pb centre, a silicon atom at the interface back-bonded to three silicon atoms with an unpaired electron in the fourth orbital, written •Si≡Si3. On a (111) interface there is one variant, called Pb; on (100) there are two, Pb0 and Pb1 (Lenahan & Conley 1998). The same centre is the interface trap of the radiation-effects course, EEE 5416 M07.

On dry-oxidised (111) silicon the Pb centre produces two peaks in the interface-trap density, at Ev + 0.26 eV and Ev + 0.84 eV (Poindexter et al. 1984). They are the two transitions of the amphoteric bond of §3.1: the lower is where it changes between positive and neutral, the upper where it changes between neutral and negative, 0.58 eV higher. Midgap lies at Ev + 0.56 eV, so the levels sit about 0.3 eV either side of it, each as close to its nearer band edge as to midgap. The notes compress this into the statement that an interfacial trivalent silicon atom introduces "a deep trap level near the Si midgap" (p. 26); what spans midgap is the range between the two levels, over which the bond is neutral.

3.3 How many is too many

The notes make the fraction quantitative (p. 25), and the result shows how small a fraction is still too many. The calculation needs the threshold shift a sheet of charge produces, which is the Qi/Cox term of the threshold equation written as a change.

ΔVT = − QoxCox

"Effective" means weighted by position: a charge at distance x from the gate in an oxide of thickness tox contributes only x/tox of the shift it would produce at the silicon interface (Sze & Ng p. 223). The dangling-bond threshold shift

A (100) silicon surface has 6.8×10¹⁴ silicon atoms per cm² (notes p. 25; Sze & Ng p. 221, Table 1, gives the same).

Suppose oxidation leaves one in a thousand dangling, and each gives one surface state holding one electronic charge. The interface trapped charge density is Nit = 6.8×10¹⁴ cm⁻² / 1000 = 6.8×10¹¹ cm⁻².

As charge per area: Qit = qNit = 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ C × 6.8×10¹¹ cm⁻² = 1.09×10⁻⁷ C/cm².

For a 20 nm gate oxide, tox = 20 nm = 200 Å = 2×10⁻⁶ cm, and Cox = 3.9 × 8.85×10⁻¹⁴ F/cm / 2×10⁻⁶ cm = 1.73×10⁻⁷ F/cm².

The magnitude of the shift is Qit/Cox = 1.09×10⁻⁷ C/cm² / 1.73×10⁻⁷ F/cm².

|ΔVT| = 0.63 V

A defect rate of one in a thousand — an interface 99.9% perfect — moves the threshold by 0.63 V. The notes apply the fraction to the atom count, which is the same as taking one dangling bond per surface atom, as the square grid of Figure 5 shows. An ideal (100) surface atom has two outward bonds, 1.36×10¹⁵ bonds/cm² (M05a §2), so the same fraction of bonds would double the density; the calculation above is the notes' version.

The lecture (L7) sets the target one decade lower: one in 10⁴, which gives 6.8×10¹⁰ cm⁻² and |ΔVT| = 0.063 V. After a hydrogen anneal the density can be as low as 10¹⁰ cm⁻², about one trap per 10⁵ surface atoms (Sze & Ng p. 214).

Oxidation leaves one in 2000 of the (100) surface atoms with a dangling bond, under a 5 nm gate oxide. What is the threshold shift, and what does the comparison with 0.63 V show?

Nit = 6.8×10¹⁴ / 2000 = 3.4×10¹¹ cm⁻², so Qit = 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ × 3.4×10¹¹ = 5.44×10⁻⁸ C/cm². Cox = 3.9 × 8.85×10⁻¹⁴ / 5×10⁻⁷ = 6.90×10⁻⁷ F/cm². |ΔVT| = 5.44×10⁻⁸ / 6.90×10⁻⁷ = 0.079 V.

Half the trap density and a quarter of the thickness give one-eighth of the shift. For a fixed density of interface charge, ΔVT is proportional to tox, because Cox is inversely proportional to it — which is why the same trap density matters less in a thin oxide than in the 20 nm film of the notes' example.

3.4 The other interface defects

Dangling bonds are not the only source. Crystal defects near the surface and foreign atoms bonded at the surface are other interface perturbations identified as sources of surface states (notes p. 25). The notes' drawings on pp. 25–26 follow Wolf's Figure 3-7, which collects the bond-stretching and broken-bond models of the interface and the oxide bulk (Wolf p. 85). Figure 6 redraws them on one strip.

A Si–Si stretched bond is what remains where the bridging oxygen between a substrate silicon and an oxide silicon is missing — an oxygen vacancy — and the two silicons bond to each other directly. The name compares the bond with an ordinary Si–Si bond in crystalline silicon, 2.35 Å long, and the notes draw it as a long, weak link. Compared with the positions the two atoms held while the oxygen bridged them, 3.12 Å apart (notes p. 22), they have moved toward each other. Calculations for vacancies in bulk amorphous SiO₂, where both silicons belong to the oxide, place the relaxed Si–Si distance near 2.5 Å, typically longer than the crystal bond, spread between about 2.3 and 2.7 Å from site to site (Mukhopadhyay et al. 2004). At the interface one of the two silicons is held by the crystal, so those values give the scale, not a measured interfacial distance. The surrounding network resists the contraction, which leaves the bond strained and weak. The lecture (L7) states the consequence: these bonds are very weak and trap holes.

A Si–O stretched bond has its oxygen present and bonded but displaced from the position the network wants, further from the silicon than its equilibrium distance. Nothing is missing and nothing is broken; the bond is longer and weaker (L7: the oxygen "is not missing but is stretched from the atomic position"). An impurity at the interface is a foreign atom bonded to a surface silicon in place of the bridging oxygen — mostly hydrogen or a hydroxyl group (notes p. 26).

Where a defect sits decides what kind of level it produces. The notes (p. 26, after Wolf p. 85) give the assignments, collected here with the defect names above.

The last row and §4 describe different levels, and the difference matters. A hydrogen atom on an interfacial silicon removes that silicon's level from the silicon band gap, so the site stops exchanging charge with the channel; that is why the hydrogen anneal of §4 works (Plummer p. 356). The electron trap the notes assign to an impurity-tied bond is a site that captures electrons travelling in the oxide — hot electrons injected from the channel, or tunnelling current — and Wolf presents the assignment as a model ("is thought to", p. 85). Hydroxyl and water in the oxide are the documented case: as-grown wet oxides contain some oxide traps because they contain OH⁻ and H₂O (Wolf p. 90).

3.5 Where each defect puts its level

The table's two columns are two band gaps. The silicon gap, 1.12 eV wide, lies inside the SiO₂ gap of about 9 eV: the oxide's conduction band is 3.1 eV above the silicon's, and its valence band 4.6 eV below the silicon's (Taur & Ning 3/E Fig. 4.2; M06 §1.1). A defect at the interface with a level inside the silicon gap can exchange carriers with the silicon, which makes it an interface trap. A defect in the oxide bulk has its level somewhere in the much wider oxide gap, and it charges only when carriers enter the oxide, which makes it an oxide trap. A deep level lies far from both band edges and holds a captured carrier; a shallow level lies close to one and releases the carrier to that band quickly.

In the notes' account, stretched bonds at the interface give a continuum of deep levels (p. 26), not a single one, because no two stretched bonds in an amorphous network have quite the same length and angle, and each distortion shifts the level a little. Interface-trap levels are so closely spaced that they cannot be told apart and appear as a continuum over the band gap (Sze & Ng p. 221). That is why interface traps are counted per unit energy.

Theory and measurement put the stretched-bond levels away from midgap

The notes and Wolf (p. 85) call the interface continuum deep. The calculation behind Wolf's Figure 3-7 places the levels differently: a silicon dangling bond gives states near midgap, while oxygen vacancies and weak Si–Si bonds give states above midgap and weak Si–O bonds states below it (Sakurai & Sugano 1981). Measured interface-trap densities have the matching shape: Dit is roughly constant near midgap and rises toward both band edges (Sze & Ng p. 220), a distribution usually called U-shaped. On that reading the Pb centre supplies the states near midgap and the strained bonds the states toward the band edges. Both accounts agree that the spread of bond distortions makes the distribution a continuum; the notes' word for its depth is kept above.

Dit = 1q dQitdE
(5b.3)
Dit interface-trap density per unit area per unit energy (cm⁻² eV⁻¹) · q electronic charge, 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ C · Qit interface trapped charge (C/cm²) · E energy of the trap level in the gap (eV). The number of traps whose levels lie in an energy range is the integral of Dit over it, which gives Nit in cm⁻². Measured by how Qit changes as the gate moves the Fermi level through the gap (Sze & Ng Eq. 38).

Midgap is the worst place for a trap because of how traps emit. How readily a trap captures a carrier depends on its capture cross-section and on how many carriers are available, and is independent of where its level sits. What depends steeply on the level is how quickly a captured carrier is thermally re-emitted to the band it came from. A shallow trap captures an electron and returns it to the conduction band long before a hole arrives, so no recombination is completed; a midgap trap holds the electron long enough for a hole to be captured too. Minimising re-emission is what makes a midgap level the most efficient generation–recombination centre.

3.6 Why (100) silicon

The notes (p. 28) give two measures that minimise the trap density: use (100)-oriented silicon, and anneal in hydrogen at the end of the fabrication process. A (100) plane carries the fewest silicon atoms per unit area of the common orientations — 6.8×10¹⁴ cm⁻² against 7.8×10¹⁴ for (111) — yet each atom on an ideal (100) surface carries two outward bonds where a (111) atom carries one, so (100) presents the larger density of bonds to leave unsatisfied. It is nonetheless the low-defect plane: its fixed charge is about a third of that on (111), in the order (111) > (110) > (100) (Deal et al. 1967), its interface-trap density is about an order of magnitude lower (Sze & Ng p. 220), and fewer Pb centres survive oxidation on (100) than on (111) (Poindexter et al. 1981). The ordering follows the defects the oxidised interface keeps — a question about the chemistry and strain of that interface — not the density of bonds available beforehand.

Sze & Ng (p. 221) count differently and reach the same ordering by another route. They tabulate the bonds available to react during oxidation — 11.8×10¹⁴ cm⁻² on (111) against 6.8×10¹⁴ on (100) — and argue that fewer available bonds means slower oxidation, less excess silicon left at the interface, and therefore fewer interface traps. On either account (100) is the orientation for MOS devices — all modern MOSFETs are built on it (Sze & Ng p. 221) — and every wafer in M04 is specified as (100).

3.7 What interface traps do

The notes (p. 27) list four effects of interface traps on a device. The first three follow from a level in the gap whose charge follows the Fermi level; the fourth is an observed association.

  1. They change the surface potential. The charge in the interface traps interacts with the charge in the silicon near the surface, changing the silicon charge distribution and therefore the surface potential, the quantity that defines threshold.
  2. They act as generation–recombination centres, giving rise to leakage currents. A midgap level offers a two-step path across the gap, far more probable than the single step. The effect was large enough to shape the technology: in 1958 a Bell Laboratories group led by Atalla found that growing a thin oxide where a pn junction meets the surface cut the junction leakage by a factor of 10 to 100 (Wolf p. 88).
  3. They add a capacitance. Because the trapped charge changes with surface potential, charge flows into and out of the traps as the gate voltage changes, and that is a capacitance, Cit.
  4. They weaken the oxide. The notes state that an increase in interface-state density weakens the oxide, causing the second stage of breakdown in a gate oxide (M06 §5). Their source is more guarded: Wolf (p. 87) reports that the increase "has been correlated to weakening of the oxide", which appears to be a prelude to second-stage breakdown. The link is a measured correlation, not an established cause.

Which capacitance Cit is in parallel with

The notes (p. 27) place the trap capacitance "in parallel with the silicon gate capacitance", after Wolf p. 87; Lecture 7 said "parallel to Cox". The silicon capacitance here is the depletion capacitance of the silicon, CD. The interface traps sit at the silicon surface and charge in step with it, so Cit is in parallel with CD, and that pair is in series with Cox (Sze & Ng Fig. 14). It is not in parallel with Cox. The distinction decides what a measurement shows: in series with Cox, no density of traps can raise the measured capacitance above Cox.

C = Cox (CD + Cit)Cox + CD + Cit ,  Cit = q2Dit
(5b.4)
C measured gate capacitance per unit area, low-frequency limit (F/cm²) · Cox oxide capacitance (F/cm²) · CD depletion capacitance of the silicon (F/cm²) · Cit interface-trap capacitance (F/cm²) · Dit from (5b.3). With Dit per joule, q2Dit is in F/cm²; with Dit in the usual cm⁻² eV⁻¹, Cit = q × Dit numerically, q in coulombs. At high frequency the traps cannot follow and the Cit term drops out (Sze & Ng Eqs. 41–43).

Only traps that fill and empty faster than the measurement signal contribute to Cit; slower traps do not (notes p. 28). Cit is therefore a function of three things: the interface-trap density; the gate voltage, which sets the surface potential and so the occupancy of the traps; and the frequency of the capacitance measurement. The same device measured at two frequencies gives two curves, and the gap between them measures Dit (Sze & Ng p. 216). Together with the stretch-out, that is how interface traps are told apart from the other three charges, which shift the curve without stretching it.

4 · Tying the bond off: hydrogen and other terminators

The dangling bonds oxidation leaves are removed chemically, by giving each one an atom to bond to. The atom used in manufacture is hydrogen. This section states the anneal that supplies it, what the sources say about hydroxyl as an alternative, how hot carriers break the Si–H bond during operation, and how hydrogen compares with the fluorine and nitrogen that later processes introduce.

4.1 The hydrogen anneal

The notes (p. 26) give the process: an anneal in a hydrogen ambient — 100% H₂, or 4% H₂ in N₂, called forming gas — lets hydrogen penetrate the oxide and tie up the remaining dangling bonds that thermal oxidation did not. Wolf §3.2.2 (p. 85) gives the same two ambients, at approximately 450 °C. The lecture (L7) gives the temperature range as 400–450 °C. The mechanism is that molecular hydrogen diffuses through the oxide to the interface, dissociates, and the atomic hydrogen bonds to the trivalent silicon (Plummer p. 356):

H2 → 2H,  ≡Si• + H → ≡Si–H
(5b.5)
≡Si• a trivalent silicon: three bonds (≡) and one unpaired electron (•) · H atomic hydrogen · ≡Si–H the passivated site. Both reactions are reversible (Plummer Eq. 6.61); the second runs backward when the Si–H bond is broken, which is §4.3. Fixed charge is not affected.

A passivated silicon has four satisfied bonds, and once the traps are bonded to hydrogen they are no longer electrically active and no longer trap carriers (Plummer p. 356). The orbital picture of §3.1 gives the reason: the former dangling orbital and the hydrogen's orbital combine, as any two overlapping orbitals do, into a filled bonding level and an empty antibonding level, and these lie below the valence-band edge and above the conduction-band edge, so nothing is left in the gap. That is how the anneal takes the interface from the 6.8×10¹¹ cm⁻² of §3.3 toward 10¹⁰ cm⁻².

The anneal is the last thermal step in the process, done after metallization and before assembly and packaging (Wolf p. 85); it is variously called the post-metallization anneal, the final anneal, the sinter or the alloy anneal, the last because it also alloys the aluminium contacts (Plummer p. 89, which gives 400–450 °C for about 30 minutes). Two things fix its place. The reaction runs readily at temperatures the metal layers tolerate, and if it were done earlier the Si–H bonds would break again at the higher temperatures of diffusions and implant anneals, and the hydrogen would diffuse out (Plummer p. 356). The 450 °C ceiling exists because the aluminium is already on the wafer.

Hydrogen is flammable in air between about 4% and 75% by volume, an unusually wide range, and pure hydrogen at 450 °C demands correspondingly careful handling. Dilution is the remedy. How far a mixture must be diluted depends on the diluent, because the inert gas that escapes with the hydrogen also dilutes the air it mixes with. By the standard method for classifying gas mixtures (ISO 10156), hydrogen in nitrogen stays non-flammable up to about 5.5% H₂, which covers the notes' 4%; argon is the weaker diluent, and in argon the ceiling is about 3.1% H₂, which is why argon mixtures are sold near 3%. The 10% H₂ in N₂ that Plummer gives (pp. 89, 357) lies above the ceiling and is classed as flammable. The lecture nonetheless warned that even 3% hydrogen demands care, and described a 3% anneal whose end cap was blown off; the classification concerns only whether escaping gas can burn in air.

The lecture's forming gas is diluted in argon

Lecture 7 gave the mixture as 3% hydrogen and 97% argon; the notes give 4% H₂ in N₂. By definition forming gas is hydrogen diluted in nitrogen, which is the usual carrier because it is cheap and unreactive with silicon and its oxide at 450 °C. Hydrogen diluted in argon is also sold, at about 3%, and used where nitrogen itself must be excluded. Both compositions are classed non-flammable, the notes' under the nitrogen ceiling of about 5.5% and the lecture's under the argon ceiling of about 3.1%. Nothing in the passivation physics depends on the choice: the carrier dilutes the hydrogen and takes no part in the reaction.

4.2 Hydroxyl

Hydrogen is not the only species that bonds to a trivalent silicon. A hydroxyl group, OH, is also available — the wafer meets water at every clean — and it terminates a dangling bond as Si–OH, the same structure that ends a non-bridging oxygen in the oxide network (M05a §3). The notes do not separate the two species. Their p. 26 sentence, after Wolf p. 85, is that a dangling bond tied up with an impurity, "mostly H or OH", produces an electron trap, and on p. 27 both Si–H and Si–OH bonds are easily broken by hot electrons. Hydrogen's standing as the passivant rests on the separate observation that an interface trap bonded to hydrogen is no longer electrically active (Plummer p. 356); §3.4 reconciles the two statements. The documented electrical effect of hydroxyl lies in the oxide: as-grown wet oxides contain oxide traps because they contain OH⁻ and H₂O (Wolf p. 90).

A student asked in Lecture 7 whether OH has the same passivating effect. The lecture's answer was that it does not, because OH is released with less energy than hydrogen: a small energy frees it, and it arrives from the water used in cleaning.

Which bond is the weaker

Releasing a hydroxyl group from Si–OH breaks a Si–O bond. The mean Si–O single-bond energy is 452 kJ/mol, 4.68 eV per bond, against 318 kJ/mol, 3.30 eV, for Si–H (Huheey; 96.485 kJ/mol = 1 eV per bond). Si–OH is the stronger attachment, not the weaker, so the lecture's reason does not hold as stated. The course's sources give no bond-level account of why hydroxyl is the poorer terminator; what they document is the electron trapping that OH and water bring to the oxide (Wolf p. 90).

4.3 Hot carriers undo the passivation

A hot carrier is an electron or hole that a strong electric field has accelerated to a kinetic energy far above the thermal average; the energy comes from the field, which does work on the carrier between collisions faster than collisions remove it. In a MOSFET this happens where the field is highest, near the drain (M02 §6.1, and §5.3 below). The notes (pp. 26–27) state what hot electrons do to a passivated interface: Si–H or Si–OH bonds can be easily broken by injected hot electrons, giving rise to interface traps. The released hydrogen leaves the dangling bond behind, and reaction (5b.5) has run backward.

Each link follows from something already on this page. More dangling bonds raise Qit; by (5b.2) that shifts VT; and the drain current depends on VG − VT (squared, in saturation), so it falls. The lecture and Wolf (pp. 87–88) describe VT rising, which is the n-channel case: at inversion the surface Fermi level of an n-channel device is high in the gap, where the amphoteric traps of §3.1 take a second electron and become negative. Because interface traps are generated as long as hot carriers reach the interface, Qot is not the only charge that grows in service: Qit does too.

The bond that breaks is the weakest in Figure 11: 3.30 eV for the mean Si–H bond, and for a hydrogen on an interfacial Pb site at a (111) interface a thermal dissociation energy of about 2.83 eV (Stesmans 2000). Hence the notes' conclusion (p. 27): hydrogen introduced in the post-anneal can increase hot-electron degradation, and the ideal gate dielectric should contain as little hydrogen as possible. Read beside §4.1, which needs hydrogen to passivate the interface at all, the statement concerns hydrogen beyond what the interface needs — hydrogen in the oxide bulk and in the films over it, which Wolf (p. 85) traces to the steam reflow of a borophosphosilicate glass and to silicon nitride deposition as well as to the anneal. That hydrogen is the supply for further bond breaking.

The clearest demonstration is isotopic. Annealing in deuterium instead of hydrogen passivates the same bonds, and it reduces hot-electron degradation by more than an order of magnitude (Lyding, Hess & Kizilyalli 1996). The bond is broken by a succession of vibrational excitations, and the explanation offered is that the Si–D bending vibration lies close in frequency to a vibration of the silicon lattice, so it hands its energy to the lattice before enough accumulates to break the bond (Van de Walle & Jackson 1996). Hot carriers are not the only route: in p-channel devices held at negative gate bias and elevated temperature, interfacial Si–H bonds are broken with no hot carriers involved, a mechanism called negative-bias temperature instability (Schroder & Babcock 2003).

4.4 Four terminators compared

A terminator has to satisfy the dangling bond, leave no level in the silicon gap, and hold against hot carriers. Figure 11 sets the four species that appear in this course side by side. The energies are mean single-bond enthalpies from one compilation (Huheey), a comparison of order; the energy needed to break a particular bond at a particular site differs, as the 2.83 eV for interfacial Si–H shows.

Fluorine is the terminator chosen for strength. Fluorine atoms in the gate oxide form Si–F bonds, which are stronger than Si–H bonds, and replace Si–H at the Si/SiO₂ interface, raising the interface's resistance to hot electrons (notes pp. 41–42; Wolf p. 120). The cost is that fluorine in the oxide can increase boron penetration from a p⁺ polysilicon gate, which the notes limit by keeping the implanted fluorine dose below 1×10¹⁴ cm⁻² (p. 42). Nitrogen, with three bonds, can cap a dangling bond and still bond into the network on two sides; a small amount accumulated at the interface reduces interfacial defects by relieving strained Si–O bonds and dangling bonds (notes p. 37; Wolf p. 112). The processes that introduce both are in M06 §9.

5 · Oxide trapped charge, Qot

Oxide trapped charge is due to holes or electrons trapped in the bulk of the oxide, and it can therefore be positive or negative (notes p. 28). The carriers are trapped at trap centres, which are microscopic defects in the oxide structure. Well-grown SiO₂ contains few of them, but they are easily introduced by bombardment with energetic photons or particles, and as-grown wet oxides are the exception, carrying some traps from their OH⁻ and H₂O (Wolf pp. 89–90). This section draws what a trap is, identifies the principal hole trap, lists what creates traps in a process, and states the anneal that removes them.

5.1 What a trap is

The notes (pp. 28–29) name the defects as the products of reactions that "bend or break Si–O bonds in the oxide". A strained bond is one whose length or angle has been forced away from its equilibrium; a broken bond has come apart, leaving an unbonded orbital on each side. Such a site is usually neutral when empty and becomes charged when it captures an electron or a hole that has entered the oxide (Sze & Ng p. 224).

5.2 The oxygen vacancy and the E′ centre

The notes identify the hole trap: an oxygen vacancy, or weak Si–Si stretched bond, produces a hole trap (p. 26). In the oxide bulk the neutral vacancy is two silicons bonded to each other where the bridging oxygen is missing, O₃≡Si–Si≡O₃ (Wolf p. 89) — the bottom-row defect (6) of Figure 6. What happens when a hole arrives has a name the literature uses throughout, and it explains why the charge stays.

Capturing a hole removes one electron from the Si–Si bond. In the model worked out for crystalline SiO₂ (α-quartz) the bond then breaks. One silicon keeps the remaining electron, unpaired, in the orbital that pointed into the vacancy. The other silicon is left positive and bonded to only three oxygens, and it relaxes back into the plane of those three oxygens (Feigl, Fowler & Yip 1974) and through it, until it bonds to an oxygen behind it: the puckered configuration (Rudra & Fowler 1987). Calculations for amorphous SiO₂, taken site by site, find a range of outcomes: some positively charged vacancies keep a weakened Si–Si bond, and of those that break, most stay unpuckered, with the puckered form in the minority (Mukhopadhyay et al. 2004). Either way the result is the E′ centre: positively charged, and paramagnetic because one electron is left unpaired, so the same defect is seen electrically as trapped charge and in ESR as a signal. It is the dominant deep hole trap in thermally grown oxides (Lenahan & Conley 1998; Lenahan, Conley & Wallace 1997).

Because the atoms rearrange around the trapped hole, the charge remains after the stress that put it there is removed. It is lost only slowly — by electrons tunnelling in from the silicon, or when the oxide is heated — which is why a deliberate anneal is needed. Radiation creates the same centres in far larger numbers; EEE 5416 M07 follows their formation and annealing in detail.

5.3 Where the traps come from

The notes (pp. 28–29) and Lecture 8 name the same sources; the list follows the lecture's order.

  1. Microscopic defects formed as the oxide grows. The lecture places these first: trap centres present in the film as grown.
  2. Ionizing radiation from process steps. The notes list three processes that introduce Qot: ion implantation, sputter deposition and plasma etching. The lecture adds plasma-enhanced chemical vapour deposition (PECVD) of oxide and oxynitride films, names the plasma etch as reactive ion etching (RIE), the only etch used at small dimensions, and notes that sputtering, used to deposit metals such as titanium, molybdenum, cobalt, nickel and tungsten, is itself a plasma process. The common cause is energy delivered to the oxide network: a plasma exposes the oxide to energetic ions and electrons (Plummer p. 295) and to ultraviolet photons, and the source/drain implant is performed after the gate oxide exists, often through an oxide layer (Plummer p. 295; L8).
  3. Hot-carrier injection and high current through the oxide. The current may be Fowler–Nordheim tunnelling (M06 §1.2). The lecture locates the injection: the lateral field peaks at the curved edge of the heavily doped n⁺ drain junction, and the heavier channel doping used to keep the channel longer than twice the depletion width, against punch-through, raises it further (M02 §6.1).
  4. Breaking of Si–O bonds under high field. Each silicon is bonded to four oxygens, and the lecture describes those bonds breaking in the field, with the resulting fragments trapping charge.

The sources divide into two kinds. Process damage is created during manufacture and is largely removed before the part is finished; operational damage — hot carriers, tunnelling current, field — accumulates during use. The lecture's illustration is a device that works when it is made and fails after a year because traps have formed in its oxide. Wolf (p. 90) records the shift in emphasis: in early MOSFETs the concern about Qot was ionizing radiation in space, while in deep-submicron devices process damage and high-field degradation made it serious in ordinary parts.

5.4 Removing them: the two anneals

The notes (p. 29) state that Qot can be annealed out by low-temperature treatments (above 550 °C), and that for this reason it is considered relatively less important than the other oxide charges; Wolf p. 90 gives the same temperature and adds that some neutral traps may remain. The lecture gives the ambient as argon or nitrogen. "Low-temperature" is relative to oxidation, which runs at 1000–1100 °C (M01 §7.1); the treatment is hotter than the 400–450 °C hydrogen anneal of §4.1. The notes add that not all Qot may be annealed out by the end of fabrication, and that what remains may cause long-term reliability problems.

Lecture 8 gave about 500 °C

Lecture 8 stated the Qot anneal as argon or nitrogen "at around 500 °C". The notes (p. 29) and Wolf (p. 90) both give above 550 °C, and the page follows them. Annealing of trapped charge is progressive, not a threshold: trapped holes from radiation begin to anneal at room temperature (EEE 5416 M07), and 550 °C is the temperature above which the notes treat Qot as removed.

A treatment above 550 °C cannot follow aluminium metallization, whose ceiling is 450 °C (§4.1), so it belongs before the metal: Plummer (p. 295) states that oxide traps are normally repaired by a high-temperature anneal before device fabrication is complete. Traps created after that point, by metal sputtering, metal etching and dielectric deposition, cannot receive it, which is one reason not all Qot is removed. The two anneals are easily confused; they differ in every respect.

6 · Mobile ionic charge, Qm

Mobile ionic charge — the notes' "mobile charge", Na⁺ and K⁺ — consists of alkali ions that entered the oxide during processing, and it is distinguished from the other three charges by one property: it moves. This section gives the atomic reason it moves, what the motion does, where the ions come from, and how they were brought under control.

An alkali ion is not part of the oxide network. In the terms of M05a §3 it is a network modifier: it sits in the open spaces of the network beside a non-bridging oxygen that carries the extra electron, Si–O⁻. It is bound ionically, by the non-directional attraction to that negative oxygen, not by covalent bonds fixed into the network. Thermally grown SiO₂ is open enough for this — only 43% of its volume is occupied (Wolf p. 82) — and the ion moves by thermally activated hops from one such site to the next. Under a gate voltage it drifts, toward the silicon under positive bias and toward the gate under negative bias, and it moves faster at elevated temperature (Sze & Ng p. 224).

That mobility is what makes the ions intolerable. By (5b.2) the same ion shifts the threshold more the closer it is to the silicon, so a device biased one way for a day has a different threshold from the same device biased the other way; the characteristic drifts with operating history, and in severe cases the C–V curve shows hysteresis when the gate voltage is swept in opposite directions (Sze & Ng p. 224). Snow and co-workers first showed that alkali ions, sodium above all, were responsible for the instability of oxide-passivated devices (Sze & Ng p. 224).

The sources are the chemicals of the process itself (L6). Sodium, potassium and lithium are major impurities in water, in process chemicals and in solvents, and ordinary water also carries iron, magnesium and zinc. Control therefore means controlling the whole chemical supply: low-sodium reagents, high-purity potassium- and lithium-free chemicals, and deionised water. People are a source too — the human body is full of sodium, and breathing over a wafer transfers enough into a film to affect the device, which is one reason engineers and technicians in a fabrication area wear masks. The oxide can also be protected: films impervious to mobile ions, such as silicon nitride and Al₂O₃, serve as sodium barriers, as does phosphosilicate glass (Sze & Ng p. 224); a phosphosilicate-glass layer over the oxide was shown to stabilise oxide-passivated devices against this instability as early as 1964 (Kerr et al. 1964).

The lecture (L6) gives the historical consequence as p-channel thresholds of six to eight volts in the early 1970s and 1980s, with mobile charge as the cause. Two refinements belong beside that. Plummer (p. 295) dates mobile ions as a serious problem of the 1960s that had largely disappeared by the 1970s. And the magnitude of those thresholds did not need mobile ions: oxides of that era were 1000–1500 Å thick (L8), and through a 1000 Å oxide 5×10¹¹ cm⁻² of positive interface charge alone contributes −2.32 V to a p-channel threshold, before the work-function and depletion terms are added (M03 §7.1). Fixed charge, one component of that charge, is about three times larger on (111) silicon than on (100) (Plummer Fig. 6-40; Deal et al. 1967). Mobile ions are held responsible for the instability, the drift under bias and temperature (Sze & Ng p. 224). Following the sign analysis of M03 §6, positive ionic charge drives a p-channel threshold further negative, adding to terms that are already negative.

Two nominally identical MOS capacitors are measured. One shows a threshold that shifts by 0.4 V after a week of positive gate bias at 150 °C and returns after the opposite bias; the other shows a threshold that shifts progressively during electrical stress and does not return, with a C–V curve that becomes stretched. Which charge dominates in each, and which atoms are responsible?

The first is mobile ionic charge, Qm: sodium or potassium ions held beside non-bridging oxygens, drifting through the oxide. The signature is a shift that reverses with bias polarity and is accelerated by temperature; bias–temperature stress is the standard test for exactly this reason.

The second is interface trapped charge, Qit, possibly with some Qot. Electrical stress injects hot carriers, which break interfacial Si–H bonds and restore Pb dangling bonds. The bonds do not re-form when the bias is removed, so the shift is progressive and permanent, and because the new traps follow the surface Fermi level they stretch the C–V curve, which drifting ions do not.

Summary

  • Qi = Qf + Qit + Qot + Qm, each in C/cm² or, divided by q, in cm⁻². Position classifies them: mobile ions anywhere, oxide traps in the bulk, fixed charge in the transition region, interface traps on the interface.
  • The notes say Qm and Qf are "not well controlled"; their source, Wolf, says "not only well controlled", with an impact that does not grow as oxides scale. Qit and Qot are the ongoing reliability problem.
  • Qf is positive, within ~2 nm of the interface, from incompletely oxidised silicon; it is set by the last high-temperature step, its temperature and ambient, and by orientation, and is minimised by a high-temperature argon anneal.
  • A dangling bond is one sp3 orbital with one unpaired electron on a trivalent silicon. Its level lies in the gap; it is amphoteric (+, 0, −). At the interface it is the Pb centre, with transition levels at Ev + 0.26 and + 0.84 eV, about 0.3 eV either side of midgap (the notes: "a deep trap level near the Si midgap"). The lecture attributes it to incomplete, abrupt oxidation; the literature to lattice mismatch and interfacial stress.
  • One in 1000 of the 6.8×10¹⁴ atoms/cm² on (100) gives 6.8×10¹¹ cm⁻² and 0.63 V through 20 nm; one in 10⁴ gives 0.063 V. (100) has about a third of the fixed charge of (111) and an order of magnitude lower interface-trap density, despite more outward bonds per area.
  • Si–Si stretched bond = oxygen vacancy: longer than crystal Si–Si (2.35 Å), shorter than the 3.12 Å it replaced; a hole trap, or in Wolf's word its precursor. Si–O stretched bond: O present, displaced. At the interface the notes give both a continuum of deep levels, hence Dit per eV (theory and the U-shaped Dit put them nearer the band edges); in the bulk, shallow levels.
  • Interface traps shift VT, generate leakage, add Cit in parallel with CD (in series with Cox), stretching the C–V curve, and are correlated with weakening of the oxide (the notes state it as cause).
  • Passivation: 100% H₂ or 4% H₂ in N₂ (notes), 400–450 °C, after metallization, the last thermal step; ≡Si• + H → ≡Si–H. The lecture's 3% H₂ in Ar is an argon-diluted variant; both mixtures are classed non-flammable, though the lecture warned that even 3% needs care.
  • The notes assign an electron trap to a dangling bond tied to H or OH alike. The lecture ruled OH out as a passivant because it is released easily, but its Si–O attachment (4.68 eV) is stronger than Si–H (3.30 eV). Hot electrons break Si–H → dangling bond → Qit ↑ → VT ↑ → ID ↓ → failure. Si–F (5.86 eV) and interfacial N are the stronger alternatives.
  • Qot: either sign, in the bulk, from growth defects, implantation, sputtering and plasma etching (the lecture adds PECVD and names RIE), hot carriers and F–N current. The oxygen vacancy becomes the E′ centre on hole capture (puckered in α-quartz; several forms in amorphous SiO₂). Annealed above 550 °C (lecture: ~500 °C, N₂ or Ar), before metallization.
  • Qm: Na⁺, K⁺ held beside non-bridging O⁻, drifting with bias and temperature; from water, chemicals, solvents and people. The number is fixed in manufacture; the position is not.
