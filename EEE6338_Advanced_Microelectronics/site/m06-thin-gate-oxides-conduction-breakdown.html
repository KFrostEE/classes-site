Module 06

Thin Gate Oxides: Conduction and Breakdown

How electrons cross a film that is supposed to insulate, what that current does to the film, how the film eventually fails, and the thinnest silicon dioxide that any of this leaves usable. Then the growth techniques and the chemical modifications that were invented to push that limit down.

Prerequisites M05 Read time ~55 min Sources Class notes pp. 29–48 (Chapter 3); Wolf Vol. 4 ch. 3; Taur & Ning ch. 4

M05 treated the gate oxide as a dielectric that holds charge. That treatment assumed no current flows through it, which is a good assumption for a 20 nm film and a false one for a 2 nm film. Silicon dioxide has a band gap near 9 eV and presents a 3.1 eV barrier to electrons at the silicon surface, so a thick film is an excellent insulator; but tunnelling depends on barrier width as steeply as on barrier height, and by the time the film is ten atomic layers thick the barrier is narrow enough that electrons cross it continuously under ordinary operating bias.

That changes what the oxide is. It stops being a passive insulator and becomes a component carrying current, dissipating power, and accumulating damage from the carriers passing through it. This module follows that sequence. It begins with the two distinct tunnelling mechanisms and the thickness at which each takes over, then the criterion that converts a tunnelling current into a hard limit on how thin the film may be. It then treats what the current does on its way through — trapping electrons, generating and trapping holes, and creating the traps that let still more current through — and how that damage accumulates into breakdown. The second half is about the response: the growth techniques that make a 2 nm film worth having, the nitrogen and fluorine chemistries that strengthen it, and the point at which silicon dioxide runs out of margin altogether and a different material becomes unavoidable, which is M07.

1 · How electrons cross an oxide

Two mechanisms carry electrons through a gate oxide, and which one operates is decided by the oxide thickness and the field across it. Both are quantum-mechanical tunnelling: the electron does not acquire enough energy to pass over the barrier, it passes through a classically forbidden region because its wavefunction has a non-zero amplitude on the far side. The probability of that falls off exponentially with the width of the forbidden region, which is why thickness matters so much more than any other variable.

Establishing the barrier first makes both mechanisms follow from one picture. A band diagram of the complete gate stack — gate, oxide and semiconductor drawn side by side on a common energy axis — is the reference state that everything in this module perturbs.

1.1 The barrier, drawn

A band diagram plots electron energy vertically against position through the structure horizontally. Higher on the page means higher electron energy. Within each material the conduction band edge Ec and the valence band edge Ev mark the energies bounding the forbidden gap, and the Fermi level EF marks the energy at which a state has a one-half probability of being occupied. In silicon the gap is 1.12 eV; in silicon dioxide it is about 9 eV, and that gap straddles the silicon gap asymmetrically. The conduction band of the oxide sits 3.1 eV above the conduction band of silicon, and this 3.1 eV offset — not the 9 eV gap — is the barrier an electron in the silicon conduction band actually faces.

The gate is n⁺ polysilicon rather than a metal, for the reasons given in M03 §8. Doping it degenerately places its Fermi level at the conduction band edge, so as a source or sink of electrons it behaves much like a metal — with one exception that §6 is devoted to.

Applying a bias tilts the oxide bands. A positive voltage on the gate lowers the electron energy on the gate side, so the oxide conduction band slopes downward from the silicon toward the gate, and the rectangular barrier of Figure 1 becomes a trapezoid. The slope is the oxide field Eox, and how much of the trapezoid the electron must traverse is what separates the two conduction mechanisms.

1.2 Fowler–Nordheim tunnelling

When the oxide is thick enough and the field large enough, the sloping oxide conduction band drops below the electron's energy part-way across the film. The electron tunnels through the triangular part of the barrier only, emerges into the conduction band of the oxide, and then drifts the rest of the way to the gate as a free carrier in SiO₂. This is Fowler–Nordheim tunnelling. The distance actually tunnelled is φox/Eox, the barrier height divided by its slope, and is shorter than the oxide.

Because the tunnelling distance is set by the field and not by the film thickness, the Fowler–Nordheim current density depends on the field alone:

1.3 Direct tunnelling

Make the oxide thin enough and the electron reaches the gate before the sloping oxide conduction band has dropped to meet it. There is then no triangular barrier to tunnel through and no free flight afterwards: the electron tunnels through the entire forbidden gap of the oxide in one step, from the silicon conduction band directly into the gate. This is direct tunnelling, and it takes over for oxides of roughly 4 nm and below.

The crossover is properly a condition, not a thickness. Fowler–Nordheim tunnelling requires the band edge to fall past the electron within the film, which happens when φox/Eox < tox — equivalently when the voltage across the oxide exceeds the barrier, Vox > φox ≈ 3.1 V. Below that voltage the transport is direct whatever the thickness. Since supply voltages fell below 3.1 V at the same time as oxides fell below 4 nm, the two statements coincide in practice, and 4 nm is the thickness usually quoted.

The two mechanisms are not two regimes of one equation. Direct tunnelling has no simple dependence on voltage or field, because the barrier the electron crosses is trapezoidal and its width is fixed by the film rather than by the bias. What it does have is an extreme sensitivity to thickness, since thickness is now exactly the tunnelling distance. The notes put the point at which the direct tunnelling current becomes very large below 3 nm.

2 · The tunnelling limit on oxide thickness

Direct tunnelling puts a floor under the gate oxide thickness, and the floor can be located precisely because there is a natural point of comparison. A MOSFET that is nominally off already leaks: subthreshold conduction carries a source-to-drain current even below threshold, as M05 §3.1 described. Gate leakage becomes intolerable at the point where it stops being a small addition to that existing leakage. The limit is therefore reached when the gate-to-channel tunnelling current becomes equal to the off-state source-to-drain subthreshold leakage current.

Making that concrete requires a number for the subthreshold leakage and a channel area to spread it over. The notes take an off-state leakage specification of Ioff/W = 10 nA/µm — a current per unit channel width, which is how off-state leakage is always specified, because it scales with width.

From a leakage specification to a current density

Take a MOSFET with a 1 µm gate length and a 1 µm gate width. Its channel area is 1 µm². At 10 nA/µm of width, a 1 µm-wide device leaks 10 nA = 10⁻⁸ A from source to drain in the off state.

For the gate tunnelling current to stay below that, it must not exceed 10⁻⁸ A spread over the 1 µm² of channel, which is a current density of 10⁻⁸ A/µm². Converting to the units in which tunnelling currents are quoted: 1 cm² = 10⁸ µm², so

10⁻⁸ A/µm² × 10⁸ µm²/cm² = 1 A/cm²

One ampere per square centimetre is the ceiling on gate tunnelling current density. It is worth noticing how permissive that sounds and how little margin it actually leaves: §7 shows the current density climbing through twelve orders of magnitude as the oxide thins by a factor of two, so a ceiling twelve decades above the starting point is crossed within a couple of nanometres.

The gate length enters this, and it is worth being explicit about it. Gate leakage scales with channel area while the subthreshold leakage it is compared against scales with channel width, so the ratio of the two carries a factor of L. The 1 A/cm² figure is the value for L = 1 µm; a shorter gate has less area to leak through per unit width and so tolerates a proportionally larger current density. The number is a well-defined benchmark rather than a constant of nature, and it is the benchmark the literature quotes.

That criterion is what ends the era of silicon dioxide as the sole gate dielectric. The notes state the consequence directly: alternate gate dielectric materials with reduced gate leakage will be required for 50 nm MOSFETs and beyond. Those materials, and the argument that lets a physically thicker film behave electrically like a thinner one, are M07.

Where "very large" actually falls

Measured thin-oxide gate currents place the 1 A/cm² ceiling nearer 1.5 to 2 nm than 3 nm. On the leakage figures of §7.2 — 10⁻¹² A/cm² at 35 Å rising to 1 A/cm² at about 15 Å, roughly six decades per nanometre — a 3 nm oxide sits around 10⁻⁹ A/cm², nine decades below the ceiling derived above. Taur & Ning's own milestones are 4 nm for the current beginning to rise sharply, 2 nm for direct tunnelling becoming important, and about 1.5 nm for the standby power it dissipates becoming unacceptable.

Three nanometres is where the current stops being negligible, which is a real transition and is what the notes are marking; it is not where the current reaches the limit. Use the notes' figure for anything assessed, and keep the two thresholds distinct — §7 depends on the difference.

3 · What the current does to the oxide

An electron crossing the oxide does not leave it unchanged. Some electrons are captured by defects in the film; some generate holes, which are captured in turn; and the passage of current generates new defects that were not there before. Each of these alters the field distribution inside the oxide, and because the tunnelling current depends exponentially on that field, each alteration feeds back on the current that produced it. The sign of the feedback differs between the two carriers, and that difference governs how an oxide behaves under stress.

Two terms are needed first. The cathode is the electrode acting as the electron source, and the anode is the electrode acting as the electron sink. Which physical layer is which depends on the bias polarity: under positive gate bias the inverted silicon surface is the cathode and the polysilicon gate is the anode, and under negative gate bias the roles reverse. The behaviour below is stated in terms of cathode and anode because it does not depend on which layer happens to be playing each role.

3.1 Electron trapping, and negative feedback

Electrons enter the oxide from the silicon substrate or from the polysilicon gate, either by the tunnelling of §1 or by hot-electron injection — a carrier heated by the lateral field in the channel arriving at the interface with enough energy to surmount the barrier rather than tunnel through it. Some fraction of these electrons is captured by electron traps already present in the film.

Trapped electrons are negative charge sitting in the middle of the dielectric, and they redistribute the field. The field near the cathode is decreased and the field near the anode is increased, because the trapped negative sheet terminates some of the field lines that would otherwise have run the full thickness of the film.

A decrease in the field near the cathode causes the Fowler–Nordheim current to decrease, by equation (6.1). This is negative feedback: the current fills traps, the filled traps suppress the current. In a constant-voltage measurement the observed signature is a tunnelling current that falls with time. The effect is large enough to matter in practice. In the classic measurements of Lenzlinger and Snow the samples were first held at a current density of about 10⁻¹⁰ A/cm² for two hours, over which the tunnelling current fell by about an order of magnitude from its initial value; the Fowler–Nordheim data they reported were taken after that conditioning. How long the decrease runs before a more or less steady state is reached depends on the thickness of the film and on how it was grown.

3.2 Hole generation and trapping, and positive feedback

Holes reach the oxide by two routes, and neither is a hole simply tunnelling in from the silicon valence band.

The first route is impact ionisation inside the oxide itself. An electron travelling in the oxide conduction band gains energy from the oxide field. The most it can gain is qVox, so if the voltage dropped across the film exceeds about 9 V — putting that energy above the ≈9 eV band gap of silicon dioxide — the electron can promote an oxide valence electron across the gap and create an electron–hole pair in the film. Note the units: the comparison is between an energy qVox and an energy Eg, which is the same discipline equation (6.1) applies to φox.

That condition is necessary and not sufficient. Electrons in the oxide conduction band lose energy continuously to optical phonons, so they reach a field-dependent steady-state energy distribution rather than accumulating the full qVox. Appreciable impact ionisation additionally requires a high field, above roughly 7–8 MV/cm, and a film thick enough for an electron to reach the threshold energy before it scatters. It is not observed in thin films however large Vox is made — which is why the second route matters more in the films this module is about.

The second route is anode hole injection, and it operates at lower voltages. A tunnelling electron arrives at the anode carrying the energy it gained crossing the oxide and causes impact ionisation in the anode material, close to the anode–oxide interface. If the arriving electron was energetic enough, the hole so created comes from deep in the anode valence band and is therefore hot. A hot hole sitting at the anode–oxide interface can be injected back into the oxide, and it is then captured as it drifts toward the cathode.

Trapped holes are positive charge, so they tilt the field the opposite way: the oxide field near the cathode increases and the field near the anode decreases. Because the tunnelling current depends exponentially on the cathode field, a small increase there causes a large increase in tunnelling current. Hole trapping near the cathode therefore provides positive feedback to the electron tunnelling process — more current injects more holes, which raise the cathode field, which drives still more current. The underlying tendency is a current that rises with time.

The two signatures are not separable in a real measurement

Read together, §3.1 and §3.2 suggest that the sign of dI/dt under constant-voltage stress identifies which mechanism is running. It does not. Electron trapping and hole trapping proceed at the same time, and the increase in tunnelling current due to hole trapping is usually not readily observable because it is masked by the decrease due to electron trapping. A constant-voltage trace on a 20 nm oxide typically falls monotonically for the whole stress and then jumps at breakdown; the rising phase becomes visible only close to breakdown.

Anode hole injection itself is a model rather than a settled result. It has never been completely verified, photons generated by the Fowler–Nordheim current in the gate have been proposed as the dominant source of the measured substrate hole current instead, and the standard account of oxide degradation built on anode hole injection may yet be revised. It is presented above as mechanism because it is the account the course follows and the one the degradation literature is organised around. Wolf, Vol. 4 §3.3.4.

3.3 Trap-assisted tunnelling

A third process changes the transport rather than the field. An electron need not cross the whole oxide in one step: it can tunnel from the cathode into a trap inside the oxide, and then tunnel from that trap to the anode. Traps in the film act as stepping stones, splitting one long tunnelling step into two short ones and raising the current substantially, since each step is exponentially more probable than the single step it replaces.

The enhanced current then generates more traps, which provide more stepping stones. This is a second positive feedback loop, independent of the hole-trapping one, and it is the mechanism that connects an oxide carrying current to an oxide that eventually fails. It is also, in its low-field form, the mechanism of §4.

4 · Stress-induced leakage current

When a thin oxide is exposed to high electric field stress, and therefore to Fowler–Nordheim gate current, it is left afterwards conducting more current at low field than it did before. The film has been changed by the stress, and the change shows up as leakage under ordinary operating conditions. This effect is stress-induced leakage current, abbreviated SILC, and it was first reported in 1987.

The mechanism is trap generation followed by trap-assisted tunnelling. High-field stress current generates neutral oxide traps — defect sites that carry no charge, and so do not shift the threshold voltage the way the four charges of M05 §6 do, but that facilitate electron tunnelling by the stepping-stone process of §3.3. The oxide is not more conductive because it now holds charge; it is more conductive because it now holds a denser population of intermediate states for electrons to hop through.

SILC behaves differently in thick and thin films, and the difference is about whether the traps stay filled. In thicker oxides SILC is a transient phenomenon: the leakage decays as the traps fill, because once an electron occupies a trap it does not readily tunnel out again, and a full trap is not a stepping stone. In thinner oxides the leakage is a continuous current, because tunnelling out of a trap is now as easy as tunnelling in, and a steady state is reached in which trap filling and trap emptying balance. That steady state is a permanent current path.

SILC is a particular problem for non-volatile memory. Such a device stores data as charge on a floating gate, and it writes and erases that charge by driving Fowler–Nordheim current through a thin tunnel oxide — so it inflicts high-field stress on its own dielectric as part of normal operation, repeatedly, over the life of the part. The resulting leakage lets the stored charge escape, which reduces data retentivity directly. The requirement is usually ten years of retention, and SILC makes non-volatile memory tunnel-oxide scaling much below 8 nm difficult unless that ten-year requirement is relaxed.

Soft breakdown

Below about 5 nm a further failure mode appears between SILC and the catastrophic breakdown of §5. Stressing a thin oxide beyond a critical field causes the SILC to transition into soft breakdown, also called quasi-breakdown: a sudden but modest jump in gate current, with a post-breakdown current–voltage characteristic that follows a power law rather than becoming ohmic. In 2.5 nm oxides stressed at 4.1 V and 140 °C, soft breakdown raises the gate current to around 10⁻⁴ A while hard breakdown raises it to around 10⁻² A. Soft breakdown is triggered once the oxide field exceeds roughly 10.7–11.0 MV/cm, and it becomes progressively softer and less abrupt as the oxide is thinned. Hard breakdown dominates in thicker films and is often not observed at all in thin gate oxides. Wolf, Vol. 4 §3.8.3 — beyond the class notes.

5 · Dielectric breakdown

Breakdown is the terminal failure: the oxide stops being a dielectric and becomes a conductor, permanently. In an integrated circuit it must be avoided outright rather than managed, because a single shorted gate oxide destroys the transistor that contains it, and a circuit has hundreds of millions of them sharing one process and one supply voltage.

The engineering constraint is stated as a field limit tied to a lifetime. A twenty-year lifetime can be achieved if the oxide field remains below 8 MV/cm. Design practice keeps a wide margin below that: for CMOS logic and memory chips the typical operating field is restricted to the 5 MV/cm range. The gap between the two numbers is not conservatism for its own sake — the failure statistics are steep, and a field a little above the design point shortens the lifetime by far more than proportionally.

Field limits govern the intrinsic population, the oxides that are as good as the process can make them. A separate population fails early because of localised defects, and no field limit screens those out. They are removed instead by burn-in: chips are operated at elevated voltage and temperature before shipment, which accelerates the failure of any part containing an oxide that would have failed under normal operating conditions during its service life. Burn-in does not repair anything; it converts a field failure years later into a factory failure now.

5.1 The two stages

Dielectric breakdown proceeds in two stages with very different time scales.

In the first stage the oxide is slowly degraded over time, either by the high electric field across it or by the passage of current through it. The processes are the ones of §3 and §4: trap generation, hole injection, interface-state creation. These defects accumulate until they form a localised conductive path through the oxide — a chain of defect sites dense enough to span the film. This stage occupies essentially the whole life of the device and is what the field limit and burn-in are designed to control.

The second stage is the very short runaway breakdown process. Once a conductive path exists, the current through it is concentrated into a very small area, the local heating is severe, and the path grows rapidly. The oxide is shorted and a very large current flows between the gate and the substrate. The transition from the first stage to the second is abrupt, which is why oxide lifetime is characterised as a time-to-breakdown rather than as a gradual drift in any device parameter.

6 · Polysilicon depletion

Every band diagram so far has treated the polysilicon gate as an ideal equipotential region: a perfect conductor whose potential is uniform, so that the whole applied gate voltage appears across the oxide and the silicon. For a thick gate oxide that approximation has insignificant effects on device behaviour and can be used freely. For a thin one it cannot, because polysilicon is a semiconductor and a semiconductor can be depleted.

6.1 Where the depletion region comes from

Consider an n⁺ polysilicon gate on a p-type silicon substrate, with a positive bias applied between gate and substrate. That bias causes depletion, or depletion and then inversion, in the silicon substrate, as M01 §2 described: negative charge is induced in the silicon. Charge neutrality requires an equal positive charge on the gate side of the oxide.

In a metal that charge is supplied by a surface layer of electrons rearranging over an atomic distance, and nothing else happens. In n⁺ polysilicon it is supplied by mobile, negatively charged electrons leaving the region of the gate next to the oxide, which exposes the fixed positive donor ions they were compensating. Those ionised donors constitute the positive charge — and because the donor concentration is finite, exposing enough of them requires emptying a layer of finite thickness. A depletion region forms in the polysilicon at the poly–oxide interface. This is the polysilicon depletion effect, abbreviated PDE.

A depletion region contains uncompensated charge, so the bands bend across it, so an electric field exists in it, so a voltage is dropped across it. That last consequence is the one that matters.

6.2 What it costs

The applied gate voltage divides between the poly depletion region, the oxide and the silicon substrate. Any voltage dropped in the poly is subtracted from the sum available to the other two, so the remaining voltage is smaller than it would be if the gate were an ideal conductor. A smaller voltage across the oxide and substrate induces a smaller inversion-layer charge density, and since drain current is proportional to inversion charge, the drain current of the MOSFET is degraded. The speed of the device falls with it.

At any given bias the effect on inversion charge is the same as if the gate oxide had been made thicker. Polysilicon depletion increases the equivalent oxide thickness tox,eq — the thickness of an ideal oxide that would produce the capacitance actually measured. That framing is worth holding on to, because M07 uses the same quantity for the opposite purpose: there it is a physically thick high-permittivity film behaving like a thin oxide, here it is a physically thin oxide behaving like a thick one.

The equivalence is bias-dependent rather than a fixed offset, which is what distinguishes it from actually growing a thicker film: the poly depletion layer has zero width at flat band and widens as the gate voltage rises. For a gate doped to 10²⁰ cm⁻³ operating at 5 MV/cm — an oxide field that corresponds to an inversion sheet density of Qg/q = 10¹³ cm⁻² — polysilicon depletion is equivalent to adding 3 to 4 Å to the gate oxide. On a 10 nm oxide that is 3 or 4%, which is why it was ignored for decades. On a 1.5 nm oxide it is 20 to 25%, which is why it stopped being ignorable and why gate materials had to change.

Quantitatively, the depletion region in the poly is a capacitor in series with the gate oxide capacitor. No metal electrode exists at the poly–oxide interface, but the equivalent circuit is still valid, because the two notional plates there carry equal and opposite charge and their effects cancel.

For capacitors in series the smaller capacitor largely determines the total, and that is what makes polysilicon depletion a scaling problem rather than a fixed penalty. Cox increases as the oxide is thinned. Cpoly dep does not hold still either: in the depletion approximation it is εsiqNp/Qg, inversely proportional to the gate charge density, so the larger charge a thinner oxide supports widens the poly depletion layer and lowers Cpoly dep further. Both terms of the series sum move against the designer.

The onset is a condition on thickness and supply voltage together, not on thickness alone, because the poly depletion width is set by the gate charge and the gate charge depends on both. Polysilicon depletion begins to degrade device performance appreciably when the gate oxide is scaled below about 10 nm and the gate voltages are 2.5 V or lower. Above that, Cox is much the smaller of the two capacitances and dominates the series combination, so the poly contributes nothing measurable.

The capacitance–voltage signature follows from that. A low-frequency measurement on an ideal MOS capacitor recovers Cox in strong inversion, because the inversion layer can follow the signal and screens the substrate. With poly depletion present the capacitance never returns to Cox: it rises to a local maximum somewhat below Cox and then falls again, because Cpoly dep keeps decreasing as the gate voltage rises. How far below Cox that maximum sits is set by the effective doping of the gate. The non-monotonic shape, rather than a simple offset, is what the measurement shows.

That signature is not by itself diagnostic. Quantisation of the inversion layer depresses the same curve for the same structural reason — the inversion charge centroid sits a finite depth below the surface, which adds its own series capacitance — and it does so with no polysilicon involved at all. The two are separated by fitting a model to the measured curve, not by inspection.

The mitigation follows from the mechanism. A higher polysilicon doping concentration means more donors per unit volume, so the required positive charge is supplied by depleting a thinner layer; the depletion region is narrower, its capacitance larger, and its effect on the series total smaller. Doping the gate more heavily is therefore the direct fix, and it is bounded by how much dopant can be made electrically active in polysilicon — about 10²⁰ cm⁻³ for n-type and only the mid-to-upper 10¹⁹ cm⁻³ for p-type, which is why p⁺ gates suffer polysilicon depletion worse than n⁺ gates — and by the boron penetration problem that §9 addresses for those same p⁺ gates.

What polysilicon depletion does, in order

  • The gate capacitance is more affected the thinner the oxide gets.
  • It reduces the total MOS gate capacitance.
  • It decreases the potential at the silicon surface for the same gate voltage, and so decreases the inversion layer charge density.
  • The drain current decreases, and the speed of the MOSFET decreases.

7 · The minimum usable oxide thickness

Everything to this point converges on one engineering question: how thin can the gate oxide actually be made in production. Six factors play a role in setting that minimum, and they are the subject matter of this module and the last one:

  1. Oxide reliability — the time-to-breakdown of §5.
  2. Tunnelling leakage current — §1 and §2.
  3. Stress-induced leakage current — §4.
  4. Polysilicon depletion — §6.
  5. Process-induced oxide damage — §10.
  6. Hot-carrier-induced damage — §3, and M05 §6.3.

The answer is not a single number, because the type of application determines which factors bind. Logic devices, DRAMs and non-volatile memories each tolerate a different failure and therefore each settle at a different thickness. A non-volatile memory is limited by SILC and data retention, as §4 showed, at thicknesses a logic device would find unremarkable; a DRAM is expected to stop at about 3 nm, since a leaking gate oxide discharges the stored bit.

7.1 Defects and tunnelling

Two of the six factors — defect densities in the film, and direct tunnelling current through it — dominate, and they are treated together because they happen to set approximately the same limit.

The reliability limit is computed by extrapolating time-to-breakdown data to a ten-year operating life. The extrapolation is run at the supply voltage plus 10%, which is the worst case a part must survive rather than its nominal rating. For defect-free oxides, the following thicknesses meet a ten-year time-to-breakdown:

The published production figures do not follow from it: 1.30 × 80 Å is 104 Å, not 110 Å, and the three rows imply increases of 36 to 44%, averaging nearer 39%. Both columns are quoted here as published, with the discrepancy shown rather than smoothed over — it lives in the source, and rounding does not account for it.

The projections continue downward from there, and they did not agree with each other. The next generation of work targeted 50 nm gate lengths using oxides below 2.0 nm. A 1998 study put the production minimum at 2.6 nm for a 1.0 V supply, on the argument that intrinsic oxide degradation and breakdown would become the dominant limit — which also implied that a 2.2 nm oxide would fail the roadmap requirement outright. A 1999 study countered that 1.6 nm could meet the same ten-year target at 1.2 V, provided film-thickness uniformity were substantially improved, since reliability at a given thickness improves with uniformity. In 1999 a 100 nm CMOS process shipped with a 2.0 nm oxide at 1.5 V, and by 2000 the same group reported 130 nm CMOS with a 1.5 nm oxide at 1.3 V. Practice overtook both projections.

The tunnelling limit lands in the same region. Tunnelling leakage currents become excessive once the oxide thickness falls below 1.4 nm, which is close to the thickness the reliability extrapolation permits, so neither consideration rescues the other.

7.2 How steeply leakage climbs

The steepness is the whole point, and it is worth seeing as numbers. At a gate bias of about 1 V, the leakage current density through a silicon dioxide film changes from 1×10⁻¹² A/cm² at 35 Å to 1×10⁻¹⁰ A/cm² at about 15 Å.

The second figure lost a sign in transcription, and the published span is off by a decade

The 15 Å value is wrong as written, and tracing it to the primary literature is worth doing because the correction runs the opposite way from the obvious guess. The published value at about 15 Å is 1×10 A/cm², that is 10 A/cm² — not 10⁻¹⁰, and not the 10⁰ that the accompanying prose might suggest. What was lost in transcription is the absence of an exponent, read as a negative one.

Two checks settle it. The same source states a few pages later that films as thin as 20 Å leak 1–10 A/cm², and tunnelling current increases monotonically as a film thins, so a 15 Å film cannot leak less than a 20 Å one; 10⁻¹⁰ A/cm² is twelve decades below that and 1 A/cm² is below it too. Independently, measured 1.5 nm oxides at 1 V sit in the 10 to 10² A/cm² band, and gate current densities up to about 10³ A/cm² are tolerated in the highest-performance parts at 1 to 1.5 nm. 10 A/cm² is the value consistent with all of it.

One inconsistency survives the correction and belongs to the literature rather than to the transcription. The source describes the change between its two endpoints as twelve orders of magnitude, but 10⁻¹² to 10¹ is thirteen. The endpoints and the span as printed disagree by one decade. That is worth reporting rather than repairing: choosing the endpoint that makes the round phrase come out even is how 1 A/cm² would be arrived at, and it is not what the source prints.

Note also that 1 A/cm² and 1.4 nm come from different places and are not two readings of one fact. 1 A/cm² is a design criterion, derived in §2 from subthreshold leakage. The 1.4 nm floor is a measurement: it is where the gate current of a particular low-leakage oxide, grown by RTO after an in-situ clean, crosses that criterion. The 35 Å / 15 Å pair above comes from a third body of data again. That they land close together is the point of §7 — it is not evidence that any one of them implies another.

Use the notes' figures for anything assessed. Wolf, Vol. 4 §3.8.1 footnote and §3.4.2, after D. A. Buchanan, IBM J. Res. Develop. 43, 245 (1999); Taur & Ning §4.6.1.2, Fig. 4.41.

Whichever endpoint is taken, the structural conclusion is the same and is the one to carry forward: direct tunnelling current is exponential in thickness, so the transition from negligible gate leakage to prohibitive gate leakage occupies a couple of ångströms rather than a generation of scaling. There is no thickness at which the problem is manageable and the next node is a little worse. This is why the response had to be a change of material rather than a change of dimension.

8 · Growing an oxide this thin

A 2 nm gate oxide is not a thin version of a 100 nm one. The growth model that describes thick oxides fails in this regime, and the furnace that grows them well cannot control a film this thin. Both had to be replaced.

8.1 The models fail below 200 Å

The Deal–Grove model allows the thickness of oxide films thicker than 350 Å to be predicted accurately as a function of temperature, furnace ambient, silicon doping concentration and silicon orientation. It is the standard treatment of thermal oxidation and it is genuinely predictive over its range.

Two separate limitations are worth keeping apart. First, Deal–Grove is a rate-constant description and does not give a detailed understanding of the mechanisms that produce the dependencies it captures — that is true at every thickness, including the range where it predicts accurately. Second, and independently, it is not valid below 200 Å: for dry oxidation in that range the oxide grows faster than the model predicts, so the model does not merely fail to explain, it returns the wrong number. That second discrepancy is the anomalous region of the Deal–Grove model. Several models have been proposed to explain it:

  1. Models based on space charge effects, in which the oxidation enhancement in the thin regime is due to field-assisted oxidant diffusion, arising from O₂⁻ coupled diffusion with holes.
  2. Models based on the existence of structural defects in the oxide, such as micropores or channels around 10 Å in diameter, which would allow oxygen molecules to diffuse more rapidly when the oxide is thin.
  3. Models based on parallel O₂ and O reactions at the Si/SiO₂ interface — molecular and atomic oxygen contributing through separate pathways.
  4. Models postulating a blocking layer near the Si/SiO₂ interface, which forms as the oxide grows and slows the rate for thicker layers. Here the thin-oxide rate is the undisturbed one and the thick-oxide rate is the anomaly.
  5. A model by Massoud based on the presence of a thin surface layer in the silicon where additional reaction sites are available, their concentration decaying exponentially with depth over a characteristic length.

None of them has been convincingly accepted as more valid than the others. Two responses followed, and they are different in kind.

The first is an empirical fit that abandons Deal–Grove entirely. Reisman and Nicollian's 1987 model fits all published dry-O₂ data:

tox = a(tg + τ)b
(6.3)
tox oxide thickness (Å) · tg growth time, the measured duration of the oxidation · τ the time that would have been required to grow an oxide of thickness toxi already present on the surface, which shifts the time origin so that a pre-existing native oxide is accounted for · a, b fitting constants. Empirical, for dry O₂. It is a fit to data, not a mechanism — it makes no claim about which of the five models is right. The exponent b is not universal: extracted values run from 0.25 to 1.0 depending on temperature and oxidant partial pressure. Its notable property is that it fits down to essentially zero thickness, so on this description there is no anomalous regime at all — the anomaly is an artefact of insisting on the Deal–Grove form.

The second response keeps Deal–Grove and repairs it, and it is the one that was actually adopted. Massoud's correction adds a second term to the Deal–Grove growth rate — one that decays exponentially with thickness, so it dominates while the film is thin and vanishes once it is not:

dtoxdt = B2tox + A + C exp (− toxL2 )
(6.5)
tox oxide thickness · t time · A, B the ordinary Deal–Grove linear and parabolic rate constants · C = C0exp(−EA/kT), with C0 ≈ 3.6×10⁸ µm/hr and EA ≈ 2.35 eV · L2 ≈ 7 nm, the decay length of the enhancement. For dry oxidation of lightly doped substrates over 800–1000 °C, and independent of surface orientation. The first term is Deal–Grove unchanged; the second is the anomalous enhancement, negligible once tox ≫ L2.

Equation (6.5) is the thin-oxide growth model implemented in the standard process simulators, and Reisman's is not — because a great deal of other oxidation physics is already expressed in terms of the Deal–Grove constants A and B, and a correction that preserves them inherits all of it while a replacement discards it. Note also which of the five models Massoud's term belongs to: it is number 5 in the list above. The mechanistic question was never settled, but one of the five candidates nonetheless became the form every simulator runs.

8.2 Rapid thermal oxidation

Conventional batch furnace oxidation faces two linked problems at these thicknesses. Furnace oxidation is difficult to scale below 2 nm, and the low temperatures used to slow growth enough for such ultra-thin films degrade the oxide quality. Growing slowly by growing cold trades the property being optimised for the one being controlled.

Rapid thermal oxidation, or RTO, breaks that trade by controlling the duration instead of the rate. It grows the oxides at higher temperatures, where the film quality is good, and keeps the total thickness small by keeping the oxidation time down to just a few seconds — where the minimum practical oxidation time in a batch furnace is at least a few minutes. It is also conducive to cluster tooling, meaning a tool in which several process steps are performed in connected chambers without breaking vacuum.

The sequence is short. A robot wafer handler inserts the wafer onto a silicon carbide rotating support ring. The wafer is heated rapidly, at rates of 50–100 °C/s, to a process temperature of 800–1050 °C. Process gases are introduced at reduced pressure in an axial flow pattern, where they react to form water vapour, hydroxyl ions, oxygen ions and other species — which species form, and in what proportion, depends on the hydrogen-to-oxygen ratio and on whether N₂, NH₃, HCl or other reactant gases are added. At the conclusion of the oxidation step the wafer is cooled rapidly.

Growing at higher temperature is not merely tolerable, it is better, in three measurable ways:

  1. Oxides exhibit smaller gate leakage currents as their growth temperature increases, up to 1050 °C, beyond which the leakage current increases again.
  2. They show higher resistance to boron penetration. A 2 nm oxide grown at 800 °C was compared with one grown by rapid thermal processing at 1000 °C, and boron penetration was suppressed in the RTP-grown oxide.
  3. MOSFETs built with RTP-grown oxides had higher effective carrier mobilities, for both electrons and holes, as the oxide growth temperature increased from 800 to 1000 °C. This is due to the smoother Si/SiO₂ interface that the higher temperature produces — surface roughness scattering is one of the two mechanisms that degrade inversion-layer mobility, as M01 §4 noted.

Cluster tools produce better quality oxides because they allow process sequences to be carried out in an integrated fashion. The specific gain concerns cleaning: an oxide growth process requires a cleaning procedure before the growth step, and if that clean can be performed in situ, with no exposure to atmosphere between cleaning and growth, the quality of the oxide improves. Cluster tools suit single-wafer processing and are impractical for batch furnace tools.

8.3 Wet RTO and in-situ steam generation

Dry oxides are expected to be limited to minimum thicknesses somewhere between 1.6 nm and 2.5 nm. Wet rapid thermal oxidation is one route below that. The technique called in-situ steam generation, or ISSG, has allowed oxides in the 2.0 nm range to be formed with better reliability than dry oxides of the same thickness.

ISSG is a low-pressure oxidation, typically below 20 Torr for safety reasons, performed in a cold-wall rapid thermal processing reactor. The safety constraint is not incidental, and it answers a question the next paragraph would otherwise raise: what is being limited is a premixed charge of hydrogen and oxygen in a hot chamber.

The distinguishing feature is where the steam comes from. A conventional hot-wall furnace generates wet ambient in a pyrogenic torch upstream and pipes it in. In ISSG no pre-combustion occurs: electronic-grade hydrogen and oxygen are premixed in a plenum and injected, the hot wafer acts as the ignition source, and H₂O, OH and atomic oxygen are generated directly in the reaction chamber at its surface. The hydrogen fraction is a process variable rather than a fixed stoichiometry — the wide range of temperatures and hydrogen concentrations available is what allows controlled oxidation at any thickness.

Two properties of the process follow from that arrangement and are unusual enough to be worth stating explicitly. The oxidation rate rapidly increases as the pressure is decreased below 20 Torr — the opposite of conventional oxidation, where the rate decreases as the pressure is decreased, since both Deal–Grove rate constants scale roughly linearly with the oxidant partial pressure. And the oxidation rate shows a strong correlation to the atomic oxygen concentration and not to any other atomic or molecular species, which identifies atomic oxygen as the reactive agent. The oxidation is also more strongly temperature dependent than wet atmospheric oxidation. ISSG oxides demonstrate better reliability than comparable furnace oxides.

9 · Changing the chemistry of the film

Growing pure silicon dioxide better eventually stops being enough. The next response was to stop growing pure silicon dioxide: adding nitrogen, or fluorine, to the film changes its bonding in ways that address specific failure modes directly. These are the last modifications before the material itself is replaced in M07.

9.1 Why nitrogen helps

Oxynitrides have been studied as replacements for silicon dioxide as the gate dielectric of MOSFETs for thicknesses below 4.0 nm. Four advantages over SiO₂ are claimed for them:

  1. They exhibit improved boron penetration resistance. Boron from a p⁺ polysilicon gate diffuses through a thin gate oxide into the channel, shifting the threshold voltage of the device it was supposed to set — one of the five failures of a very thin oxide in M05 §3.2.
  2. They exhibit better breakdown characteristics.
  3. They show greater immunity to hot-electron injection effects.
  4. MOSFETs made with such layers exhibit improved high-field channel electron mobility. The claim is made for electrons; it is not made for holes.

The mechanism is interfacial. The improved performance of nitrided SiO₂ has been associated with the accumulation of a small amount of nitrogen at the SiO₂/Si interface, and interfacial nitrogen accumulation reduces interfacial defects by relieving strained Si–O bonds and dangling bonds. These are precisely the defects of M05 §6.2: the stretched bonds forced out of equilibrium by the lattice mismatch between crystalline silicon and amorphous oxide, and the unsatisfied bonds left by incomplete oxidation. Nitrogen at the interface provides the bonding flexibility to relieve them.

The amount matters, and so does which face of the film it sits on. About 1–2 atomic percent peaked at the Si/SiO₂ interface is what raises the breakdown field and lowers the fixed oxide charge density. Suppressing boron penetration needs a higher concentration, above about 5 atomic percent, and it is needed at the opposite face — the poly/SiO₂ interface, because that is where the boron enters the film from the p⁺ gate above it. Nitrogen in the bulk of the film degrades the oxide and is deliberately kept out, so an engineered stack places nitrogen in two thin layers with as little as possible between them.

Nitridation of SiO₂ is achieved by using an ambient such as NH₃, N₂O, NO, or a mixture of O₂ and NO. Which one is used determines the process and its problems, and the two families differ enough to be treated separately.

9.2 Ammonia nitridation, and its hydrogen problem

Early oxynitride films were formed by nitriding a thermally grown oxide film in an NH₃ ambient at high temperature, then re-oxidising the nitrided oxide to reduce the amount of hydrogen remaining in the film after NH₃ nitridation. These are called reoxidized nitrided oxide, or ROXNOX, films. They exhibit better hot-carrier resistance than pure SiO₂.

The process is difficult to optimise, and the reason is that the re-oxidation step has to correct a problem the nitridation step created without going too far. Ammonia is NH₃, so nitriding in it necessarily introduces hydrogen, and the consequences run in both directions:

  • The ROXNOX process may leave excess hydrogen, giving a higher density of electron traps. Electron trapping is a dominant degradation mechanism in p-channel MOSFETs, which therefore exhibit higher hot-carrier degradation.
  • If the re-oxidation step is excessive, the film loses strength against interface-state generation by hot carriers, and n-channel MOSFETs containing such films are prone to more hot-carrier degradation.
  • The best NH₃-nitrided films always exhibit higher densities of positive fixed charge Qf than the best SiO₂ films — the comparison is between the best of each, not between an arbitrary pair.
  • The ROXNOX process involves multiple high-temperature steps, which is a thermal budget cost paid by every dopant profile already in the wafer.

9.3 Nitrous oxide and nitric oxide

The hydrogen incorporation can be avoided by replacing NH₃ with N₂O as the nitriding ambient, since N₂O contains no hydrogen at all.

One approach grows the dielectric by directly reacting N₂O at the silicon surface in an RTP system. Less hydrogen is incorporated in the film, and secondary ion mass spectrometry — a technique that sputters the film away layer by layer and mass-analyses the ejected ions, so giving a concentration profile against depth — confirms both less hydrogen in the film and a higher nitrogen concentration at the Si/SiO₂ interface, which is where §9.1 says it is wanted.

The growth is diffusion-limited. Thickness increases approximately as the square root of time, which indicates that growth is limited by diffusion of N₂O through the oxide bulk rather than by the reaction at the interface — the same square-root signature that distinguishes a diffusion-limited from a reaction-limited regime in the Deal–Grove model. n-channel MOSFETs fabricated with these films exhibited low electron trapping density, less hot-carrier degradation than those made with control oxides, and smaller values of ΔDit, the increase in interface trap density under stress.

Four limitations were observed against pure oxide films:

  1. Larger initial values of Dit than pure oxide films.
  2. Initial values of Qf were also higher.
  3. Slow growth makes these films practical only for ULSI applications, which use dielectric films thinner than 100 Å — the process cannot deliver a thicker film in a reasonable time. For this course's purposes that restriction is not a problem, since 100 Å is already far thicker than the films §7 is concerned with.
  4. The peak electron mobility in MOSFETs made with these films is about 5% lower than in MOSFETs made with oxide. The comparison reverses at large lateral electric fields, where the mobility is actually higher.

The two-step process

The alternative is to separate growth from nitridation. Oxides are first grown in O₂, and the films are then subjected to an oxynitridation step in N₂O or NO. The films have been grown by furnace at atmospheric pressure, by low pressure, and by RTP.

The low-pressure process has a threshold rather than a window. A minimum N₂O pressure, reported as somewhere between 1 and 50 Torr, is needed before any measurable nitrogen is incorporated at all. Working just above that onset is what the process is for: the interfacial nitrogen concentration can be controlled while almost no additional oxide grows during the nitridation step, so the final thickness stays tightly controlled.

Using N₂O as the nitrogen source in the second step has a disadvantage all the same. The nitrogen concentration it incorporates may be too low for some applications, especially suppression of boron penetration, unless a much higher thermal budget is permitted.

NO has more recently been used as the source in the second step. It offers a low thermal budget for incorporating a higher concentration of nitrogen, as well as better thickness control for scaled dielectrics.

The chemistry explains why NO outperforms N₂O, and the numbers explain the thermal budget. Nitrogen incorporation from N₂O is driven by the NO species, which is a product of N₂O dissociation — so N₂O works only by becoming NO first, and it does so inefficiently. The molecule dissociates into NO at about 4.7%, N₂ at about 64.3% and O₂ at about 31%, and the three products act in competition: NO incorporates nitrogen, O₂ continues oxidising the silicon by reacting with it, and N₂ — nearly two thirds of the gas, and the majority product — dilutes the partial pressure of the nitriding species and drives the thermal budget up. Dissociation also requires a temperature above 850 °C. Supplying NO directly avoids both costs: it delivers the nitriding species without the 95% that is not doing the job, and without the dissociation temperature. The percentage of nitrogen incorporated depends on the ratio of the NO + O₂ mixture when using RTP.

A representative recipe: a 4 nm oxynitride is formed by growing 4 nm of oxide using O₂ at 800 °C with 6% HCl and a post-oxidation anneal in N₂, then annealing the oxide in a diluted NO ambient at 800 °C for 45 minutes.

9.4 Fluorinated gate oxides

Incorporating fluorine into the gate oxide increases hot-electron resistance. The reason is a direct bond-strength argument, and it follows on from the passivation discussion in M05 §6.3: the presence of fluorine replaces Si–H bonds at the Si/SiO₂ interface with Si–F bonds, and Si–F bonds are stronger than Si–H bonds. A hot carrier that would have had enough energy to break the Si–H bond and create an interface trap does not have enough to break the Si–F bond.

Fluorine may be introduced inadvertently rather than deliberately, by two common process steps:

  1. A BF₂⁺ source/drain implant, where boron is implanted as the BF₂ molecular ion and the fluorine comes along with it.
  2. An LPCVD-W or LPCVD-WSix process, where WF₆ and SiH₄ are the reactants — tungsten hexafluoride carries six fluorine atoms per tungsten atom into the chamber.

The benefit has a cost that runs against §9.1, and the cost falls on only half of a CMOS pair. Fluorine in the gate oxide may increase boron penetration from p⁺ polysilicon gates, which is a potential problem for deep-submicron p-channel MOSFETs; n-channel devices, whose gates are n⁺, get the hot-carrier benefit with none of the penalty. That asymmetry is what makes fluorine an integration problem rather than a device problem.

The two effects are separable by dose, and the dose that matters is the one implanted into the polysilicon gate — the BF₂⁺ implant lands in the poly, and the fluorine then diffuses from there into the gate oxide during the activation anneals. Holding that to a medium dose of about 1×10¹⁴ cm⁻² gives no noticeable enhanced boron penetration, and under the same conditions the charge-to-breakdown of 4 nm oxides on p-channel capacitors is significantly improved.

9.5 Two oxide thicknesses on one wafer

Not every transistor on a chip wants the thinnest possible oxide. High voltage may be used for analogue and input/output circuitry, which requires thicker oxides to withstand it, while logic applications use the thinner gate oxide that gives the drive current and speed. Both are built on the same wafer, which requires a dual gate oxide thickness structure.

A two-step oxidation produces them. The sequence is:

  1. Pattern photoresist, exposing the regions that are to receive the thinner oxide.
  2. Perform a low-energy nitrogen implant through the exposed areas.
  3. Strip the resist.
  4. Strip the sacrificial screen oxide the implant was performed through, and clean.
  5. Perform the thermal oxidation.

Typical implant conditions are 10–30 keV at a dose of 2×10¹⁴ to 1×10¹⁵ cm⁻². The differentiation comes from that implant: nitrogen implanted into silicon before oxidation reduces the rate at which that silicon subsequently oxidises. The suggested mechanism is the formation of Si–N bonds at the interface of the growing oxide, which constrain further growth — a different chemistry from §9.1, where nitrogen relieves strain in a film that has already been grown. The single oxidation step at the end therefore grows a thinner film over the implanted regions and a thicker film everywhere else, and one oxidation produces two thicknesses.

10 · Process damage, and measuring the film

Two practical matters close the chapter. Both concern the gap between the oxide as designed and the oxide as it exists on a finished wafer.

Process-induced damage arrives long after the oxide is grown, and whether it gets worse as the film thins is not settled. The coupling is electrical rather than physical: charge collected by the large metal and polysilicon structures connected to the gate is driven through the gate oxide, which is why a step several mask levels later, during interconnect fabrication and never in contact with the gate, can damage it.

The evidence runs both ways, and the two thickness pairs involved are almost the same:

  • A 1996 analysis found thinner oxides less damaged — 6.4 nm oxides showed an order of magnitude smaller interface-trap density than 11.6 nm oxides. The reasoning is that plasma charging behaves as a constant-current source rather than a fixed voltage, so the same current passes whatever the thickness, and a thinner oxide tolerates that current stress better. The conclusion drawn was that plasma damage would not become more serious below 5 nm.
  • A 2000 report found the opposite: damage increased as the gate oxide was thinned from 11.5 nm to 5.5 nm, with the RF back-sputter via-clean process used to prepare vias for metal deposition as the main source. Plasma damage was found to correlate with long-term oxide reliability degradation, so it does not announce itself at test; it shortens the life of parts that pass.
  • A third report from the same conference held that the available measurement techniques cannot determine whether low-level plasma damage is occurring at all.

The question is open. What is not in doubt is that the damage is real, that its route is the antenna of connected conductors rather than the plasma reaching the oxide, and that it is counted among the six factors of §7.

Measuring a film this thin is itself difficult, and two techniques are used. Ellipsometry measures the change in polarisation of light reflected from the surface and infers thickness and refractive index from it. It is optical, non-contact and fast, which makes it the production measurement, and it can be pushed down below 50 Å — but accuracy degrades there, and multiple-wavelength or multiple-angle variants are needed to recover it. The reason is structural rather than instrumental: the Si/SiO₂ transition region is several ångströms and can reach about 10 Å, which is 20 to 50% of an ultra-thin film, and it has a different refractive index, dielectric constant, band gap and effective mass from the bulk oxide the measurement assumes. High-resolution transmission electron microscopy, HRTEM, images a thinned cross-section of the actual stack at atomic resolution, and oxide thickness as low as 2.4 nm has been measured with it. It is destructive and slow, which makes it the calibration measurement rather than the production one.

An oxide is stressed at constant voltage and the gate current is monitored. In one sample the current falls steadily for the first hour and then levels off; in another it rises slowly and then jumps abruptly by two orders of magnitude and stays there. Identify what is happening in each.

The first is electron trapping, §3.1. Electrons tunnelling into the oxide are captured, the trapped negative charge reduces the field at the cathode, and the reduced cathode field reduces the tunnelling current through the exponential in equation (6.1). The levelling-off is the trap population approaching saturation. This is negative feedback and it is not, by itself, a failure.

The second is hole trapping followed by breakdown. The slow rise is the positive feedback of §3.2 — anode hole injection putting positive charge near the cathode, raising the cathode field, raising the current, injecting more holes. The abrupt jump is the transition from the first stage of breakdown to the second, §5.1: defects have accumulated into a localised conductive path spanning the film, and the oxide is now shorted. The magnitude distinguishes the outcome — a jump to an intermediate current with a power-law characteristic afterwards is soft breakdown, while a jump to a much larger current with an ohmic characteristic is hard breakdown.

Summary

  • The Si–SiO₂ electron barrier is 3.1 eV, inside a 9 eV oxide gap. Fowler–Nordheim tunnelling crosses only the triangular part of the barrier, a distance φox/Eox, and then drifts; its signature is a straight line in log(J/Eox2) against 1/Eox. Direct tunnelling crosses the whole forbidden gap in one step and takes over at 4 nm and below, becoming very large below 3 nm.
  • The tunnelling limit is reached when gate-to-channel tunnelling equals off-state source-to-drain subthreshold leakage. At 10 nA/µm over a 1 µm² channel that is 10⁻⁸ A/µm², which is 1 A/cm² for a 1 µm gate length. Alternate gate dielectrics are required for 50 nm MOSFETs and beyond.
  • Trapped electrons lower the cathode field and suppress the current — negative feedback, current falls with time. Trapped holes, generated by impact ionisation in the oxide above 9 eV or by anode hole injection, raise the cathode field and enhance the current — positive feedback, current rises with time. Traps also act as stepping stones for two-step tunnelling.
  • SILC is low-field leakage left behind by high-field stress, caused by neutral oxide traps that assist tunnelling. It is transient in thicker oxides and continuous in thinner ones, and it limits non-volatile memory tunnel oxide scaling to about 8 nm at ten-year retention.
  • A 20-year lifetime requires the oxide field below 8 MV/cm; CMOS logic and memory restrict it to the 5 MV/cm range, and burn-in screens defective oxides. Breakdown has two stages: slow degradation forming a localised conductive path, then a very short runaway that shorts the oxide.
  • Polysilicon depletion forms a depleted layer of ionised donors at the poly–oxide interface, dropping voltage that no longer reaches the oxide or the substrate. Its capacitance is in series with Cox, and both terms move the wrong way as the oxide thins: Cox grows while Cpoly dep = εsiqNp/Qg falls. It bites below 10 nm of oxide and 2.5 V or lower, raises tox,eq by 3–4 Å at 5 MV/cm, lowers inversion charge, ID and speed. Heavier poly doping narrows it, bounded by the active doping limit — which is about three times lower for p⁺ than n⁺, so p-channel devices suffer more.
  • Six factors set the minimum thickness: reliability, tunnelling leakage, SILC, poly depletion, process damage and hot-carrier damage. Extrapolated at supply +10%, the defect-free thicknesses are 80, 45 and 33 Å for 5, 3.3 and 2.5 V supplies, and the published production figures are 110, 65 and 45 Å — an increase of 36 to 44%, not the 30% the rule names. Tunnelling leakage becomes excessive below 1.4 nm.
  • Deal–Grove fails below 200 Å for dry oxidation — the anomalous region — with five competing models and none accepted; Reisman and Nicollian's empirical tox = a(tg + τ)b fits the data instead. RTO grows at 800–1050 °C for seconds at 50–100 °C/s ramp rates, giving lower leakage, better boron resistance and higher mobility. ISSG generates H₂O, OH and atomic oxygen in the chamber below 20 Torr, with the rate rising as pressure falls.
  • Nitrogen relieves strained and dangling bonds: 1–2 atomic percent at the Si/SiO₂ interface for breakdown field and fixed charge, above 5 atomic percent at the poly/SiO₂ interface for boron penetration, and as little as possible in the bulk. NH₃-based ROXNOX introduces hydrogen and its attendant electron traps; N₂O and NO avoid it, with NO offering the lower thermal budget because N₂O must first dissociate above 850 °C and yields only about 4.7% NO against 64.3% N₂. Fluorine replaces Si–H with the stronger Si–F, but aggravates boron penetration in p-channel devices above a 1×10¹⁴ cm⁻² dose into the poly gate. A pre-oxidation nitrogen implant slows local oxidation and gives two oxide thicknesses from one oxidation.
