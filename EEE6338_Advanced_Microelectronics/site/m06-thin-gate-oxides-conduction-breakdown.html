Module 06

Thin Gate Oxides: Conduction and Breakdown

How electrons cross a film that is supposed to insulate, what that current does to the film, how the film eventually fails, and the thinnest silicon dioxide that any of this leaves usable. Then the growth techniques and the chemical modifications that were invented to push that limit down.

Prerequisites M05, M05b Read time ~75 min Sources Class notes pp. 29–48 (Chapter 3); Lectures 8–9; Wolf Vol. 4 ch. 3; Taur & Ning ch. 4; Plummer ch. 6

M05 treated the gate oxide as a dielectric whose thickness is set by what the transistor needs, and M05b treated it as a film that holds charge. Both assumed that no current flows through it, which is a good assumption for a 20 nm film and a false one for a 2 nm film. Silicon dioxide has a band gap near 9 eV and presents a 3.1 eV barrier to electrons at the silicon surface, so a thick film is an excellent insulator; but the probability of tunnelling through a barrier falls exponentially with its width, and by the time the film is ten atomic layers thick the barrier is narrow enough that electrons cross it continuously under ordinary operating bias.

That changes what the oxide is. It stops being a passive insulator and becomes a component that carries current, dissipates power and accumulates damage from the carriers passing through it. This module follows that sequence. It begins with the two tunnelling mechanisms and the thickness at which each takes over, then the criterion that converts a tunnelling current into a limit on how thin the film may be. It then treats what the current does on its way through — trapping electrons, generating and trapping holes, creating new traps — and how that damage accumulates into breakdown, followed by the depletion of the polysilicon gate, which thins the film electrically without any current at all. The second half is about the response: the growth techniques that make a 2 nm film worth having, the nitrogen and fluorine chemistries that strengthen it, and the point at which silicon dioxide runs out of margin and a different material becomes unavoidable, which is M07.

1 · How electrons cross an oxide

Two mechanisms carry electrons through a gate oxide, and the oxide thickness and the field across it decide which one operates. Both are quantum-mechanical tunnelling: the electron does not acquire enough energy to pass over the barrier; it passes through a classically forbidden region because its wavefunction has a non-zero amplitude on the far side. The probability falls off exponentially with the width of the forbidden region, which is why thickness matters more than any other variable.

Tunnelling itself does not damage the film. It is a current that flows while the bias is applied and stops when the bias is removed, and it is reversible in that sense. Dielectric breakdown, §5, is the opposite: a permanent loss of insulation after which the oxide conducts like a resistor. The two are connected only indirectly. A tunnelling current injects electrons that are trapped, generates holes that are trapped, and creates new traps (§3–§4), and it is that accumulated damage, not the tunnelling itself, that eventually ends in breakdown.

1.1 The barrier, drawn

A band diagram plots electron energy vertically against position through the structure horizontally; higher on the page means higher electron energy. Within each material the conduction band edge Ec and the valence band edge Ev bound the forbidden gap, and the Fermi level EF is the energy at which a state has a one-half probability of being occupied. In silicon the gap is 1.12 eV. The notes give the band gap of silicon dioxide as 8.9 eV, in the table of gate dielectrics that opens Chapter 4 (notes p. 49). That gap straddles the silicon gap asymmetrically: the oxide conduction band sits 3.1 eV above the silicon conduction band, and the oxide valence band sits 4.6 eV below the silicon valence band. The three steps close the oxide gap, 3.1 + 1.12 + 4.6 = 8.8 eV ≈ 9 eV, in agreement with the notes' 8.9 eV to within the precision of the offsets. The 3.1 eV conduction-band offset, not the 9 eV gap, is the barrier an electron in the silicon conduction band actually faces.

One notation clash needs settling. The notes' own flat-band diagram (p. 31), like Wolf's, labels the 3.1 eV barrier arrow Eox. There Eox is an energy, the barrier height. This module writes the barrier as qφox and keeps Eox for the oxide electric field in V/cm, which is how equation (6.1) and every field limit below use it.

The gate is n⁺ polysilicon rather than a metal, for the reasons given in M03 §8. Degenerate doping places its Fermi level at the conduction band edge, so as a source or sink of electrons it behaves much like a metal — with one exception, which §6 treats.

Applying a bias tilts the oxide bands. A positive gate voltage lowers the electron energy on the gate side, so the oxide conduction band slopes downward from the silicon toward the gate and the rectangular barrier of Figure 1 becomes a trapezoid. The slope is the oxide field Eox. The same positive bias bends the silicon bands down at the surface until the surface inverts: a thin layer of electrons collects in the potential well at the interface (M01 §2). Those inversion-layer electrons are the ones that tunnel, and how much of the trapezoid they must traverse separates the two conduction mechanisms.

1.2 Fowler–Nordheim tunnelling

When the oxide is thick enough and the field large enough, the sloping oxide conduction band drops below the electron's energy part-way across the film. The electron tunnels through the triangular part of the barrier only, emerges into the conduction band of the oxide, and is then accelerated by the oxide field toward the gate, where it is collected as gate current. This is Fowler–Nordheim tunnelling, abbreviated F–N. The distance actually tunnelled is φox/Eox, the barrier height divided by its slope, and it is shorter than the oxide thickness.

The notes' chain is the physically correct one, and it is the one this module follows.

1.3 Direct tunnelling

When the oxide is very thin, less than 4 nm in the notes, the electron reaches the gate before the sloping oxide conduction band has dropped to its energy. There is no triangular barrier to tunnel through and no flight in the oxide conduction band afterwards: electrons from the inverted silicon surface tunnel through the entire forbidden gap of the oxide in one step, directly into the gate. This is direct tunnelling.

The crossover is properly a condition, not a thickness. Fowler–Nordheim tunnelling requires the band edge to fall past the electron within the film, which happens when φox/Eox < tox — equivalently, when the voltage across the oxide exceeds the barrier, Vox > φox ≈ 3.1 V. Below that voltage the transport is direct whatever the thickness. Supply voltages fell below 3.1 V over the same generations in which oxides fell below 4 nm, so in practice the two statements coincide, and 4 nm is the thickness quoted.

The two mechanisms are not two regimes of one equation. Direct tunnelling has no simple dependence on voltage or field, because the barrier is trapezoidal and its width is fixed by the film rather than by the bias. What it has instead is an extreme sensitivity to thickness, since thickness is now exactly the tunnelling distance. The notes put the point at which the direct tunnelling current becomes very large at below 3 nm.

2 · The tunnelling limit on oxide thickness

Direct tunnelling puts a floor under the gate oxide thickness, and the floor can be located because there is a natural point of comparison. A MOSFET that is nominally off already leaks: subthreshold conduction carries a source-to-drain current below threshold, as M05 §3.1 described. Gate leakage becomes intolerable when it stops being small beside that existing leakage. The notes state the criterion: the limit is reached approximately when the gate-to-channel tunnelling current becomes equal to the off-state source-to-drain subthreshold leakage current.

Applying the criterion needs a number for the subthreshold leakage and a channel area over which the gate current is spread. Off-state leakage is specified per unit channel width, Ioff/W, because it scales with width; the notes take 10 nA/µm. Gate tunnelling current scales with channel area, W × L. Setting the gate current JmaxWL equal to the off-state current Ioff gives the ceiling:

Where the 1 A/cm² ceiling is actually crossed

The notes' 3 nm marks where direct tunnelling current becomes large; it is not where the current reaches 1 A/cm². On the leakage figures of §7.2 — 1×10⁻¹² A/cm² at 35 Å and, once the notes' transcription error is corrected, about 10 A/cm² at 15 Å — the current climbs roughly six to seven decades per nanometre, so a 3 nm film sits many decades below the ceiling. The measured gate current of Wolf's low-leakage oxide (§3.4.2, Fig. 3-21) crosses the 1 A/cm² line at 1.4 nm. Taur & Ning's milestones are 4 nm for the current beginning to rise sharply, 2 nm for direct tunnelling becoming important, and about 1.5 nm for the standby power becoming unacceptable. The span between 35 Å and 15 Å is quoted in the source as "twelve orders of magnitude", although its printed endpoints differ by thirteen; §7.2 traces both discrepancies.

3 · What the current does to the oxide

An electron crossing the oxide does not always leave it unchanged. Some electrons are captured by defects in the film; some generate holes, which are captured in turn; and the passage of current creates new defects that were not there before. Charge held in the film redistributes the field inside it, and because the tunnelling current depends exponentially on the field at the injecting electrode, each change feeds back on the current that caused it. The sign of the feedback differs between the two carriers, and that difference governs how an oxide behaves under stress.

Two terms are needed first. The cathode is the electrode acting as the electron source, and the anode is the electrode acting as the electron sink. Which physical layer plays which role depends on the bias polarity: under positive gate bias the inverted silicon surface is the cathode and the polysilicon gate is the anode, and under negative gate bias the roles reverse. The behaviour below is stated in terms of cathode and anode because it does not depend on which layer is playing each role.

Traps affect the current in two distinct ways, and they are kept apart here. In §3.1 and §3.2 the charge held in traps reshapes the barrier: it changes the field at the cathode, and with it the width of the triangular barrier an electron must tunnel through. Lecture 8's "tunnelling due to oxide traps" refers to this. In §3.3 the traps themselves carry the current, as intermediate states through which an electron crosses the film in two short steps; that is trap-assisted tunnelling, a different mechanism.

3.1 Electron trapping, and negative feedback

Electrons enter the oxide from the silicon substrate or from the polysilicon gate, either by the tunnelling of §1 or by hot-electron injection — an electron heated by the lateral electric field in the channel near the drain, arriving at the interface with enough kinetic energy to surmount the 3.1 eV barrier rather than tunnel through it. Some of these electrons are captured by electron traps already present in the film.

Trapped electrons are negative charge inside the dielectric, and they modify the field in it. The field near the cathode is decreased and the field near the anode is increased: the trapped negative charge terminates some of the field lines that would otherwise have run the full thickness of the film, while the total voltage across the film stays fixed. A smaller cathode field means a shallower slope of the oxide conduction band where the electrons enter, so the tunnelling distance φox/Eox of §1.2 lengthens: the barrier becomes broader.

A decrease in the field near the cathode causes the Fowler–Nordheim current to decrease, through the exponent of equation (6.1). This is negative feedback: the current fills traps, and the filled traps suppress the current. In a constant-voltage measurement the signature is a tunnelling current that falls with time. In the classic measurements of Lenzlinger and Snow the samples were first held at a current density of about 10⁻¹⁰ A/cm² for two hours, over which the current fell by about an order of magnitude; the Fowler–Nordheim data they reported were taken after that conditioning. How long the decrease runs before a more or less steady state is reached depends on the thickness of the film and on how it was grown.

3.2 Hole generation and trapping, and positive feedback

Holes matter for a reason beyond their charge. The notes open their treatment of hole trapping (p. 31) with it: the transport of holes through the oxide damages the film by creating traps in the bulk oxide. Hole transport has been shown to precede breakdown, so the holes are part of the degradation that §5 describes, not only a perturbation of the field. Holes reach the oxide by two routes.

The first route is impact ionisation inside the oxide. An electron travelling in the oxide conduction band gains kinetic energy from the field, and once that energy exceeds about 9 eV, measured from the bottom of the oxide conduction band — roughly the band gap of silicon dioxide — it can promote an oxide valence electron across the gap and create an electron–hole pair in the film. A Fowler–Nordheim electron emerges into the oxide conduction band with almost no kinetic energy, at the point where the sloping band edge has fallen qφox below its value at the cathode face, so crossing the rest of the film gives it at most q(Vox − φox). The ballistic condition is therefore Vox − φox ≳ 9 V, that is Vox ≳ 12 V for injection from silicon. That condition is necessary and not sufficient: electrons in the oxide conduction band lose energy continuously to optical phonons and reach a field-dependent steady-state energy distribution, so appreciable impact ionisation also requires a high field, above roughly 7–8 MV/cm. A thin film cannot hold the voltage: at 10 MV/cm a 5 nm oxide carries only 5 V, and it breaks down long before 12 V. Impact ionisation in the oxide is therefore confined to thick films, which is why the second route matters more in the films this module is about.

The second route is anode hole injection, and it operates at lower voltages. Figure 4 follows it step by step. An electron tunnels from the cathode into the oxide conduction band by Fowler–Nordheim tunnelling (1) and is accelerated across the film, arriving at the anode with a large kinetic energy (2). There it transfers that energy to an electron deep in the valence band of the anode close to the interface, exciting it to the conduction band and leaving a hole deep in the valence band (3). That hole is hot — far from the band edge in energy — and it can tunnel into the valence band of the oxide (4). It then drifts toward the cathode and is captured by a hole trap near it (5).

Trapped holes are positive charge, so they tilt the field the opposite way from trapped electrons: the oxide field near the cathode increases and the field near the anode decreases. A steeper band at the cathode shortens the tunnelling distance: the barrier becomes narrower. Because the tunnelling current depends exponentially on the cathode field, a small increase there causes a large increase in current. Hole trapping near the cathode therefore provides positive feedback to the electron tunnelling process — more current injects more holes, which raise the cathode field, which drives more current. The underlying tendency is a current that rises with time.

The two signatures are not separable in a real measurement

The sign of dI/dt under constant-voltage stress does not identify which mechanism is running. Electron trapping and hole trapping proceed at the same time, and the decrease in tunnelling current due to electron trapping usually masks the increase due to hole trapping. A constant-voltage trace on a 20 nm oxide typically falls for the whole stress and then jumps at breakdown; the rising phase becomes visible only close to breakdown.

Anode hole injection is itself a model rather than a settled result. It has never been completely verified, photons generated by the Fowler–Nordheim current in the gate have been proposed as the dominant source of the measured substrate hole current, and the account of oxide degradation built on anode hole injection may yet be revised. It is presented above as mechanism because it is the account the notes follow and the one the degradation literature is organised around. Wolf, Vol. 4 §3.3.4.

3.3 Trap-assisted tunnelling

Here the traps change the transport rather than the field. An electron need not cross the whole oxide in one step: it can tunnel from the cathode into a trap inside the oxide, and then tunnel from that trap to the anode. The trap splits one long tunnelling step into two short ones, and since the tunnelling probability falls exponentially with distance, two short steps are far more probable than the single long one they replace. Wolf draws such trap levels as short bars inside the oxide gap, alongside the direct paths of Figures 2 and 3.

The enhanced current generates more traps, which provide more intermediate states. This is a second positive feedback loop, independent of the hole-trapping one, and it connects an oxide carrying current to an oxide that eventually fails. In its low-field form it is the mechanism of §4. What the traps are at the atomic level — the oxygen vacancy and the E′ centre it becomes on capturing a hole, and the hydrogen-related sites — is the subject of M05b §5.

4 · Stress-induced leakage current

When a thin oxide is exposed to high electric-field stress, and therefore to Fowler–Nordheim gate current, it afterwards conducts more current at low field than it did before. The stress has changed the film, and the change shows up as leakage under ordinary operating conditions. This effect is stress-induced leakage current, abbreviated SILC; it was first reported in 1987. The notes compress the definition into "Fowler Nordheim gate current increases. This is termed as SILC"; the increase that carries the name is the low-field leakage measured after the stress, not the stress current itself.

The mechanism is trap generation followed by trap-assisted tunnelling. High-field stress current generates neutral oxide traps — defect sites that are uncharged while empty, so their creation alone does not shift the threshold voltage the way the four charges of M05b §1 do, although each becomes charged, and shifts it, while it holds a captured carrier. What matters for leakage is that they help electrons tunnel by the two-step process of §3.3: the added current comes from the denser population of intermediate states, not from charge stored in the film.

SILC behaves differently in thick and thin films, and the difference is whether the traps stay filled. In thicker oxides SILC is a transient: the leakage decays as the traps fill, because an electron in a trap does not readily tunnel out again across the thick remainder of the film, and a full trap is not an intermediate state. In thinner oxides the leakage is a continuous current: tunnelling out of a trap is as easy as tunnelling in, and a steady state is reached in which trap filling and trap emptying balance.

SILC is a particular problem for non-volatile memory. Such a device stores data as charge on a floating gate and moves that charge through a thin tunnel oxide at high field — by Fowler–Nordheim tunnelling in both directions in EEPROM and NAND flash, and for erasure in NOR flash, which programs by hot-electron injection. It therefore inflicts high-field stress on its own dielectric as part of normal operation, in program/erase cycles repeated over the life of the part, and Wolf records that SILC grows under those cycles. The resulting leakage lets the stored charge escape and reduces data retention. The requirement is usually ten years of retention, and SILC makes non-volatile memory tunnel-oxide scaling much below 8 nm difficult unless that ten-year requirement is relaxed.

Soft breakdown — beyond the notes

Below about 5 nm a further failure mode appears between SILC and the catastrophic breakdown of §5. Stressing a thin oxide beyond a critical field turns the SILC into soft breakdown, also called quasi-breakdown: a sudden but modest jump in gate current, with a post-breakdown current–voltage characteristic that follows a power law rather than becoming ohmic. In 2.5 nm oxides stressed at 4.1 V and 140 °C, soft breakdown raises the gate current to around 10⁻⁴ A while hard breakdown raises it to around 10⁻² A. Soft breakdown is triggered once the oxide field exceeds about 10.7–11.0 MV/cm, and it becomes softer and less abrupt as the oxide is thinned. Hard breakdown dominates in thicker films and is often not observed at all in thin gate oxides. Wolf, Vol. 4 §3.8.3.

5 · Dielectric breakdown

Breakdown is the terminal failure: the oxide stops being a dielectric and becomes a conductor, permanently. The notes state the requirement plainly — dielectric breakdown in integrated circuits must be avoided — because a single shorted gate oxide destroys the transistor that contains it, and a circuit has hundreds of millions of them sharing one process and one supply voltage.

The engineering constraint is a field limit tied to a lifetime. A twenty-year lifetime can be achieved if the oxide field remains below 8 MV/cm. For CMOS logic and memory chips the typical operating field is restricted to the 5 MV/cm range. The margin between the two is set by the failure statistics: time-to-breakdown falls steeply with field, so a field somewhat above the design point shortens the lifetime by far more than proportionally, and 5 MV/cm leaves room for 20–30-year lifetimes.

Field limits govern the intrinsic population — the oxides that are as good as the process can make them. A separate population fails early because of localised defects, and no field limit screens those out. They are removed by burn-in: finished, packaged chips are operated at elevated temperature and elevated voltage for a period, then electrically re-tested, and the parts that fail are discarded. The stress accelerates the failure of any part containing an oxide that would have failed under normal operating conditions during its service life. Burn-in repairs nothing; it converts a field failure years later into a factory failure now.

5.1 The two stages

The notes describe dielectric breakdown as a two-stage process with very different time scales.

In the first stage the oxide is slowly degraded over time, either by the high electric field across it or by the passage of current through it. The processes are the ones of §3 and §4: electron and hole trapping, trap generation, bond breakage. These defects accumulate until they form a localised conductive path through the oxide. This stage can take years and occupies essentially the whole life of the device. Because the breakdown arrives only after the film has been held under stress for an extended time, the phenomenon is called time-dependent dielectric breakdown, TDDB, and the first stage is the part that takes the time.

The second stage is the very short runaway breakdown process, which may take microseconds. Once a conductive path exists, current concentrates in it, severe Joule heating forms a conductive filament, the oxide is shorted, and a very large current flows between the gate and the substrate. The notes draw the result as a poly short: a filament of polysilicon joining the gate to the substrate through the oxide. The abrupt transition from the first stage to the second is why oxide lifetime is characterised as a time-to-breakdown rather than as a gradual drift in a device parameter.

Lecture 8 presented breakdown as two types: slow degradation with time under a high field, and current through the oxide causing a runaway through a short. The two accounts fit together. High field and current are the two drivers of the first stage — Wolf treats them as the two classes of TDDB model, field-driven and current-driven — and the runaway through the short is the second stage, which ends every hard breakdown whichever of them drove the first. In films thinner than about 5 nm the completed path may instead give the soft breakdown of §4, a modest current jump with no runaway.

The lecture's drawing adds where the path forms. At a defect or a locally thin spot the same voltage falls across less oxide, so the local field E = V/t is higher: a spot 30% thinner than its surroundings carries 1/0.7 ≈ 1.4 times the field. Degradation runs fastest there, and the short forms there. This is also the reason §7.1 adds a thickness margin for defects.

6 · Polysilicon depletion

Every band diagram so far has treated the polysilicon gate as an ideal equipotential region: a perfect conductor whose potential is uniform, so that the whole applied gate voltage appears across the oxide and the silicon. The notes state the rule that this idealisation hides. Polysilicon depletion exists whenever the MOS capacitor is biased into depletion or inversion. It can be ignored by treating the gate as an ideal equipotential region only as long as ignoring it has insignificant effects on device behaviour. For a thick gate oxide that holds; for a thin one it does not, because polysilicon is a semiconductor and a semiconductor can be depleted. Unlike everything in §1–§5, this effect involves no current at all: it thickens the oxide electrically.

6.1 Where the depletion region comes from

Consider an n⁺ polysilicon gate on a p-type silicon substrate, with a positive bias applied between gate and substrate. The bias causes depletion, or depletion and then inversion, in the substrate, as M01 §2 described: negative charge is induced on the silicon side. Charge neutrality requires an equal positive charge on the gate side of the oxide.

In a metal that charge is supplied by the electron density rearranging over an atomic distance, and nothing else happens. In n⁺ polysilicon it is supplied by mobile, negatively charged electrons leaving the region of the gate next to the oxide, which exposes the fixed positive donor ions they were compensating. Those ionised donors constitute the positive charge, and because the donor concentration is finite, exposing enough of them requires emptying a layer of finite thickness. A depletion region forms in the polysilicon at the poly/oxide interface. This is the polysilicon depletion effect, abbreviated PDE.

A depletion region contains uncompensated charge, so the bands bend across it, so an electric field exists in it, so a voltage is dropped across it. In the n⁺ gate the bands bend upward toward the oxide. Figures 10 and 11 draw the same capacitor at the same applied bias, first with the gate treated as ideal and then with the depletion layer included, labelled as the notes label them (p. 45).

6.2 What it costs

The voltage dropped across the poly depletion region is taken from the sum available to the oxide and the substrate, so the remaining voltage is smaller than it would be if the gate were an ideal conductor. The notes carry the chain through: the reduced voltage induces a smaller inversion-layer charge density; drain current is proportional to that charge, so ID is degraded; and the speed of the MOSFET falls with it.

At any given bias the effect on inversion charge is the same as if the gate oxide had been made thicker. Polysilicon depletion increases the equivalent oxide thickness tox,eq, the thickness of an ideal oxide that would give the capacitance actually measured. M07 uses the same quantity for the opposite purpose: there a physically thick high-permittivity film behaves like a thin oxide; here a physically thin oxide behaves like a thick one. The equivalence is bias-dependent rather than a fixed offset, because the poly depletion layer has zero width at flat band and widens as the gate charge grows.

The depletion layer in the gate is a capacitor in series with the gate oxide capacitor. No metal electrode exists at the poly/oxide interface, but the equivalent circuit is still valid: the two notional plates there, the "virtual electrodes", carry equal and opposite charge, and their effects cancel.

The shape of the C–V curve — beyond the notes

In inversion the curve rises to a local maximum somewhat below Cox and then falls again, rather than settling at a reduced plateau, because Cpolydep keeps decreasing as the gate charge grows with gate voltage. The signature is not by itself diagnostic: quantisation of the inversion layer depresses the same curve for a similar structural reason — the inversion charge centroid sits a finite depth below the surface, which adds its own series capacitance — with no polysilicon involved. The mitigation follows from the mechanism. In the notes' words, the higher the polysilicon doping concentration, the narrower the poly depletion region, and the smaller its effect: more donors per unit volume supply the required charge from a thinner layer, whose capacitance is larger. Heavier gate doping is therefore the direct fix. It is bounded by how much dopant can be made electrically active in polysilicon — about 10²⁰ cm⁻³ for n-type and only the mid-to-upper 10¹⁹ cm⁻³ for p-type, which is why p⁺ gates suffer polysilicon depletion worse than n⁺ gates — and, for those same p⁺ gates, by the boron penetration problem that §9 addresses.

7 · The minimum usable oxide thickness

Everything to this point converges on one engineering question: how thin the gate oxide can actually be made in production. The notes list six factors that play a role in setting that minimum, each treated in this module or the last:

  1. Oxide reliability — the time-to-breakdown of §5.
  2. Tunnelling leakage current — §1 and §2.
  3. Stress-induced leakage current — §4.
  4. Polysilicon depletion — §6.
  5. Process-induced oxide damage — §10.
  6. Hot-carrier-induced damage — §3.1, and the breaking of Si–H bonds by hot carriers in M05b §4.3.

The answer is not a single number, because the type of application determines which factors bind. The notes name three types — logic devices, DRAMs and non-volatile memories — and each tolerates a different failure, so each settles at a different thickness. A non-volatile memory is limited by SILC and data retention, as §4 showed, at thicknesses a logic device would find unremarkable; a DRAM is expected to stop at about 3 nm, since logic circuits can withstand more leakage than a DRAM's stored charge can.

8 · Growing an oxide this thin

A 2 nm gate oxide is not a thin version of a 100 nm one. The growth model that describes thick oxides fails in this regime, and the batch furnace that grows thick oxides well becomes very hard to control below about 2 nm. The model was answered by empirical fits and a corrected form; the furnace by single-wafer rapid thermal oxidation, which grew up as the alternative while batch furnaces still grew most gate oxides. This section takes them in that order and ends with the wet process that extended silicon dioxide furthest.

8.1 The Deal–Grove model and its anomalous region

Thermal oxidation, introduced in M01 §7, proceeds by the oxidant — O₂ in dry oxidation, H₂O in wet — diffusing through the oxide already grown and reacting with silicon at the Si/SiO₂ interface. The interface therefore moves down into the substrate and the film grows from the bottom, consuming silicon as it goes: about 0.46 of the final oxide thickness, the ratio M05a §4 derives from the atom densities of silicon and its oxide (Plummer rounds it to 0.45). Deal and Grove modelled the two steps in series — oxidant transport through the oxide, then reaction at the interface — and obtained a growth law for the thickness against time [B. E. Deal and A. S. Grove, J. Appl. Phys. 36, 3770 (1965)]:

tox2 + A tox = B(t + τ)
(6.5)
tox oxide thickness (µm) · t oxidation time (hr) · B parabolic rate constant (µm²/hr), set by oxidant diffusion through the oxide · B/A linear rate constant (µm/hr), set by the reaction at the interface · A (µm) · τ (hr) shift of the time origin that accounts for an oxide of thickness ti present at the start, τ = (ti2 + Ati)/B. Both rate constants depend on temperature (Arrhenius) and on the ambient, and doping affects them; silicon orientation affects only the linear constant B/A, the interface reaction — B, set by diffusion through the amorphous oxide, is the same for all orientations. For a thin film the linear term dominates: tox ≈ (B/A)(t + τ), linear growth, reaction-limited. For a thick film the square term dominates: tox2 ≈ B(t + τ), parabolic growth, diffusion-limited. Growth on a bare wafer therefore starts linear and becomes parabolic as the oxide thickens.

In the notes' words, the Deal–Grove model allows the thickness of oxide films thicker than 350 Å to be well predicted as a function of temperature, furnace ambient, silicon doping concentration and silicon orientation. It was developed for the much thicker oxides of the 1960s. The notes then locate its limit: the model does not give a detailed understanding of the oxidation mechanism for oxides thinner than 200 Å, specifically for dry oxidation. This is called the anomalous region of the Deal–Grove model.

Wolf and Plummer add what goes wrong numerically. In that range dry-O₂ oxidation runs faster than the model predicts — Deal and Grove themselves found that the model did not fit the first 20 nm or so of dry growth — so the model returns the wrong thickness, not only an unexplained one. Wolf also notes that the model is a rate-constant description that does not explain the mechanisms behind its dependencies at any thickness.

350 Å or 250 Å

Lecture 8 gave the lower limit of the Deal–Grove model's validity as 250 Å. The notes (p. 33) and Wolf (§3.5) give 350 Å, and 350 Å is the course's figure. Between 200 Å and 350 Å the notes make no claim either way: the model is not said to predict well there, and the anomalous region is not said to begin until 200 Å.

Several models have been proposed for the anomalous region. The notes list five, and each carries one idea:

  1. Space charge. The enhancement is field-assisted oxidant diffusion arising from O₂⁻ diffusing coupled with holes. If the diffusing oxidant were the ion O₂⁻, charge neutrality would require positive carriers, holes, to diffuse with it. Holes diffuse faster, run ahead, and the charge separation sets up a field that slows the holes and pulls the O₂⁻ forward. The field extends about one extrinsic Debye length, which comes out near 15 nm for dry O₂ — matching the thickness over which growth is fast. H₂O is about 10³ times more soluble in the oxide, which shrinks the Debye length below 1 nm, so wet oxidation should show no anomaly: this is why the anomalous region is specific to dry oxidation. Plummer records that the premise, O₂⁻ as the diffusing species, has since been largely discounted.
  2. Structural defects. Micropores or channels about 10 Å in diameter in the oxide let oxygen molecules diffuse more rapidly while the oxide is thin.
  3. Parallel O₂ and O reactions at the Si/SiO₂ interface — molecular and atomic oxygen reacting through separate pathways.
  4. A blocking layer near the Si/SiO₂ interface forms as the oxide grows and slows the rate for thicker layers. Here the thin-oxide rate is the undisturbed one and the thick-oxide rate is the slowed one.
  5. Massoud's model: a thin surface layer in the silicon where additional reaction sites are available, their concentration decaying exponentially with depth over a characteristic length of about 7 nm.

None of them has been convincingly accepted as more valid than the others. Two responses followed, and they differ in kind.

8.2 Single-wafer technology: rapid thermal oxidation

The notes head this part "Single Wafer Technology of Thin Oxide Growth", and the heading is the point. Rapid thermal oxidation is a single-wafer technology: one wafer at a time in a small lamp-heated chamber, because each cycle lasts seconds, where a conventional furnace oxidises a batch of wafers standing in a quartz boat for minutes to hours.

Four terms name the same kind of tool. Rapid thermal processing, RTP, is the general technique: a single wafer heated by lamps at tens to hundreds of °C per second, held at temperature for seconds, and cooled as fast. Run in an oxidising ambient it is rapid thermal oxidation, RTO; run as an anneal it is rapid thermal annealing, RTA; run in a nitrogen-bearing ambient such as NH₃, N₂O or NO it performs the nitridation of §9, and is called rapid thermal nitridation. The notes use RTO and RTP; Lecture 9 used RTA for all of them.

The notes give two problems of conventional furnace oxidation at these thicknesses: furnace oxidation is difficult to scale below 2 nm, and the low temperatures used to slow growth enough for such ultra-thin films degrade the oxide quality. RTO answers both. It grows the oxides at higher temperatures, where film quality is good, and keeps the total thickness small by keeping the oxidation time to just a few seconds, where the minimum practical oxidation time in a batch furnace is at least a few minutes. And it is conducive to cluster tooling, below.

High-pressure oxidation — beyond the notes

Lecture 9 described the route RTO displaced as batch oxidation at high oxygen pressure and reduced temperature, and said that at atmospheric pressure no growth would occur at those temperatures. Neither statement is in the notes, which describe the conventional route as a low-temperature batch furnace. The second conflicts with the data: at 800 °C and 1 atm the Reisman fit of §8.1 grows 4–5 nm in an hour — 4.35 nm on the 1.85 nm starting layer of the example, and 0.302 × 600.704 = 5.4 nm from bare silicon — slow, but growth. Raising the pressure raises the growth rate roughly in proportion to the oxidant partial pressure, and Plummer describes high-pressure oxidation as a way to grow thick oxides with a small thermal budget. Plummer, Deal & Griffin §6.3; Wolf Vol. 4 §3.5.

The sequence is short. A robot wafer handler inserts the wafer onto a silicon carbide rotating support ring. The wafer is heated rapidly, at 50–100 °C/s, to the process temperature, which the notes give as 800–1000 °C; at those rates the ramp from room temperature to 1000 °C takes 10 to 20 seconds. Process gases are introduced at reduced pressure in an axial flow pattern, where they react to form water vapour, 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O(g). Other gases used are N₂, NH₃, HCl or other reactants, and which species form, and in what proportion, depends on the hydrogen-to-oxygen ratio and on those additions. At the conclusion of the oxidation step the wafer is cooled rapidly.

Three temperature windows for the same process

The notes give the RTO process temperature as 800–1000 °C (p. 36), and that is the course's figure. Two other windows are in circulation. Wolf's sentence, which the notes condense, reads 800–1050 °C, and the notes' own first advantage, one page earlier, puts the leakage optimum at 1050 °C — the top of Wolf's range, and outside the notes'. Lecture 9 gave 1000–1050 °C as the working window and 1050 °C as a ceiling. The notes' 1000 °C coincides with the upper temperature of the boron-penetration and mobility studies in advantages 2 and 3 below. Physically, leakage keeps improving up to 1050 °C, so Wolf's wider window is the one consistent with advantage 1.

Growing at higher temperature is not merely tolerable; the notes list three advantages:

  1. Oxides exhibit smaller gate leakage currents as their growth temperature increases, up to 1050 °C, beyond which the leakage current increases.
  2. They show higher resistance to boron penetration. A 2 nm oxide grown at 800 °C was compared with one grown by rapid thermal processing at 1000 °C, and boron penetration was suppressed in the RTP-grown oxide.
  3. MOSFETs built with RTP-grown oxides had higher effective carrier mobilities, for both electrons and holes, as the growth temperature increased from 800 to 1000 °C. This is due to the smoother Si/SiO₂ interface the higher temperature produces: surface-roughness scattering is one of the mechanisms that degrade inversion-layer mobility, as M01 §4 noted.

Cluster tools produce better-quality oxides because they allow process sequences to be carried out in an integrated fashion. A cluster tool is a set of single-wafer process chambers arranged around a central transfer chamber held under vacuum, in which a robot moves the wafer from chamber to chamber without exposing it to air. The specific gain the notes name is cleaning: an oxide growth process requires a clean before the growth step, and if that clean can be performed in situ, with no exposure to air between cleaning and growth, the quality of the oxide improves. Lecture 9 drew the gate-stack version: oxidation, then nitridation, then polysilicon deposition, each in its own chamber. Cluster tools suit single-wafer processing and are impractical for batch furnaces.

8.3 Wet RTO and in-situ steam generation

The notes give the minimum thickness of dry oxides as 1.6–2.5 nm. Wet rapid thermal oxidation is one of the techniques explored to push below that limit. Applied Materials developed the technique called in-situ steam generation, ISSG, which in the notes' words allowed oxides in the 2.0 nm range; Wolf reports it as giving 2.0 nm oxides with better reliability than dry oxides of the same thickness.

ISSG is a low-pressure oxidation, below 20 Torr, performed in a cold-wall RTP reactor. The distinguishing feature is where the steam comes from. A conventional hot-wall furnace generates its wet ambient in a pyrogenic torch upstream and pipes it in. In ISSG no pre-combustion occurs: electronic-grade hydrogen and oxygen are premixed and injected, the hot wafer acts as the ignition switch, and H₂O, OH radicals and atomic oxygen are generated directly in the reaction chamber at the wafer surface. Wolf gives the pressure limit as a safety measure: what is being contained is a premixed charge of hydrogen and oxygen in a hot chamber. The hydrogen fraction is a process variable, and the wide range of temperatures and hydrogen concentrations available allows controlled oxidation at any thickness.

Two properties of the process are unusual. The oxidation rate rapidly increases as the pressure is decreased below 20 Torr — the opposite of conventional oxidation, where both Deal–Grove rate constants scale roughly linearly with the oxidant partial pressure. And the oxidation rate shows a strong correlation to the atomic oxygen concentration and not to any other atomic or molecular species, which identifies atomic oxygen as the reactive agent. The oxidation is also strongly temperature dependent. ISSG oxides demonstrate better reliability than comparable furnace oxides.

9 · Changing the chemistry of the film

Growing pure silicon dioxide better eventually stops being enough. The next response was to stop growing pure silicon dioxide: adding nitrogen, or fluorine, changes the bonding of the film at its interfaces in ways that address specific failure modes — boron penetration, interface defects, hot-carrier damage. These are the last modifications before the material itself is replaced in M07.

9.1 Why nitrogen helps

Nitridation of SiO₂ uses a nitrogen-bearing ambient. The notes name four: ammonia, NH₃; nitrous oxide, N₂O; nitric oxide, NO; or a mixture of O₂ and NO. Nitrous oxide and nitric oxide are different molecules — N₂O carries two nitrogen atoms and one oxygen, NO one of each — and they behave differently in the process, §9.3. Molecular nitrogen, N₂, is not on the list: held together by a triple bond, it is too inert to nitride the film at these temperatures, which is also why N₂ serves as an inert furnace ambient.

The film that results is a silicon oxynitride, written SiOxNy: silicon dioxide carrying a few atomic percent of nitrogen, concentrated at the faces of the film rather than spread through it. It is not silicon nitride, Si₃N₄, which is a different dielectric (M07). The notes list four advantages of such films over SiO₂ as MOSFET gate dielectrics below 4 nm:

  1. They exhibit improved boron penetration resistance. Boron from a p⁺ polysilicon gate diffuses through a thin gate oxide into the channel and shifts the threshold voltage — one of the negative factors of a very thin oxide in M05 §3.2.
  2. They exhibit better breakdown characteristics.
  3. They show greater immunity to hot-electron injection effects.
  4. MOSFETs made with them exhibit improved high-field channel mobility. Wolf makes the claim for electrons.

The mechanism is interfacial. In the notes' words, the improved performance of nitrided SiO₂ has been associated with the accumulation of a small amount of nitrogen at the SiO₂/Si interface, and interfacial nitrogen accumulation reduces interfacial defects by relieving strained Si–O bonds and dangling bonds. Wolf, from whom the notes take the sentence, words the mechanism as a hypothesis: interfacial nitrogen "is thought to" reduce those defects. Both defects come from the mismatch between the crystal and the amorphous oxide on it (M05b §3): where the bond positions of the silicon surface do not line up with the oxide network, some Si–O bridges are stretched out of their equilibrium geometry and some silicon bonds are left unsatisfied — dangling — when oxidation stops.

One reading of why nitrogen helps — the one Lecture 9 drew, and the one Figure 17 follows — is the number of bonds it forms. Oxygen forms two bonds, so it can only bridge two silicon atoms, at a bond length and angle the network must accommodate. Nitrogen forms three. A single nitrogen atom at the interface can bond to the substrate silicon that would otherwise keep a dangling bond and to two silicon atoms of the oxide, taking the place of an oxygen bridge that would have had to be stretched to reach. Lecture 9 drew the first half at the board: the interfacial silicon left with an unsatisfied bond when oxidation stopped is bonded to nitrogen, the dangling bond becomes a Si–N bond, and the interface-trap density falls. Hydrogen and fluorine terminate a dangling bond with a single bond and go no further; nitrogen terminates it while still bonding into the network. M05b §4.4 compares the terminators.

The amount of nitrogen matters, and so does which face of the film it sits on. An interfacial peak of about 1–2 atomic percent at the Si/SiO₂ interface is typically associated with a higher breakdown field and a lower fixed oxide charge density. Suppressing boron penetration needs a higher concentration, above about 5 atomic percent, and it is needed at the opposite face — the poly/SiO₂ interface, because that is where boron enters the film from the p⁺ gate above it. Nitrogen in the bulk of the film degrades the oxide and is kept out, so an engineered stack places nitrogen in two thin layers with as little as possible between them.

What nitrogen does to interface traps and mobility, measured

Lecture 9 summarised the benefit as a lower interface-trap density and, from it, a higher channel mobility. The measurements in the notes (pp. 39–40, for films grown in N₂O, §9.3) qualify that. As grown, the oxynitride has larger initial Dit and somewhat higher Qf than pure oxide, and its peak electron mobility is about 5% lower. What improves is behaviour under stress: interface traps are generated much more slowly under hot-carrier or Fowler–Nordheim injection, so ΔDit, the increase during stress, is much smaller; and the mobility is higher at high field, by about 10% in Wolf's example. The gain is hardness against damage and high-field mobility, not a cleaner interface as grown. Notes pp. 39–40; Wolf Vol. 4 §3.7.1, Figs. 3-30 and 3-31.

9.2 Ammonia nitridation, and its hydrogen problem

Early oxynitride films were made from ammonia, in three steps:

  1. Grow the oxide thermally — in Lecture 9's version, by RTO in O₂ or in H₂/O₂.
  2. Nitride the oxide by exposing it to an NH₃ ambient at high temperature, briefly.
  3. Re-oxidise the nitrided oxide, to reduce the amount of hydrogen left in the film by the NH₃ nitridation.

The product is a reoxidized nitrided oxide, ROXNOX: RE-OXidized Nitrided OXide, the expansion in the notes (p. 38) and in Wolf. Lecture 9's captions render it as "re-oxidized nitrous oxide"; the process uses ammonia, and the hydrogen-free gases N₂O and NO are the subject of §9.3. The notes file ROXNOX under the heading "Oxynitridation of SiO₂ in N₂O", where it serves as the problem N₂O solves. ROXNOX films exhibit better hot-carrier resistance than pure SiO₂, but the process is difficult to optimise, because the re-oxidation has to correct a problem the nitridation created without going too far. Ammonia carries hydrogen, and the notes list the consequences:

  • The ROXNOX process may leave excess hydrogen, giving a higher density of electron traps; hydrogen-bearing species left in the oxide are a source of electron traps, as the hydroxyl case in M05b §4.2 shows. Electron trapping is the dominant degradation mechanism in p-channel MOSFETs, which therefore show higher hot-carrier degradation.
  • If the re-oxidation step is excessive, the film loses strength against interface-state generation by hot carriers, and n-channel MOSFETs containing such films are prone to more hot-carrier degradation.
  • NH₃-nitrided films always exhibit higher densities of positive fixed charge Qf than the best SiO₂ films — the comparison is between the best of each.
  • The ROXNOX process involves multiple high-temperature steps, a thermal budget paid by every dopant profile already in the wafer.

Wolf records the outcome: ultra-thin oxynitrides are not formed in production with NH₃ as the nitrogen source.

9.3 Nitrous oxide and nitric oxide

The hydrogen can be avoided by replacing NH₃ with nitrous oxide, N₂O, as the nitriding ambient, since N₂O contains no hydrogen at all.

The first approach grows the dielectric by reacting N₂O directly with the silicon surface in an RTP system, in one step. Less hydrogen is incorporated. Secondary ion mass spectrometry, SIMS — which sputters the film away layer by layer and mass-analyses the ejected ions, giving concentration against depth — shows both less hydrogen in the film and a nitrogen profile that peaks at the Si/SiO₂ interface, which is where §9.1 wants it; Wolf gives the peak as about 0.5 atomic percent for this process. The thickness increases approximately as (time)1/2, which indicates that growth is limited by diffusion of N₂O through the oxide bulk — the parabolic, diffusion-limited regime of eq. (6.5).

n-channel MOSFETs fabricated with these films exhibited a low electron-trapping density, less hot-carrier degradation than those made with control oxides, and smaller ΔDit. The notes list four limitations against pure oxide films:

  1. Larger initial values of Dit.
  2. Higher initial values of Qf.
  3. Slow growth makes these films practical only for ULSI applications, which use dielectric films thinner than 100 Å.
  4. The maximum electron mobility in MOSFETs made with these films is about 5% lower than with oxide; the mobility is higher at large fields. The notes and Wolf both write "lateral" field, but Wolf's figure plots mobility against the effective normal field, the transverse field of the gate — which is the field meant.

Wolf adds a fifth limitation that the notes omit, and calls it the most severe: RTP-grown N₂O films show unacceptably large non-uniformity in composition and thickness across the wafer.

The two-step process

The alternative separates growth from nitridation. Oxides are first grown in O₂, and the films are then subjected to an oxynitridation step in N₂O or NO. The notes list three ways the films have been grown: by furnace at atmospheric pressure, at low pressure, and by RTP.

The notes print a second published furnace recipe beside the drawing: 50 Å of SiO₂ grown at 850 °C for 30 min, then N₂O annealing at 950 °C that grows a further 35 Å, for an 85 Å film. The N₂O step both nitrides and oxidises, so the thickness is budgeted across both steps. The 900 °C of the figure and the 850 °C of this recipe belong to two different processes.

The low-pressure process runs at 1–50 Torr of N₂O: a minimum N₂O pressure somewhere in that range is needed before a measurable amount of nitrogen is incorporated at all. Just above that onset the interfacial nitrogen can be controlled while almost no additional oxide grows during the nitridation step, so the final thickness stays under tight control.

Using N₂O as the nitrogen source in the second step has a disadvantage: the nitrogen it incorporates is too low for some applications, especially suppression of boron penetration, unless a much higher thermal budget is permitted. More recently NO has been used as the source in the second step. It offers a low thermal budget for incorporating a higher concentration of nitrogen, as well as better thickness control for scaled dielectrics.

The chemistry explains why. Nitrogen incorporation from N₂O is driven by the NO species, which is a product of N₂O dissociation — N₂O works only by becoming NO first. In the notes' words, NO incorporates nitrogen while O₂ continues the oxidation by reacting with silicon, and N₂O dissociation needs a temperature above 850 °C. Wolf gives the dissociation products, from a calculation: NO about 4.7%, N₂ about 64.3%, O₂ about 31%. Nitrogen, nearly two-thirds of the gas, dilutes the partial pressure of the nitriding species and drives the thermal budget up; only 4.7% becomes the species that nitrides. Supplying NO directly avoids both the dissociation temperature and the dilution. With RTP, the percentage of nitrogen incorporated depends on the ratio of the NO + O₂ mixture. A published furnace recipe: a 4 nm oxynitride is formed by growing 4 nm of oxide in O₂ at 800 °C with 6% HCl and a post-oxidation anneal in N₂, then annealing the oxide in a diluted NO ambient at 800 °C for 45 min.

9.4 Fluorinated gate oxides

Incorporating fluorine into the gate oxide increases hot-electron resistance. The reason is a bond-strength argument that follows on from the hydrogen passivation of M05b §4: fluorine atoms form Si–F bonds, which are stronger than Si–H bonds, and the presence of fluorine replaces Si–H bonds at the Si/SiO₂ interface. A hot carrier with enough energy to break a Si–H bond and create an interface trap does not have enough to break the Si–F bond.

Fluorine may be introduced inadvertently, by two common process steps:

  1. A BF₂⁺ source/drain implant, where boron is implanted as the BF₂ molecular ion and the fluorine comes with it.
  2. An LPCVD-W or LPCVD-WSix process, where WF₆ and SiH₄ are the reactants — tungsten hexafluoride carries six fluorine atoms per tungsten atom into the chamber.

The benefit has a cost that runs against §9.1, and it falls on only half of a CMOS pair. Fluorine in the gate oxide may increase boron penetration from p⁺ polysilicon gates, a potential problem for deep-submicron p-channel MOSFETs; n-channel devices, whose gates are n⁺, get the hot-carrier benefit without the penalty. The two effects are separable by dose. The notes: if the fluorine dose during implantation is kept below 1×10¹⁴ cm⁻², no noticeable enhanced boron penetration is observed. The study Wolf reports used 1×10¹⁴ cm⁻² itself, which he calls a medium dose, so the source supports "at or below" where the notes say "below". Wolf also specifies that it is the dose implanted into the polysilicon gate: the BF₂⁺ extension implant lands in the poly, and the fluorine diffuses from there into the gate oxide during the activation anneals. At that dose the charge-to-breakdown of 4 nm oxides on p-channel capacitors is significantly improved.

9.5 Two oxide thicknesses on one wafer

Not every transistor on a chip wants the thinnest possible oxide. High voltage may be used for analogue and input/output circuitry, which requires thicker oxides to withstand it; logic uses the thinner gate oxide that gives drive current and speed. Both are built on the same wafer, which requires a dual gate oxide thickness structure. The notes head the process "2 step oxidation" (p. 42); the flow under that heading is a masked nitrogen implant followed by a single thermal oxidation that grows both thicknesses at once. Wolf reserves "two oxidation steps" for the older route this one replaced: oxidise, remove the oxide from the thin-oxide regions through a mask, and oxidise again. The notes' flow:

  1. Pattern photoresist, exposing the regions that are to receive the thinner oxide.
  2. Perform a low-energy nitrogen implant into the exposed areas.
  3. Strip the resist.
  4. Strip the oxide the implant was performed through — a sacrificial screen oxide — and clean.
  5. Perform the thermal oxidation.

The differentiation comes from the implant. In the notes' words, the rate of thermal oxidation of silicon is reduced by nitrogen implantation, so nitrogen implanted before oxidation can be used to build the dual-oxide structure. The mechanism Wolf suggests is the formation of Si–N bonds at the interface of the growing oxide, which constrain further growth — a different use of nitrogen from §9.1, where it relieves strain in a film already grown. Wolf gives typical implant conditions of 10–30 keV at doses of 2×10¹⁴ to 1×10¹⁵ cm⁻².

10 · Process damage, and measuring the film

Two practical matters close the chapter. Both concern the gap between the oxide as designed and the oxide as it exists on a finished wafer.

Process-induced damage arrives long after the oxide is grown. The notes give one result: plasma damage increased as the gate oxide thickness was decreased from 11.5 nm to 5.5 nm. The main damage source was the RF back-sputter via-clean process used to prepare vias for metal deposition, and the plasma damage was found to correlate with long-term oxide reliability degradation — it does not announce itself at test; it shortens the life of parts that pass. The coupling is electrical: charge collected from the plasma by the large metal and polysilicon structures connected to a gate — the antenna — is driven through the gate oxide, which is how a step several mask levels later, during interconnect fabrication, can damage an oxide it never touches.

The literature disagrees — beyond the notes

The notes' result is one of three reported around it, and the other two do not agree with it. A 1996 analysis found thinner oxides less damaged: 6.4 nm oxides showed an order of magnitude smaller interface-trap density than 11.6 nm oxides, on a model that treats plasma charging as a constant-current source, so that the same current passes whatever the thickness and the thinner oxide tolerates it better; it concluded that plasma damage would not become more serious below 5 nm. A third report, from the same 2000 conference as the notes' result, held that the available measurement techniques cannot determine whether low-level plasma damage is occurring at all. Whether plasma damage worsens as oxides thin is unresolved in the literature; that it is real, and counted among the six factors of §7, is not. Wolf, Vol. 4 §3.8.5.

Measuring a film this thin is itself difficult, and the notes name two techniques. Ellipsometry measures the change in polarisation of light reflected from the surface and infers thickness and refractive index from it. It is optical, non-contact and fast, which makes it the production measurement, and it reaches into the very thin regime, below 50 Å — but accuracy degrades there, and multiple-wavelength or multiple-angle variants are needed to recover it. Part of the reason is structural: the Si/SiO₂ transition region is several ångströms and can reach about 10 Å, which is 20 to 50% of an ultra-thin film, and it has a different refractive index, dielectric constant, band gap and effective mass from the bulk oxide the measurement assumes. Surface roughness, an ad-layer of airborne contamination that the ellipsometer counts as film, and parameter correlation in stacked films add to it; a single angle of incidence can also return several thicknesses that fit, which multiple angles resolve. High-resolution transmission electron microscopy, HRTEM, images a thinned cross-section of the actual stack at atomic resolution; oxide as thin as 2.4 nm has been measured with it. It is destructive and slow, which makes it the calibration measurement rather than the production one.

An oxide is stressed at constant voltage and the gate current is monitored. In one sample the current falls steadily for the first hour and then levels off; in another it rises slowly and then jumps abruptly by two orders of magnitude and stays there. What does each trace indicate, and what can it not establish?

The first trace is consistent with net electron trapping near the cathode, §3.1. Trapped negative charge lowers the field at the cathode and broadens the barrier, and the reduced cathode field reduces the tunnelling current through the exponent of equation (6.1). The levelling-off is the trap population approaching saturation. This is negative feedback, and it is not by itself a failure.

The second trace ends in breakdown. The abrupt jump is the transition from the first stage to the second, §5.1: defects have accumulated into a localised conductive path. Its size and what follows distinguish the outcome — an intermediate current with a power-law characteristic afterwards is soft breakdown, a much larger current with an ohmic characteristic is hard breakdown. The slow rise before the jump is consistent with two processes the trace cannot separate: net positive charge trapped near the cathode, the positive feedback of §3.2, and trap-assisted current growing through traps the stress has generated, §3.3 and §4. Both run alongside electron trapping, which usually masks them until close to breakdown. The sign of dI/dt shows which kind of change is winning at the moment; it does not identify the mechanism.

Summary

  • Tunnelling is a reversible, non-destructive current; breakdown is a permanent short. Tunnelling leads to breakdown only through the trapping and trap generation it causes.
  • SiO₂'s gap is 8.9 eV in the notes; the offsets 3.1 + 1.12 + 4.6 eV make up ≈9 eV. The electron barrier is 3.1 eV. Fowler–Nordheim tunnelling crosses only the triangular tip of the barrier, a distance φox/Eox, then accelerates to the gate; it is the thick-oxide, high-field mechanism, about 5×10⁻⁷ A/cm² at 8 MV/cm, six decades below the leakage limit. Direct tunnelling crosses the whole oxide in one step below 4 nm, becomes very large below 3 nm, and sets the limit.
  • The limit: gate tunnelling equals off-state subthreshold leakage, Jmax = (Ioff/W)/L. At 10 nA/µm and 1 µm × 1 µm: 10⁻⁸ A/µm² = 1 A/cm². Alternate dielectrics are needed for 50 nm MOSFETs and beyond.
  • Trapped electrons lower the cathode field, broaden the barrier and suppress the current — negative feedback. Trapped holes, from impact ionisation or anode hole injection, raise the cathode field, narrow the barrier and increase the current — positive feedback. Hole transport creates bulk traps. Traps also carry current directly: trap-assisted tunnelling.
  • SILC is low-field leakage left by high-field stress, through neutral traps; transient in thicker oxides, continuous in thinner ones; it limits non-volatile memory tunnel oxides to about 8 nm at ten-year retention.
  • A 20-year lifetime needs the oxide field below 8 MV/cm; logic and memory run in the 5 MV/cm range; burn-in screens weak parts. Breakdown has two stages: slow degradation by field or current into a localised conductive path (TDDB), then a very short runaway that shorts the oxide.
  • Polysilicon depletion exists in depletion and inversion. The depleted gate layer drops voltage, so less reaches the oxide and silicon: less inversion charge, less ID, lower speed — equivalent to a thicker oxide. Cpolydep is in series with Cox; it matters below about 10 nm because Cox rises while Cpolydep does not — the notes call it constant at fixed poly doping; it is fixed only at fixed gate charge too, and as oxide fields rose it fell, making matters worse. Heavier poly doping narrows the depleted layer.
  • Six factors set the minimum thickness: reliability, tunnelling, SILC, poly depletion, process damage, hot-carrier damage. Hu's production figures are 110, 65 and 45 Å for 5, 3.3 and 2.5 V (80, 45 and 33 Å defect-free at 5.5, 3.6 and 2.75 V, supply +10%, plus the notes' 30% margin, which the published column does not quite follow). Projections: below 2 nm at 1.0 V; tunnelling excessive below 1.4 nm. Leakage at 1 V: 10⁻¹² A/cm² at 35 Å, and 10⁻¹⁰ A/cm² at 15 Å in the notes, 10 A/cm² in the literature.
  • Deal–Grove, tox2 + Atox = B(t + τ), predicts films thicker than 350 Å; below 200 Å, dry, is the anomalous region, with five competing models. Reisman–Nicollian's empirical tox = a(tg + τ)b fits dry-O₂ data to zero thickness; Massoud's correction is what SUPREM III and IV implement.
  • RTO is single-wafer: lamp-heated at 50–100 °C/s to 800–1000 °C (notes) for seconds, giving lower leakage, better boron resistance and higher mobility, and fitting cluster tools with in-situ cleaning. ISSG (Applied Materials) makes H₂O, OH and atomic oxygen at the wafer below 20 Torr.
  • Nitridation with ammonia (NH₃), nitrous oxide (N₂O) or nitric oxide (NO) gives an oxynitride, not Si₃N₄. Interfacial nitrogen reduces interfacial defects by relieving strained Si–O bonds and dangling bonds (the notes; Wolf: "is thought to"); in the three-bond reading, trivalent nitrogen caps the dangling bond and replaces a stretched bridge. About 1–2 at.% at Si/SiO₂ goes with a higher breakdown field and lower Qf; above 5 at.% at poly/SiO₂ is needed for boron. ROXNOX (NH₃, three steps) leaves hydrogen; N₂O and NO avoid it, NO at the lower thermal budget. Fluorine replaces Si–H with stronger Si–F; keep its dose below 1×10¹⁴ cm⁻² (the notes; Wolf's study used that dose itself) to avoid enhanced boron penetration. A nitrogen implant slows local oxidation and gives two oxide thicknesses from one oxidation, under the notes' heading "2 step oxidation".
  • Plasma damage increased as oxides thinned from 11.5 to 5.5 nm, from RF back-sputter via cleans, and correlates with long-term reliability — the notes' result; a 1996 study found the opposite trend, and whether damage worsens with thinning is unresolved. Ellipsometry works below 50 Å with reduced accuracy; HRTEM has measured 2.4 nm.
