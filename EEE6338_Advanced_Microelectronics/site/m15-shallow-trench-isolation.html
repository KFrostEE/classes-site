Module 15

Shallow Trench Isolation

Why local oxidation stopped working as an isolation technology below about 0.35 µm; the buried-oxide trench process that came before shallow trench isolation and its six drawbacks; the baseline STI flow step by step; how deep the trench must be; the parasitic transistors along the trench sidewall and at its top corner; corner rounding and the divot; how the trench is filled without voids; and the dishing that the final polish leaves in wide trenches.

Prerequisites M08 (the field transistor, eq. 8.6), M13, M14 (ceria slurry, dishing) Read time ~55 min Sources Class notes pp. 131–146 (Chapter 9); Lectures 4, 16, 17, 24; Wolf Vol. 4 Ch. 9; Plummer §2.2, Ch. 6

Every transistor on a chip must be electrically separated from its neighbours, and the separation is made by a thick oxide in the silicon surface between them, the field oxide. M08 §3 showed why it must be thick: any conductor crossing the field region is the gate of a parasitic MOS transistor, and the field oxide must keep that transistor's threshold above the supply. For two decades the field oxide was grown by local oxidation of silicon, LOCOS (M02 §9). Shallow trench isolation (STI) replaced it at the 0.35–0.25 µm generations: a trench is etched into the silicon, lined with a thin thermal oxide, filled with deposited oxide and polished flat. The idea is simple and old; making it work took three enabling processes, dry trench etching, void-free gap-filling deposition and chemical mechanical polishing (M13), and it brought new problems of its own at the trench sidewall and the trench corner. This module follows the class notes' Chapter 9, which follows Wolf's Chapter 9 nearly sentence by sentence. The chapter had not been lectured in full when this page was written; Lecture 17 drew the flow as a preview.

1 · What LOCOS costs

The notes open the chapter with four limitations of LOCOS (p. 131): the bird's beak penetrates the active regions; the surface is not planar; the field oxide thins at narrow isolation spacings; and the oxidation stresses the silicon enough to create defects. Each follows from one fact: LOCOS grows its field oxide from the substrate, so the oxide's shape is set by oxidation kinetics rather than by the mask. This section recalls the process, then takes the four in turn and ends with what STI changes.

1.1 The process, and the step it leaves

In LOCOS a thin pad oxide is grown, a silicon nitride film is deposited over it and patterned to cover the future active areas, and the wafer is oxidized. Nitride is nearly impermeable to the oxidant, so the field oxide grows only where the nitride has been removed. Plummer's example grows about 500 nm in H₂O at 1000 °C for 90 min, after which the nitride is stripped in hot phosphoric acid, which etches nitride and leaves oxide (Plummer §2.2). Thermal oxidation consumes silicon: the course value is 0.46 of the oxide thickness (M05a). The remaining 0.54 of the oxide lies above the original silicon surface. LOCOS field oxide is therefore semi-recessed, partly below and partly above the surface, and the active areas are left at the bottom of a step.

A step of a quarter of a micrometre at the isolation is the first layer of the topography that M13 §1 built up level by level: the gate polysilicon runs up and over it, and every later level inherits it. CMP can polish the field oxide flat, but the notes and Wolf add a qualification at once: CMP reduces the topography but does not remove the encroachment (notes p. 131; Wolf p. 433).

1.2 The bird's beak

The oxidant does not stop at the edge of the nitride. It diffuses sideways through the pad oxide and oxidizes the silicon under the nitride edge, and the volume expansion of that oxide lifts the nitride edge, so the field oxide ends in a tapered wedge, the bird's beak (Plummer §2.2, §6; Lecture 16: "bird's beak are formed due to lateral penetration of oxidation"). The wedge is field oxide where the mask drew active area, so the active width is narrower than drawn by twice the length of the beak. Wolf gives the scaling: the lateral encroachment is proportional to the thickness of the grown field oxide, with typical values of a fraction of a micrometre for the 0.3–0.5 µm field oxides of submicron LOCOS (p. 554). That is the core of the problem. The field oxide cannot be made much thinner, because its thickness sets the field threshold (M08 §3), so the encroachment stays near a fixed fraction of a micrometre while the active width the lithography can print keeps shrinking.

The beak can be shortened by thinning the pad oxide, which narrows the path for lateral diffusion, and by thickening the nitride, which resists being lifted. Both raise the stress the nitride exerts on the silicon during the long oxidation. Poly-buffered LOCOS inserts a polysilicon layer to absorb that stress: it uses about 200 nm of nitride over about 20 nm of oxide, against about 80 nm over about 40 nm in plain LOCOS, and the polysilicon relieves stresses "which would otherwise cause defects to form in the silicon substrate during the LOCOS oxidation"; the polysilicon also oxidizes along its own edges, which sharpens the transition further (Plummer §2.2). That is the fourth limitation of the notes' list: thinning the pad oxide and thickening the nitride shorten the beak but load the silicon harder, and the polysilicon buffer is what lets both be done without defects.

The bird's beak belongs to LOCOS. Lecture 4 introduced STI with the words "whenever you do the shallow trench isolation you get these sharp corners. These are called bird's beak"; a few sentences later the same lecture described the LOCOS mechanism and credited STI with removing the beak, so the slip is in the naming only. A bird's beak is the tapered oxide that LOCOS grows under the nitride edge; STI has none (Wolf p. 433; Plummer §2.2.3). The only oxide grown under the mask edge in STI is the thin liner, which consumes some 20 nm of silicon there (§3.2). STI's corner problem is the opposite one: the top corner of the trench is sharp, and it must be deliberately rounded (§6).

1.3 Thinning at narrow spacings, and stress

The third limitation is that a LOCOS field oxide grown in a narrow opening comes out thinner than the same oxidation gives in a wide one (notes p. 131; Wolf p. 433). Neither the notes nor Wolf's Chapter 9 gives the mechanism. The paper that reported the effect attributes it to the oxidant supply: in a submicrometre nitride window less oxidant reaches the oxidizing surface, because oxidant diffusing in is also consumed sideways by the oxidation under the two nitride edges (Mizuno et al., IEEE Trans. Electron Devices 34, 2255, 1987; abstract only read). Compressive stress in the confined oxide, which Plummer shows retards oxidation at etched corners (Kao's experiments, §6.1 below), has been proposed as a second contributor; applying it to LOCOS windows is an inference, not Plummer's. Since the charge terms of the field threshold scale with the oxide thickness (M08 eq. 8.6), the narrowest isolation spaces are the ones most likely to leak. The stress-induced defects of the fourth limitation have a different source: the stress between the nitride and the silicon during the long field oxidation (Plummer §2.2; Wolf p. 446).

Wolf adds a fifth cost in the same sentence as the bird's beak: channel-stop encroachment (p. 433). The channel-stop boron implanted under the field region (M08 §3.3) diffuses sideways during the long field oxidation into the edge of the active area; Wolf credits STI with reducing that redistribution. The standard account, which Wolf defers to his Vol. 3 (not among the course sources), is that this boron and the bird's beak together raise the threshold of a narrow transistor: the narrow-channel effect of the next list.

1.4 What STI changes

Wolf lists the gains of a trench filled with deposited oxide (pp. 433–434):

  • no bird's beak, and less redistribution of the channel-stop dopant, so the isolation spacing can be smaller;
  • a fully recessed field oxide, so the surface can be completely planar after isolation;
  • no field-oxide thinning in narrow spaces, since the oxide is deposited, not grown;
  • a threshold voltage that is constant with channel width, because "the narrow-channel effect found in LOCOS" is eliminated (Wolf gives no mechanism here; see the end of §1.3);
  • larger transconductance and smaller subthreshold swing than with semi-recessed LOCOS;
  • lower trench corners that, though rounded, are tighter than the edge of a semi-recessed LOCOS oxide; the field lines spread out at such a corner, which raises the threshold there and improves isolation, the so-called corner effect.

The price is a longer process: STI takes more steps than LOCOS (Wolf Fig. 9-1b), and §3 lists twenty-one. LOCOS remained the workhorse until the 0.35 µm generation (Wolf p. 13). STI entered mainstream production in the 166 MHz Pentium (a 0.35 µm process) and the PowerPC 603e (0.45 µm), and it became the standard isolation for CMOS at about the 0.25 µm node (Wolf pp. 435, 440; notes p. 134). Lecture 16 summed up that LOCOS is no longer used in current technologies; by Wolf's dates it still served the first submicron generations, down to 0.35 µm.

A LOCOS process grows a 400 nm field oxide. How high does the field oxide stand above the active silicon after the nitride strip? If the bird's beak extended 0.1 µm under each nitride edge, what fraction of a 0.35 µm drawn active width would be lost, and why could the process engineer not simply grow 200 nm instead?

From (15.1), 0.46 × 400 = 184 nm lies below the original surface and 400 − 184 = 216 nm above it: a 216 nm step. Two beaks of 0.1 µm take 0.2 µm of the 0.35 µm drawn width, 57 %, leaving 0.15 µm of active silicon. Halving the oxide would roughly halve the beak, since Wolf gives the encroachment as proportional to the oxide thickness, but it would also halve both charge terms of the field threshold (M08 eq. 8.6), and the field transistor would turn on below the supply unless the channel-stop doping rose to compensate, which in turn spreads further into the active edge during the oxidation. The field oxide's thickness is fixed by isolation, so its encroachment cannot be scaled with the lithography; that is the case for etching the space instead.

2 · Trench isolation before STI: the BOX process

Trenches were used for isolation long before STI. The notes list four uses of non-LOCOS isolation (p. 131): replacing LOCOS between like devices in the same well of a CMOS chip; isolating bipolar transistors; isolating n-channel from p-channel devices and preventing latch-up in CMOS; and forming the trench capacitor of a DRAM cell (M07b). The first shallow trench process was buried-oxide (BOX) isolation, and its drawbacks are the list of problems that STI had to solve. This section gives the process, the six drawbacks, and the three processes that overcame them.

2.1 The BOX process

BOX isolation refills shallow trenches with CVD SiO₂ and etches the oxide back to a planar surface (notes p. 131). The original process, BOX-I, was introduced in 1981 and an improved BOX-II in 1983 (Wolf p. 435). In BOX-II:

  1. Trenches 0.5–0.8 µm deep were etched with an orientation-dependent silicon etch, which gives sidewalls inclined at about 60°.
  2. A channel-stop implant was performed; the sloped sidewalls receive enough dose to prevent weak inversion of the sidewall surface.
  3. A thermal oxide 20–50 nm thick was grown to passivate the trench walls.
  4. CVD oxide was deposited to fill the trenches.
  5. The oxide was etched back by a plasma that erodes photoresist and SiO₂ at the same rate, after two layers of resist had been applied to make the surface as planar as possible, so that oxide remained only in the recesses.

The notes write that the trenches are "isotropically etched… through orientation dependent Si-etch" (p. 132). An orientation-dependent etch attacks some crystal planes much faster than others, which is what produces a fixed sidewall angle; it is anisotropic by definition, and Wolf, whom the notes follow here, does not use the word "isotropically" (p. 435).

2.2 Six drawbacks

  1. Voids. CVD oxide deposited into 0.5 µm deep trenches narrower than about 2 µm left voids (notes p. 132; Wolf p. 435). §7 explains how a void forms.
  2. Sidewall inversion. The silicon at the sidewalls of p-type active regions could still invert, especially where vertical sidewalls were used to save space, and boron segregating into the thin passivating oxide made it worse by lowering the boron concentration at the sidewall (Wolf p. 435; §5).
  3. Non-uniform field oxide. The field-oxide profile after the planarization etch varied from one area to another.
  4. Imperfect planarization. The resist did not end up planar, even with two layers, wherever the chip had a wide range of active-area sizes and spacings; global planarity was poor.
  5. Gate-oxide thinning at the trench edge. A thinner gate oxide at the corner degrades its reliability, raises the field at the corner and lowers the threshold of the corner region (notes p. 133).
  6. The overetch. To be sure the CVD oxide was gone from every active area, the field oxide had to be overetched by about 100 nm. Its surface then lay below the active surface and exposed part of the active-area sidewall (notes p. 133).

Drawbacks 5 and 6 act through one device. Wolf explains it by comparison with LOCOS (pp. 436–437, Fig. 9-2). Over a LOCOS edge the polysilicon gate curves upward and away from the silicon as it climbs onto the field oxide, so the part of the gate over the field has little effect on the transistor. Over a trench the gate runs flat across the isolation, and the part of it over the field region sets up a two-dimensional fringing field on the corner and the sidewall of the silicon mesa, the raised block of silicon that carries the transistor. The same gate voltage then produces a larger field at the corner than in the middle of the channel and induces more inversion charge there, so the corner behaves as a second transistor in parallel with the main one, with a lower threshold. If the isolation oxide is recessed below the silicon surface, as the 100 nm overetch leaves it, the gate wraps around the corner of the mesa and the effect is stronger. §5.3 quantifies the result.

The combination of these problems, above all the complex processing needed for adequately planar surfaces, kept BOX isolation from displacing LOCOS (Wolf p. 437). The notes add a limitation of STI: it "is less effective in preventing latchup and isolating n-channel from p-channel in CMOS devices" (p. 131). They give no reason. A plausible one is that the wells and their junctions continue below a shallow trench, whereas a trench deeper than the wells replaces the junction at the well sidewall (Wolf p. 440; §5.1), though even deep trenches disappointed on latch-up.

2.3 The three enabling processes

Three developments made shallow trench isolation practical (notes pp. 133–134; Wolf pp. 438–439):

  • Dry etching. Plasma etch processes replaced the wet etches and gave trenches with a controlled slope and rounded bottom corners, which fill more easily.
  • Gap-filling CVD. Low-pressure CVD from tetraethylorthosilicate (TEOS, Si(OC₂H₅)₄), atmospheric-pressure CVD from ozone and TEOS, and high-density-plasma CVD (HDP-CVD) fill trenches without voids (§7). Because the trench fill is thicker than an intermetal dielectric, its film stress must be lower, below 200 MPa.
  • CMP. Polishing, developed at IBM in the late 1980s and introduced to the industry in the early 1990s, removes the deposited oxide from the active areas and leaves a planar surface.

The goal they serve is stated in the notes as the optimum STI structure: a gate that runs flat across the isolation, which needs a planar surface before the polysilicon is deposited. An RIE etchback followed by CMP gives one (p. 134; §3.3).

Name the BOX drawback that each STI enabling process answers, and say which of the six drawbacks no enabling process removes by itself.

Dry etching with a controlled slope and rounded bottom corners, together with gap-filling CVD, answers drawback 1, the voids. CMP answers drawbacks 3 and 4, the non-uniform field-oxide profile and the poor global planarity of the resist etchback, and it removes the need for the 100 nm overetch of drawback 6, because the polish stops on a nitride layer rather than relying on a timed etch (§3). Drawbacks 2 and 5, sidewall inversion and corner effects including the thinned gate oxide, are not removed by any of the three: they belong to the geometry of a trench and need their own measures, sidewall doping (§5.2) and corner rounding (§6).

3 · The baseline STI flow

The notes give a baseline STI process in twenty-one steps for the 0.25 µm generation and below (pp. 135–139), Wolf's baseline step for step (pp. 446–455). Lecture 17 drew a shortened version as a preview: oxidize, etch the trench through a mask, fill it with high-density CVD oxide, polish, then form the wells. The full flow falls into four stages: the mask stack and the trench etch; the liner; filling and planarizing; stripping the mask and forming the wells. Figure 3 follows the notes' own drawing (p. 136).

3.1 The mask stack and the trench etch (steps 1–6)

  1. Pad oxide. The bare wafer is cleaned and a pad oxide 10–20 nm thick is grown thermally. It keeps the nitride deposited next from stressing the silicon directly; such stresses could create defects in the substrate. Because STI has no long high-temperature field oxidation, the stress is smaller than in LOCOS and the pad oxide can be thinner, about 10 nm against 25–40 nm (Wolf pp. 446–447). Unlike LOCOS, the trenches are the first structures formed on the bare wafer; the wells come later.
  2. Nitride. LPCVD silicon nitride, 100–200 nm thick, has two roles: the hard mask for the trench etch and the polish stop for the CMP of the trench fill. LPCVD is a batch furnace process, so nitride is also deposited on the back of every wafer, and it must be removed later (step 16).
  3. Mask #1. Photoresist is applied and patterned with the first mask of the process to open the regions where the trenches will be.
  4. Hard-mask etch. The nitride and pad oxide are etched anisotropically in a plasma, usually in an etcher designed for dielectrics.
  5. Trench etch. The silicon is etched in a plasma with four goals: the correct depth; the desired sidewall slope, 70–85° depending on the node, the trench depth and the circuit; rounded bottom corners, which minimize stress during the liner oxidation and the fill; and smooth sidewalls with minimum damage. The gases are HBr-based mixtures, HBr/Cl₂/O₂ or HBr/NF₃/He/O₂; Wolf reports better corner rounding with the second (pp. 447–450). A sloped wall is easier to fill without a void but costs area, so the slope must steepen as the trenches shrink.
  6. Resist strip.

The trench etch is hard to control for two reasons (Wolf pp. 449–450). Some dry etches show aspect-ratio-dependent etching (ARDE): wide trenches etch faster than narrow ones, so trenches of different widths end at different depths. And the etch is blind: there is no stop layer at the bottom and no change of material to give an endpoint signal, so the depth must come from a timed etch that is reproducible across the wafer and from wafer to wafer. Etching the nitride and the trench in one chamber (in-situ hard-mask open) raises throughput, and its main difficulty is selectivity to the photoresist.

When is the resist stripped? The step list above, like the notes' (p. 136) and Wolf's (p. 450, step 6), strips the resist after the trench etch. Wolf's later discussion prefers the two-etcher process "with the resist being stripped after the 1st trench-etch step" (p. 456): the hard mask is opened in a dielectric etcher, the resist removed, and the silicon etched in a modified gate etcher with the nitride alone as the mask. The notes' own drawing (p. 136) shows that order, "Remove PR, Si trench etch", and so does Figure 3 (c). Plummer allows either (§2.2.3). Learn the step list as the notes give it, and know that the nitride can serve as the only trench mask.

3.2 The liner (steps 7–8)

  1. Pad-oxide undercut. A short HF dip etches the pad oxide back from the trench edge, under the nitride. This lets the next oxidation round the top corner more effectively (Wolf p. 451).
  2. Liner oxidation. A dry-O₂ thermal oxide 40–50 nm thick is grown on the trench walls; the best corner rounding comes from a rapid thermal oxidation at 1050–1100 °C (notes p. 136; Wolf p. 451).

The liner has three roles (Wolf pp. 450–451). Its growth consumes the silicon damaged by the plasma etch. It passivates the trench surface with the most electrically stable interface available, a thermal oxide on silicon, which keeps the threshold of the parasitic field transistors at the trench surfaces high and stable (§5). And it rounds the sharp top corner of the active region (§6). During this oxidation a thin layer of the nitride surface is converted to oxide, a fact that matters when the nitride is stripped (step 15). In DRAMs a thin CVD nitride liner may be added before the fill, because it reduces leakage current (notes p. 137).

The liner also changes the geometry. By (15.1) it consumes 0.46 of its thickness from every silicon surface, including the sidewall under the edge of the nitride, and it grows the other 0.54 into the trench.

What the liner does to the trench and the active area

Given the notes' middle values: pad oxide 15 nm, nitride 150 nm, a trench 500 nm deep, and a 45 nm liner; a trench 400 nm wide, the typical minimum in a 0.18 µm process, with active areas of 600 nm (Wolf p. 445). Check: the loss is fixed in nanometres, so its share grows as the active width shrinks, the same scaling argument as the bird's beak in §1.2 on a much smaller scale.

3.3 Filling and planarizing (steps 9–14)

  1. Fill. CVD oxide is deposited over the whole wafer: LPCVD TEOS, sub-atmospheric CVD from ozone and TEOS (the same ozone–TEOS process §2.3 calls atmospheric-pressure, after notes p. 133; Wolf p. 452 writes "(sub)atmospheric"), or HDP-CVD. It is a blanket process, so oxide also lands on the nitride over the active areas: 8000–9000 Å are deposited to fill a 5000 Å deep trench (notes p. 137).
  2. Densify. The fill is annealed at about 900 °C so that it etches at a rate comparable to thermal oxide; without it the HF dips of steps 15, 17 and 20 would remove too much of the trench fill (notes p. 137; Wolf p. 452). Wolf's text says densification lowers the wet-etch rate by about 20 %. His own Table 9-1, measured after an 850 °C anneal, shows the HF rate relative to thermal oxide falling from 5 to 1.3 for SACVD ozone–TEOS and from 2.1 to 1.1 for LPCVD TEOS, and unchanged at 1.1 for HDP oxide: the reduction depends on the film.
  3. Reverse mask. Photoresist is patterned as the reverse of the active-area pattern, covering the trenches, and a planarizing resist layer is spun over it.
  4. Etchback. RIE removes resist and oxide at the same rate, thinning the oxide over the active areas while the trench oxide stays covered. The point is to shorten the CMP that follows, because a shorter polish dishes the wide trenches less (Wolf p. 452; §8).
  5. Resist strip. Small spikes of oxide remain near the edges of the active regions, because the reverse mask is drawn slightly smaller than the active areas (Wolf p. 465). They must go to leave a globally planar surface.
  6. CMP. The oxide is polished back, removing the spikes, until the nitride is reached; the nitride is the polish stop. Dishing may occur in wide trenches (notes p. 138; §8).

How much nitride survives the polish depends on the slurry's selectivity, the ratio of the oxide removal rate to the nitride removal rate (M14 §1). In Wolf's comparison of trench fills the CMP rate against nitride was about 5:1 for every film (Wolf Table 9-1). Ceria slurries reach 250:1, which is why ceria is used for STI (M14 §1.2; Lecture 24: ceria "can be used for the shallow trench isolation and sometimes also the ILD").

3.4 Stripping the mask, and the wells (steps 15–21)

  1. Nitride strip, in two wet steps. The nitride is removed in hot phosphoric acid, but the thin layer converted to oxide during the liner oxidation does not etch in phosphoric acid and would block it, so a short HF dip removes that layer first. The HF also attacks the trench fill, which etches faster than the oxide on the nitride, so the dip must be carefully optimized; and if the polish left too much oxide on the nitride, the dip may not clear it and the nitride will not strip (Wolf p. 453).
  2. Backside nitride. The phosphoric acid also removes the nitride from the back of the wafer. A wet strip is used because it is cheap, it is selective (it attacks neither the pad oxide nor the silicon), and it removes the backside film at the same time.
  3. Pad-oxide strip in HF, which also etches the trench oxide.
  4. Sacrificial oxide, about 20 nm, grown to protect the active surface from contamination during the implants. Wolf gives a second reason. If the fill was densified in an ambient containing O₂, that oxygen can react with hydrogen left in the films to form water, the water can react with the nitride to form ammonia, and the ammonia nitrides the silicon surface at the active edge, as in the Kooi effect of LOCOS. A gate oxide grown over such a nitrided spot is locally thinner. The sacrificial oxidation consumes the nitrided silicon (Wolf p. 454).
  5. Wells. High-energy implants place the well dopants in the active regions and, beneath the trenches, a channel-stop layer; the threshold-adjust and punch-through implants are done at the same time (notes pp. 138–139). This is the order of M08 §4.4 and §7: isolation first, wells implanted through it afterwards, with no long drive-in.
  6. Sacrificial-oxide strip in HF, which etches the trench oxide again.
  7. Gate oxidation.

Three of these steps, 15, 17 and 20, are HF dips that etch the trench fill as well as their intended film. Wolf allows that they might recess the trench oxide as a whole but says little thickness is lost that way; the more significant effect is on the oxide near the top corner of the trench (p. 455), the subject of §6.2.

Where the trench-oxide surface ends

Given the previous example without etchback: 116.7 nm of nitride left over a 15 nm pad oxide, the trench oxide polished level with the nitride; an HDP or LPCVD TEOS fill, which etches 1.1 times as fast as thermal oxide in HF once densified (Wolf Table 9-1); a 20 nm sacrificial oxide. 4 · How deep the trench must be

The baseline trenches are about 0.5 µm deep, inside Wolf's 0.3–0.5 µm range; as devices approach the 0.1 µm node the depth is expected to fall to about 0.2 µm (notes p. 139: "decrease to 0.2 µm"; Wolf p. 456: "< 0.2-µm, at least for some circuit applications"). The sidewalls steepen at the same time, from 70–85° to as much as 88°; Wolf gives no reason, but a narrower trench plausibly has no area to spare for a slope. Steep walls are etched at low pressure with high ion flux, in non-depositing chemistries such as Cl₂/Ar (Wolf pp. 455–456). This section sets out the two considerations that fix the depth.

The field threshold. The oxide in the trench is the field oxide. Its thickness, which is the trench depth, together with the channel-stop doping under it, sets the threshold of the parasitic field transistor, and that threshold must stay high enough to prevent isolation leakage (notes p. 139). This is M08's equation (8.6) with the trench depth as tFOX. The capacitance. The trench oxide is also the dielectric between the substrate and every polysilicon line and first-level metal line that crosses the field; the shallower the trench, the higher that capacitance. Both considerations relax as supply voltages scale: a lower supply tolerates a lower field threshold (Wolf p. 456), so the trench can become shallower.

5 · Parasitic devices at the trench

A trench removes the bird's beak but creates new conduction paths that LOCOS did not have (Wolf p. 440). There are two: an inversion layer along the vertical sidewall of the trench, controlled by the voltage on the well across it; and the corner device at the top edge of the active area, controlled by the transistor's own gate. This section takes the deep trench first, because it is where the sidewall device was identified, then the sidewall, then the corner.

5.1 Deep trench isolation for CMOS

Trench isolation was also explored for twin-well CMOS, primarily to shrink the spacing between n⁺ and p⁺ regions in opposite wells (Wolf p. 439). A trench at the boundary between the n-well and the p-well stops the two wells' dopants from diffusing into each other and compensating, which would lower the surface doping at the well edges; the field transistor there therefore keeps a higher threshold at a smaller spacing. Trenches deeper than the wells replace the reverse-biased pn junction as the isolation at the well sidewalls (notes p. 134; Wolf Fig. 9-5). Deep trench isolation for CMOS is considerably more complex to fabricate than STI, and its latch-up results did not meet expectations (latch-up is the parasitic thyristor of M08 §5), so it was not widely adopted; STI became the standard at about 0.25 µm (notes p. 134; Wolf p. 440).

5.2 The sidewall MOSFET

The notes define the new leakage path in one sentence (p. 134): the sidewall inversion is caused by a horizontal parasitic MOS device in which the positively biased n-well acts as the gate electrode and the trench oxide serves as the gate oxide, its thickness equal to the trench width. The n-well sits at +VDD, and the p-type silicon on the far side of the trench is the channel region of that device. If the sidewall inverts, the inversion layer can join the source and drain of an n-channel transistor placed at the trench edge, independently of that transistor's own gate, and raise its off-state leakage; or, with the field surface also inverted by a polysilicon line, it can join an n-channel source to the n-well (Wolf pp. 440–442, Figs. 9-6 to 9-8).

The device is the field transistor of M08 turned on its side, so equation (8.6) gives its threshold with the trench width as the oxide thickness. Its gate is n-type silicon rather than polysilicon, so the work-function difference is that between n-type and p-type silicon:

Sidewall inversion is worse in p-wells than in n-wells for two linked reasons (Wolf p. 443). The fixed and trapped charge at the Si/SiO₂ interface is positive, which lowers the inversion threshold of p-type silicon (the −Qi/C term). Keeping that charge low needs a good thermal liner, but growing the liner draws boron from the p-type sidewall into the oxide by segregation (M08 §3.2), lowering the sidewall doping and the threshold further. The remedies, in Wolf's order (pp. 444–446):

  • channel-stop doping between the active devices and the well edge, as in LOCOS CMOS;
  • moving active devices back from the trench edge, and widening the trench; both cost isolation spacing;
  • doping the sidewalls directly with a low-tilt boron implant, 8° from the wafer normal, repeated four times with the wafer rotated 90° to reach all four walls; it suits only isolation between like devices, since it dopes n-well sidewalls too;
  • Davari's selective process: boron diffused into the p-well sidewalls from p⁺ polysilicon or borosilicate glass that is patterned off the n-well sidewall, with no extra dopant for the n-well, whose phosphorus piles up at the surface during the liner oxidation; used for trenches down to 0.8 µm wide, in a 0.35 µm process;
  • at 0.25 µm and below, implanting the wells after the STI is complete (§3.4): the boron is not present during the liner oxidation, so none segregates, the p-well implant puts channel-stop boron at the trench bottom, and the channel boron needed against DIBL is heavy enough to keep the sidewalls from inverting. In a 0.18 µm process the minimum trench is about 0.4 µm wide.

The last remedy is the one the baseline flow uses. Its effect on the example, with the channel-stop doping of §4 taken as an illustrative sidewall doping (no source gives one for wells implanted after STI): at 5×10¹⁶ cm⁻³ instead of 9×10¹⁴ cm⁻³, (15.3) gives 19.0 V across a 0.4 µm trench with Wolf's 1 V of body bias, and 12.3 V with the body grounded.

5.3 The corner device and the subthreshold hump

The second parasitic device is the corner of §2.2. Where the gate runs over the top corner of the active mesa, the field from the top and from the side converge, the corner inverts at a lower gate voltage than the middle of the channel, and the corner acts as a narrow transistor with a lower threshold in parallel with the main one. It shows in the transfer characteristic as a "hump" or "kink" in the subthreshold region, and it raises the off-state leakage (Wolf pp. 436–437, Fig. 9-3). A thinned gate oxide at the corner lowers the corner threshold further (BOX drawback 5), and a recessed field oxide lets the gate wrap the corner and makes it worse. Device simulations show that a corner radius of 50 nm, with a sidewall slope of no more than 80°, removes the double hump (Wolf p. 451).

The hump in the model follows from the exponential subthreshold law of M07 §5: a device with a threshold 0.3 V lower carries 100.3/0.08 ≈ 5600 times more current per unit width below threshold, so even a corner 2 % as wide dominates the off state, while above threshold the wide main channel dominates and the corner disappears from the curve.

The parasitic device is the trench turned on its side: the n-well at +VDD is the gate, the trench fill is the gate oxide with a thickness equal to the trench width, and the p-type sidewall facing it is the channel (figure above). Two effects lower its threshold in a p-well. The interface charge of the liner is positive, which in (15.3) enters as −Qi/C and favours inversion of p-type silicon. And growing the liner draws boron out of the sidewall into the oxide by segregation, which lowers NA and with it the depletion term. An n-well sidewall has neither problem: the positive charge accumulates it, and its phosphorus piles up at the surface during oxidation. Implanting the wells after the STI means the boron is not in the silicon during the liner oxidation, so none segregates, and the p-well implant also places channel-stop boron under the trench.

6 · Corner rounding and the divot

The top corner of the active region is where STI's remaining problems concentrate: the field crowding of §5.3, the gate-oxide thinning of BOX drawback 5, and a recess in the trench oxide called the divot. This section explains how the corner is rounded, how the divot forms and what it does, and the RELACS process that rounds the corner without the encroachment of the baseline method.

6.1 Rounding the corner

In the baseline flow the liner oxidation rounds the corner (§3.2), and the notes and Wolf give the conditions that work best: an RTO in dry O₂ at 1050–1100 °C (notes p. 140; Wolf p. 458). The temperature matters because of how oxide grows on a shaped surface. Kao's experiments, reported by Plummer (§6), found that oxidation is retarded at sharp corners, by a factor of two in oxide thickness for nominally 500 nm oxides, that the retardation is much stronger at low temperature, and that at 1200 °C there is virtually no corner effect. Plummer names three mechanisms: the range of crystal orientations exposed at a curved surface, two-dimensional diffusion of the oxidant, and above all the compressive stress of the expanding oxide, which is concentrated at a corner and carries the temperature dependence. At high temperature the oxide flows viscously and relaxes that stress, and Plummer attributes the corner rounding of a high-temperature liner to "the viscoelastic flow properties of SiO₂" (§2.2.3). At low temperature the stress-retarded apex is left sharp, or sharpened, because the oxide there is thinner than on the flat faces (Plummer: "The oxide is thinner on both concave and convex corners than it is on flat regions"). Neither source derives the rounded shape itself; this page takes Plummer's flow mechanism as given. Undercutting the pad oxide first (step 7) exposes more of the corner to the oxidant.

The paper that described the RTO liner also studied three other methods (notes p. 140; Wolf p. 458), and Wolf reports a fourth, from other authors:

  • adding HCl to the dry-O₂ RTO;
  • a post-CMP oxidation in dry O₂ at 1050–1100 °C, which rounds well but reduces the active area;
  • a "mini-LOCOS" edge, which adds considerable process complexity (Wolf only);
  • reported separately as an alternative, a hydrogen anneal after the trench etch and before the fill, in which silicon surface atoms migrate toward the lowest-energy shape and a sharp corner becomes a rounded one, with radii up to 60 nm. Wolf's text gives the anneal as 1100 °C; the caption of his Fig. 9-18 gives 950 °C, 380 Torr, 60 s for the case shown (Wolf only).

6.2 The divot and its four effects

The HF dips of steps 17 and 20, which strip the pad oxide and the sacrificial oxide, etch the trench fill at the same time. At the top corner of the trench, where the fill meets the active edge, the etch leaves a recess in the trench oxide, the divot (notes p. 144; Wolf p. 469, Fig. 9-30). The notes list four effects (pp. 144–145):

  1. the gate wraps around the corner, which can increase the leakage of the MOSFET in the subthreshold region (the hump of §5.3);
  2. the wrap-around makes the transistor behave like one whose isolation is recessed below the silicon surface, which enhances the reverse narrow-width effect, the fall of threshold voltage as the channel narrows, because the corner is a larger share of a narrow device (Wolf p. 470, Fig. 9-31);
  3. the threshold voltage falls as the divot deepens; together these effects produce unacceptably large threshold variations, ±200 mV;
  4. the rounded edge encroaches into the active region, under the nitride edge, which reduces the electrical width of the transistor; near the 0.1 µm node that can be a large fraction of the width of a minimum-size device.

The notes and Wolf give no number for the fourth effect. A lower-bound estimate can be made with the liner example of §3.2: the 45 nm liner consumes 20.7 nm of silicon under each nitride edge, and that encroachment is fixed while the width shrinks. The estimate leaves out the rounding itself, which takes about one corner radius of flat top surface per edge (Wolf's target radius is 50 nm, p. 451), and it treats every receded nanometre as lost, although the poly that wraps a rounded corner still gates part of it.

6.3 RELACS: rounding during the etch

The solution the notes give is to round the corner during the trench etch instead of afterwards, after the nitride hard mask is opened but before the silicon trench is etched (p. 145; Wolf pp. 470–472). It uses a resist-shrink technique that Wolf calls "a new lithographic technique from Clariant-AZ termed RELACS":

  1. The resist is exposed and developed as usual (KrF resist in the notes' figure).
  2. A RELACS agent is coated over the resist pattern.
  3. A bake crosslinks the agent where it touches the resist, through acid from the resist sidewalls (the notes' figure marks "acid").
  4. A water rinse removes the agent that did not crosslink. A thin layer remains attached to the resist sidewalls, so every opening is slightly narrower than printed.
  5. The nitride is opened through the narrowed openings, so the trench opening in the nitride is slightly smaller.
  6. A short etch of the silicon undercuts the nitride edge and leaves a rounded top corner on the silicon (Wolf p. 470).
  7. The rest of the trench is etched anisotropically.

Wolf's claim is that "the encroachment under the nitride edge by the rounded corner produced in the baseline-STI sequence is eliminated" (p. 472); he does not spell out the geometry. A consistent reading, which is this page's inference: the RELACS layer makes the nitride opening narrower than the printed trench, and the short etch then rounds the corner under that overhanging nitride, inside the margin the shrink created, so the rounding takes nothing from the active width as printed. The poly wrap-around is also less severe; and the threshold voltage varies less from wafer to wafer. Wolf's figure shows a top-corner radius of about 15 nm on trenches 180 nm and 100 nm wide (Fig. 9-32). The reverse narrow-channel effect becomes more severe if the trench oxide encroaches beneath the nitride edge, which this process avoids (notes p. 146).

The baseline flow rounds the top corner with its liner oxidation. State what that rounding costs at small widths, why it needs a high temperature, and how RELACS avoids the cost.

The liner rounds the corner by consuming silicon, including silicon under the nitride edge, so the active silicon ends narrower than the nitride that defined it: with a 45 nm liner, 20.7 nm per edge, 27.6 % of a 150 nm device (§6.2). The temperature matters because oxidation at a sharp convex corner is retarded by the compressive stress of the expanding oxide; at 1050–1100 °C the oxide flows and relaxes the stress, the apex oxidizes nearly as fast as the faces, and the corner comes out round (Kao; Plummer). RELACS narrows the resist openings slightly with a crosslinked layer, so the nitride opening is smaller than printed, and a short silicon etch rounds the corner inside that opening before the anisotropic trench etch; the rounding then does not encroach under the nitride edge.

7 · Filling the trench without voids

The trench must be filled with no void, because a void opened by a later HF dip becomes a seam that polysilicon can fill and short along (Wolf p. 459). This section gives the criteria a fill must meet, why ordinary CVD leaves a void, and how high-density-plasma CVD, the technology that won, avoids one.

7.1 Five criteria, four candidates

The notes list the criteria a trench-fill dielectric must meet (p. 140; Wolf p. 459):

  1. it must be void-free, so that HF deglazing does not open seams that later fill with polysilicon filaments;
  2. its HF etch rate relative to thermal oxide must be known, because post-deposition deglazing thins the fill and can expose silicon at the trench corners;
  3. its stress, shrinkage and composition after the densification anneal must suit the integration;
  4. its CMP rate relative to nitride, and the uniformity of its polish, set the oxide left in the trench and so influence the "corner effect" problem;
  5. the isolation of p-channel and n-channel diodes must be tested to verify the fill electrically.

Nag and co-workers compared four fills against these criteria: LPCVD TEOS, HDP-CVD oxide, sub-atmospheric CVD (SACVD) ozone–TEOS and a hydrogen-silsesquioxane spin-on glass. HDP-CVD oxide was the most suitable: it needed no high-temperature anneal, shrank by 0–0.8 % at 850 °C against 22–28 % for the spin-on glass, and etched in HF at 1.1 times the thermal-oxide rate even as deposited, against 5 for SACVD and 2.1 for LPCVD TEOS before densification; once densified all four films etch at 1.1–1.3 (Wolf p. 459, Table 9-1). The study filled trenches 0.5 µm deep and as narrow as 0.22 µm without voids; Wolf does not say with which film, and his table rates the gap fill of SACVD, HDP and the spin-on glass alike as acceptable. A two-step SACVD BPSG process later filled trenches 0.06 µm wide and 0.35 µm deep (Wolf p. 460).

7.2 Why a void forms, and how HDP-CVD prevents it

A CVD film at first covers the trench walls and bottom as well as the field. As it thickens, an overhang grows at the top of the trench, and the overhang closes the opening before the trench is full and seals a void inside (notes p. 141; Wolf p. 460). The notes and Wolf give no cause; Plummer's simulations of deposition topography do: a point near the top corner sees reactant arriving from a wider range of directions, its view angle, than a point on the wall or bottom, so more deposits there, and the corners build up into overhangs (Plummer Ch. 9). A film whose growth were perfectly conformal would not form one. The fill is easier the smaller the aspect ratio, the depth over the width: the BOX limit, 0.5 µm deep and 2 µm wide, is an aspect ratio of 0.25; the 0.5 µm by 0.22 µm trench of the Table 9-1 study, 2.27; the SACVD BPSG result, 5.8.

HDP-CVD prevents the overhang by etching it while it deposits (notes pp. 141–142; Wolf pp. 460–462). The gases are SiH₄, O₂ and Ar (or He). A high-density plasma source excites the oxygen and argon, and a bias on the wafer pulls ions out of the plasma and directs them at the surface. The oxygen reacts with the silane to deposit SiO₂, while the argon ions sputter deposited material away:

  • the sputter yield is highest on surfaces that face the ions at an angle, with the maximum at about 45° to the normal;
  • the net deposition rate is therefore higher on flat surfaces, the field and the trench bottoms, than on the 45° facets that form at the top corners of the trench;
  • by adjusting the deposition-to-etch ratio, the opening is kept from closing while the trench fills from the bottom up;
  • material sputtered from the bottom redeposits on the sidewalls and helps distribute the film.

The process runs below 10 mTorr, so ions cross the plasma sheath without collisions and arrive with the full energy of the sheath voltage, and travel nearly perpendicular to the wafer; a high-density source is needed to keep the ion density high at so low a pressure. The ion bombardment heats the wafer, so the deposition rate is limited by the highest sputter rate that can be tolerated. The sputtering can also clip the corners of the nitride. Wolf gives two remedies: sputtering with helium instead of argon, and "decrease the deposition-to-(sputter)-etching ratio", which "reduces the gap-filling capability", so a trade-off must be made. Taken literally, less deposition per unit of sputtering would clip more, not less; the remedy makes physical sense only if the ratio is read as etch to deposition, which is less sputtering (see the box below). At the end the surface carries sharp ridges and spikes, which CMP removes (Wolf p. 462).

Which way up is the ratio? The notes, like the caption of Wolf's Fig. 9-20, keep "the deposition-to-etch ratio… between 0.14 and 0.33" (p. 142). Read literally, a deposition rate of 0.14–0.33 times the etch rate would remove material 3 to 7 times faster than it arrives, and no film would grow. The range makes sense as the etch-to-deposition ratio: on flat surfaces 67–86 % of what deposits stays, and deposition outpaces sputtering 3.0–7.1 times. Wolf's clipping remedy, "decrease the deposition-to-(sputter)-etching ratio" at a cost in gap fill, is consistent only with that inverted reading too. His text also says "the ratio of deposition to removal rate should be no larger than 3.0" (p. 462). Taken literally, that excludes the whole 3.0–7.1 range except its end point; taken as one more inverted ratio, it adds no constraint. The direction of Wolf's ratio is therefore not settled by the source. Industry usage defines a deposition/sputter ratio of 3 and above, with lower values meaning more sputtering (e.g. US Patent 7,205,240), which fits the E/D reading. The page keeps the notes' figures and reads them as E/D; on a test, quote the notes' wording.

Why must an HDP-CVD gap fill be run at low pressure, and what limits how fast it can deposit?

Gap fill depends on directed ions: the argon ions must arrive nearly normal to the wafer with the full sheath energy, so that they sputter the 45° facets at the trench mouth fastest and keep it open. At pressures below about 10 mTorr they cross the sheath without collisions; at higher pressure, collisions would scatter them and remove both their energy and their direction. A high-density source keeps the ion flux high at that pressure. The deposition rate is limited by the sputtering it must be matched with: faster deposition needs proportionally more sputtering to keep the opening clear, and the ion bombardment heats the wafer, so the tolerable sputter rate caps the deposition rate (Wolf p. 462).

8 · Dishing in the STI polish

The final CMP of the trench fill must stop on the nitride, and wide trenches lose oxide during the overpolish: they dish (notes p. 143; Wolf pp. 462–468). Dishing in damascene metal was M14's subject (M14 §5); here the dished material is the field oxide, and a dished trench is a thinner field oxide and a less planar surface for the gate level. This section gives the cause, the size, and the five remedies.

Dishing occurs because the pad is not perfectly rigid and because oxide is removed faster than nitride (notes p. 143). Once the nitride is exposed, a perfectly rigid pad would press only on the nitride and the recessed trench oxide would see no pressure. A real pad flexes into a wide opening, pressure reaches the middle of the trench, and the oxide there is polished into a concave surface. The effect grows with the width of the field region: in one CMP process the field oxide thinned by more than 50 nm wherever it was wider than 100 µm (notes p. 143; Wolf Fig. 9-24). The polish should therefore stop as soon as the oxide over the nitride clears, and the quality of an STI polish is judged by its control of the trench-oxide and nitride thicknesses (Wolf p. 463).

The remedies, as the notes list them (pp. 143–144), with Wolf's explanations (pp. 464–468):

  1. RIE/CMP. The reverse-mask etchback of §3.3 thins the oxide over the active areas first, so the polish is shorter and dishes less; it also raises throughput. Its drawback is the extra complexity and cost.
  2. A nitride cap. A thin nitride is blanket-deposited on the trench fill before CMP, and a slurry with low oxide-to-nitride selectivity is used. The cap is polished off the raised oxide over the active areas, but it survives on the recessed oxide over the trenches, where it acts as a polish stop as the surfaces come level.
  3. Dummy active regions. Islands of unetched silicon are left inside wide trenches, which makes the trenches effectively narrower. Dummies are also needed around isolated active areas, because dense regions polish more slowly than isolated ones and the nitride over an isolated area would otherwise be polished through. They complicate design and masks, raise defect levels, and cannot be used in analog or mixed-signal circuits, where they make the coupling capacitance over the trenches variable.
  4. A patterned nitride cap. As 2, but the cap is removed from the active areas with a reverse block mask, so it remains only over the trenches.
  5. Direct polish. The fill is polished with none of the above, which puts the burden on the polish itself: a linear tool, or a rotary tool with a harder pad, an efficient endpoint system and, in Wolf's account, a relatively non-selective slurry; or a fixed-abrasive pad. Wolf's linear process used a ceria slurry with a nitride-inhibiting additive and held the oxide removed across a die to 20 nm at most.

Wolf credits the reduced dishing to the whole set of linear-process conditions, the selective ceria slurry included (p. 468). Part of it can be read from Preston's law, RR = KpPv (M13 §6), on this page's reading: the linear tool reaches its removal rate with a high pad velocity and a low pressure, and pressure is what the flexing pad transmits into the recessed trenches.

Linear against rotary polishing in Preston's law

Given Wolf's figures: linear polishers at 300–500 ft/min and 3–5 psi; rotary polishers at 34 ft/min and 7 psi (p. 468). Find the ratio of the Preston product Pv, linear to rotary, at the ends and middle of the linear range, and the ratio of the pressures.

Plan. At equal Preston constant, RR is proportional to Pv; the units cancel in the ratio.

Rotary. 7 × 34 = 238 psi·ft/min.

Linear. 3 × 300 = 900 (ratio 3.78); 4 × 400 = 1600 (6.72); 5 × 500 = 2500 (10.5).

Pressure. 3/7 = 0.43 to 5/7 = 0.71.

The linear tool's Pv is 3.8 to 10.5 times the rotary tool's, at 0.43–0.71 of the pressure. Check: the velocity multiplies the removal rate everywhere, the recessed trench oxide included, so clearing sooner does not by itself reduce dishing: for the same fractional overpolish the trench oxide loses the same amount. Only the lower pressure, which is what the pad transmits into the recess, works in the trenches' favour. The ratio assumes Preston's linear law, which M13 §6 showed is not exact.

The amount of overfill a process carries can be checked on the MIT density-and-pitch test wafer used to evaluate STI dishing: trenches 4800 Å deep, 150 Å of liner, 1960 Å of nitride and 8000 Å of TEOS (Wolf p. 468). The fill exceeds the 4800 + 1960 = 6760 Å step by 1240 Å less the pad-oxide thickness, which Wolf does not give; the notes' 8000–9000 Å over a 5000 Å trench carries a margin of the same order (§3.3).

A chip has both a 2 µm and a 200 µm wide trench. Which dishes more, and why? Which of the five remedies would you avoid on an analog chip, and why?

The 200 µm trench dishes more. Dishing happens because the pad flexes into an opening once the nitride is exposed, and the pressure it can exert on the recessed oxide grows with the width of the opening; in Wolf's data the loss exceeds 50 nm beyond 100 µm. Dummy active regions should be avoided on an analog chip: they make the capacitance of lines crossing the trenches variable, which disturbs analog circuits (Wolf p. 466). A nitride cap, RIE/CMP or a direct polish with a selective ceria slurry suits it instead.

Both technologies solve one problem, a field threshold above the supply between neighbouring devices, and the comparison shows where the difficulty moved. LOCOS's difficulties came from growing the oxide: encroachment, topography, thinning and stress. STI's come from the geometry of an etched hole: filling it, planarizing it, and the corner and sidewall where a gate or a well meets it. The trench also returns in EEE 5416, where trapped charge from radiation accumulates in the trench fill and inverts the same sidewall (EEE 5416 M08 §5; also EEL 5385 M14 §7).

Describe LOCOS isolation, its two drawbacks that could not be overcome below 0.35 µm, and how STI removes each, with a sketch of each structure.

LOCOS grows the field oxide by oxidizing the silicon through openings in a nitride mask laid over a pad oxide (sketch: nitride over the active areas, oxide lens between, beak under each nitride edge). The two drawbacks Wolf names are the bird's beak, oxide grown under the nitride edge by lateral diffusion of the oxidant through the pad oxide, which takes active width in proportion to the field-oxide thickness; and non-planarity, because the grown oxide is semi-recessed, 0.46 of it below the original surface and 0.54 above. STI etches a trench through a nitride hard mask, lines it with a thin thermal oxide and fills it with deposited oxide (sketch: trench with liner and fill, flush with the surface). Nothing is grown under the mask except the thin liner, so there is no bird's beak; and the fill is polished flat by CMP, stopping on the nitride, so the surface is planar.

Summary

  • LOCOS limits (notes p. 131): bird's beak into the active area, non-planarity, field-oxide thinning at narrow spacing, stress-induced defects. Step ≈ 0.54tox above the active surface (15.1). Encroachment ∝ field-oxide thickness, which isolation fixes.
  • BOX (1981/1983): 0.5–0.8 µm trenches, ~60° walls, 20–50 nm thermal oxide, CVD fill, resist etchback. Six drawbacks: voids (< 2 µm wide), sidewall inversion, varying field profile, imperfect planarization, gate-oxide thinning at the edge, 100 nm overetch.
  • Enablers: dry etch (slope, round bottom), gap-fill CVD (LPCVD TEOS, O₃–TEOS, HDP; stress < 200 MPa), CMP. Goal: a gate flat across the isolation.
  • Baseline flow: pad oxide 10–20 nm → LPCVD nitride 100–200 nm (hard mask + CMP stop) → mask #1 → etch nitride/pad → trench etch (70–85°, HBr/Cl₂/O₂ or HBr/NF₃/He/O₂) → strip → HF pad undercut → dry liner 40–50 nm (RTO 1050–1100 °C) → CVD fill 8000–9000 Å for 5000 Å → densify ~900 °C → reverse mask + planarizing resist + RIE → strip (spikes) → CMP to nitride → HF + hot H₃PO₄ → pad strip → sacrificial oxide 20 nm → wells, channel stop, VT, punch-through implants → strip → gate oxide.
  • Depth: ~0.5 µm → ~0.2 µm near 0.1 µm, set by the field threshold (8.6) and line capacitance (∝ 1/depth).
  • Sidewall MOSFET: n-well = gate, trench width = oxide (15.3); at Wolf's conditions it inverts below ≈ 0.8 µm; worse in p-wells (positive Qi, boron segregation); cured by implanting wells after STI. Deep trench: complex, disappointing latch-up results.
  • Corner: field crowding → hump; 50 nm radius at ≤ 80° removes it. Divot from HF dips: wrap-around, reverse narrow-width effect, ±200 mV VT, width loss. RELACS rounds inside a narrowed opening (~15 nm radius).
  • HDP-CVD: SiH₄/O₂/Ar, < 10 mTorr, sputter maximum ~45°, bottom-up fill; notes' ratio 0.14–0.33 read as E/D.
  • Dishing: pad flex + oxide faster than nitride; > 50 nm beyond 100 µm. Remedies: RIE/CMP, nitride cap, dummy actives, patterned cap, direct polish (linear 300–500 ft/min, 3–5 psi; ceria).
