Module 04

Silicon Wafer Preparation and the 300 mm Wafer

How a disc of dislocation-free single-crystal silicon flat to within a micrometre is produced from beach sand, and why the diameter grew to 300 mm.

Prerequisites M01 Read time ~40 min Sources Class notes pp. 13–16 (Chapter 2); Lectures 4, 5

Every process step in this course is performed on a wafer, and each one assumes properties the wafer must already have: a single crystal with a known orientation and no dislocations, a doping concentration uniform to a few percent, a surface flat enough that a lithographic exposure can bring a whole field into focus at once, and impurity levels low enough that the transition metals which destroy minority carrier lifetime are measured in parts per billion. None of those is a property of silicon. Each is the product of a processing step, and this module is the sequence of those steps.

The sequence divides into three parts. The first is chemical: extracting silicon from silica and purifying it to a degree that has few parallels in industrial chemistry. The second is crystallographic: converting a purified polycrystalline solid into one crystal. The third is mechanical: turning a cylinder of that crystal into flat discs. The module closes on the economics that drove the wafer diameter upward, and on what breaks when it rises.

1 · From sand to metallurgical-grade silicon

The starting material is sand, which is silicon dioxide. Silicon is recovered from it by carbothermic reduction: heating silica with carbon so that the carbon takes the oxygen and leaves the silicon behind. The carbon source is charcoal, and the reaction is performed in an electric arc furnace.

SiO₂ + 2C → Si + 2CO
(4.1)
Carbothermic reduction of silica. The equation as balanced produces carbon monoxide; some carbon dioxide forms as well where the monoxide meets excess oxygen. The gases leave the furnace and the silicon remains molten, since the furnace temperature exceeds the melting point of silicon, 1414 °C. On cooling it solidifies as a polycrystalline solid — many small crystals of random orientation rather than one continuous lattice. The class gives the furnace temperature as about 1400 °C; see the note at the end of this section.

The product is metallurgical-grade silicon, about 98% pure. That is adequate for its principal industrial uses, in aluminium alloys and in silicone chemistry, and it is nowhere near adequate for a transistor. A doping concentration of 10¹⁵ cm⁻³ is one dopant atom in fifty million silicon atoms, so an uncontrolled impurity at the percent level exceeds the intended doping by four orders of magnitude. The material must be purified by six or more orders of magnitude before a crystal is grown from it.

2 · Purification: trichlorosilane and the Siemens process

Purifying a solid directly is difficult. The route used instead converts silicon into a compound that is liquid near room temperature, purifies the liquid by distillation, and then converts it back. Distillation separates components by boiling point, and it can be repeated as many times as the required purity demands.

The metallurgical-grade silicon is first crushed to a powder, which exposes a large surface area to the reacting gas, and then reacted with hydrogen chloride.

Si + 3HCl ⇌ SiHCl₃ + H₂
(4.2)
Formation of trichlorosilane, SiHCl₃ — one silicon atom bonded to three chlorine atoms and one hydrogen. It boils at 31.8 °C, so it is a liquid at room temperature and a gas on gentle heating, which is what makes both distillation and subsequent transport in a gas stream possible. The reaction is reversible, and both directions are used: this one to make the compound, the reverse to recover the silicon. The class gives the temperature as about 900 °C; see the note at the end of this section.

The trichlorosilane produced is already purer than the silicon it came from, at about 99%, because most metallic impurities either do not form volatile chlorides or form ones with very different boiling points. Fractional distillation then raises it further, to better than 99.9999%.

How pure is electronic grade

The class gives the target as six nines, 99.9999%. That figure is one part per million of total impurity, and it is the specification for solar-grade polysilicon. Electronic-grade polysilicon from the Siemens process is specified at nine to eleven nines, with the electrically active impurities held below one part per billion by atom.

The difference is not academic, and the page's own specification table makes the point: a ULSI wafer is required to hold heavy metals below 0.001 ppb. Czochralski growth buys some purification, because most metals segregate strongly into the melt and are left behind — but boron and phosphorus barely segregate at all, so the dopant purity of the finished crystal is essentially the dopant purity of the feedstock. A six-nines feedstock cannot produce a wafer meeting the table two sections below.

The purified liquid is converted back to silicon in a Siemens reactor. A thin rod of high-purity silicon inside a chamber is heated by passing current through it, and trichlorosilane vapour with hydrogen is passed over it. At the rod surface equation (4.2) runs in reverse, depositing silicon onto the rod and releasing hydrogen chloride. Only a small fraction of the trichlorosilane passing through converts on any one pass, so the exhaust is not waste: the unreacted chlorosilanes, the hydrogen and the hydrogen chloride are separated and returned to the front of the process, and a plant that did not recover them would not be economic. The rod thickens as deposition continues, until a substantial polycrystalline silicon rod has been grown.

The rod is broken into chunks, which are the feedstock for crystal growth — handling it as a coarse solid rather than a powder keeps the surface area, and with it the opportunity for contamination, small. Note what has and has not been achieved: the material is now extremely pure, and it is still polycrystalline. Purity and crystallinity are separate problems solved by separate steps.

Temperatures and ambients in this chapter

The class gives 1400 °C for equation (4.1) and 900 °C for both directions of equation (4.2). Published values differ, and the differences are large:

  • Carbothermic reduction of silica is thermodynamically favourable only above roughly 1700 °C, and commercial submerged-arc furnaces operate at 1800–2000 °C.
  • Trichlorosilane synthesis, Si + 3HCl → SiHCl₃ + H₂, is run in a fluidised bed at about 300 °C. Above roughly 400 °C the yield shifts towards silicon tetrachloride, SiCl₄, which is the reason the temperature is kept low.
  • Siemens deposition runs at 1100–1175 °C, and the ambient is hydrogen, which participates in the reaction as the reducing agent rather than acting as an inert carrier.

Use the class values for anything assessed in this course. The point worth carrying away is the mechanism, which is unaffected: a reversible reaction run in one direction to make a distillable compound and in the other to recover the element.

3 · Czochralski growth

Converting the purified powder into a single crystal is done by Czochralski growth, in which a crystal is pulled slowly out of a melt. The powder is loaded into a crucible, melted, and a small seed crystal is dipped into the surface of the melt and withdrawn. Silicon freezing onto the seed adopts the seed's lattice, so the growing rod is one continuous crystal with the seed's orientation.

Three variables are controlled throughout. The melt temperature is held just above the melting point of silicon, a few degrees above 1414 °C. The seed is rotated, which averages the temperature the growth front sees around the circumference and so keeps the diameter uniform. The pull rate sets the diameter.

vrate ∝ 1Dcrystal
(4.3)
vrate pull rate (mm/min) · Dcrystal crystal diameter (mm). The relation as the class notes give it. A larger diameter requires a slower pull, because the latent heat released as silicon freezes has to leave through the crystal, and growing a larger wafer therefore costs time directly. The exponent is not −1. Doing the heat balance the sentence describes, the latent heat released per unit time goes as the cross-sectional area and so does the conduction that removes it, so the area cancels and what limits the pull rate is the axial temperature gradient at the interface. That gradient is set by how fast the crystal radiates heat from its cylindrical surface, which gives vmax ∝ D−1/2. Between 200 mm and 300 mm the notes' form predicts a 33% penalty in pull rate and the correct form predicts 18%.

The class describes pull rates below about a millimetre per minute, yielding a few centimetres per hour, and the notes state that typical growth time reaches 100 hours, four days, for a 300 mm crystal. Any flaw that develops in the crucible during that time can cause a loss of atomic alignment in the crystal, which makes the whole boule unusable. Four days of furnace time and 150 kg of purified silicon are lost with it.

The notes give the charge — the mass of silicon melted in the crucible — as 100 kg for 200 mm wafers in 1998, producing a crystal about one metre long, and about 150 kg for 300 mm. Those two are consistent with each other: at silicon's density of 2.33 g/cm³, 150 kg formed into a 300 mm cylinder is roughly 0.9 m of crystal. The finished crystal is called the boule, or the ingot.

How a 4 mm seed carries 150 kg

The seed rod is only three or four millimetres in diameter and the boule hanging from it weighs as much as two adults. It does not hold because hot silicon is strong — silicon is at its weakest near the melting point. Above about 600 °C silicon passes through a brittle-to-ductile transition, after which dislocations glide freely and the stress needed to deform it falls steeply with temperature; that softening is precisely why wafers slip and warp in a furnace, a fact §6.3 relies on. What holds is the part of the neck that has been withdrawn from the hot zone and is comparatively cool. Dislocation-free silicon in that state has a high fracture strength, of order 150 MPa in this geometry, and a 3 mm neck at that stress carries something like 140 kg — close enough to the charge mass that production 300 mm pullers add mechanical grippers to support the crystal rather than rely on the neck alone.

Immediately after the seed touches the melt the crystal is pulled rapidly, so that it narrows to a neck a few millimetres across before being allowed to widen. Contact with the melt generates dislocations — line defects in the crystal — in the seed, and dislocations in silicon glide on {111} planes, which are steeply inclined to the growth axis. As the neck lengthens, glide carries them sideways until they reach the free surface and are gone. What removes them is therefore the neck's length relative to its diameter, not the neck being thin in itself: the neck is grown to at least ten times its own diameter, in practice tens of millimetres and as much as 100 mm, before the crystal is allowed to widen. Everything grown after it is dislocation-free. The technique is due to Dash, and it is the reason a Czochralski boule can be grown without dislocations at all.

The growth ambient

Molten silicon reacts with oxygen, so growth is performed under a flowing inert gas. The class states that helium is used and that argon is not. Czochralski silicon is grown under argon essentially universally: it is inert, it is far cheaper than helium, and its low thermal conductivity relative to helium helps maintain the thermal gradients the process needs. Follow the class for anything assessed; the function of the gas — excluding oxygen and nitrogen from the melt surface — is the same either way.

One impurity is unavoidable. The crucible holding the melt is silica, and molten silicon dissolves it, so every Czochralski crystal contains oxygen at concentrations of several parts per million. For ULSI circuits the wafer must be free from grown-in defects and from oxide precipitates, which form where the dissolved oxygen exceeds its solubility limit and clusters. The notes prescribe a high-temperature anneal above 1200 °C to out-diffuse the oxygen near the surface, so that its concentration there drops below the solubility limit. The result is a surface layer clear of precipitates — a denuded zone — in which the devices are built, over a bulk that still contains oxygen.

The oxygen left in the bulk is not merely tolerated; it is wanted. Oxide precipitates deep in the wafer are strain centres that capture the fast-diffusing transition metals — iron, copper, nickel — which would otherwise reach the active surface and destroy minority carrier lifetime. Using the wafer's own precipitates as a trap for its own contamination is called intrinsic gettering, and the full treatment is a three-stage cycle rather than one anneal: a high-temperature step to denude the surface, a low-temperature step to nucleate precipitates in the bulk, and a second high-temperature step to grow them.

Float-zone growth, an alternative technique in which no crucible touches the silicon, avoids the oxygen entirely. The notes rule it out above 100 mm diameter; float-zone material is in fact available commercially to 200 mm, but not at 300 mm, and it is expensive and mechanically harder to grow at large diameter because there is nothing supporting the molten zone but its own surface tension. The conclusion the notes draw is the right one: Czochralski growth is the route to 300 mm.

4 · Why the diameter grew

A wafer is processed as a unit. The same furnace run, the same implant and the same wet clean treat every die on it, so the cost of those steps depends on the number of wafers and not on their size. Making the wafer larger therefore spreads a fixed cost over more dies, and the gain goes as the area.

A300A200 = (300200)2 = 2.25
(4.4)
A wafer area (cm² or in²) · the ratio is dimensionless. A 300 mm wafer has 2.25 times the area of a 200 mm wafer. The usable die count rises by slightly more than this, because the fixed edge exclusion zone is a smaller fraction of a larger wafer.

The cost per wafer rises too, but by less than the area. The notes give the figures below.

On the cost-per-area column the price of a square inch rises by half in going to 300 mm, which pays for the longer growth, the larger crucible and the new tooling. Against that, the area rises by a factor of 2.25 and every per-wafer process cost is now shared among 2.25 times as many dies. The 300 mm transition went ahead because the second effect is larger than the first, and that conclusion is robust to how the table's own inconsistencies are resolved.

The three columns of that table do not multiply out

Two things are worth checking before the figures are quoted. First, the areas are not computed on one convention: 28.3 in² and 50.2 in² are the areas of nominal 6-inch and 8-inch discs, whereas 109.5 in² is the area of a true 300 mm disc. On true metric diameters the three areas are 27.4, 48.7 and 109.6 in².

Second, the rows only multiply out for the first: 28.3 × $2 = $57, inside the quoted $50–60; but 109.5 × $3 = $329, which falls below the quoted $350–400. Working from the wafer-cost column instead gives $1.79 per in² at 200 mm against $3.42 at 300 mm, a rise of 1.9× rather than 1.5×. Either reading leaves the argument intact, because both are smaller than the 2.25× gain in area.

A larger wafer is not a scaled copy of a smaller one

The notes state that most tools needed to produce 200 mm wafers — crystal pullers, grinders, polishers, cleaners and inspection stations — are not suitable for 300 mm production. Three specific things change:

  • The quartz crucible must be about 30 inches in diameter, and it must survive four days at 1415 °C without developing a flaw.
  • Heat removal becomes the limiting constraint. Equation (4.3) says a larger diameter requires a slower pull, so innovative puller designs are needed that allow the heat to be removed more quickly than a conventional puller does.
  • The wafer must be thicker to resist its own weight. A 300 mm wafer is 50 µm — about 7% — thicker than a 200 mm wafer, and its back surface is polished, whereas on a 200 mm wafer the back side is not.

6 · From boule to wafer

The boule that emerges from the puller is not a smooth cylinder. Its diameter is deliberately grown slightly oversize and is not constant along its length, and it carries striations — bands of varying dopant concentration frozen in by small fluctuations in temperature and rotation at the growth front. The striations are internal rather than a surface texture; what the grinding step removes is the diameter variation. The mechanical sequence that follows removes that excess and produces flat discs.

6.1 Grinding and orientation marks

The boule is first ground on a diamond lathe to smooth the surface and bring it to exactly the specified diameter. The seed and the tail are cut off; the seed is reused.

Next, one or two flats are ground along the length of the cylinder. A flat is a plane ground parallel to the axis, and its purpose is to identify the crystal orientation and the doping type of the wafer after it has been sliced, when nothing else distinguishes one face or one rotational position from another. The position at which to grind is determined by X-ray diffraction, which identifies the crystal planes directly.

The primary flat is the longer of the two and establishes a reference direction; equipment registers wafers against it so that a whole boat of them is loaded in the same orientation. The secondary flat is shorter, and its angular position relative to the primary flat encodes the orientation and type.

Orientation and typeSecondary flat position
(100) p-type90° from primary
(100) n-type180° from primary
(111) p-typenone
(111) n-type45° from primary

Flats are used up to about 150 mm. Above that they are replaced by a small notch cut into the edge, for a reason that is purely economic: a flat removes a chord from the disc, and the area removed would otherwise have held finished dies. The professor's estimate for a 200 mm wafer is about six dies lost per flat, which at a few hundred dollars each is a substantial recurring loss. A notch identifies the orientation just as well and costs almost no area.

6.2 Slicing, lapping, etching

The ground boule is sliced into discs with a diamond saw. The blade is a fraction of a millimetre thick, and the silicon it converts to powder is lost from the product — the kerf loss. Roughly a third of the material is removed as powder. The class describes it as collected and returned to the crystal-growth feedstock; in practice the swarf carries abrasive, saw-wire metal and cutting fluid with it, so what is recovered economically is the abrasive and the fluid, and reclaiming the silicon itself yields a solar-grade material rather than something that can re-enter an electronic-grade crucible.

A sawn wafer is far from usable. The notes give the thickness and roughness at each stage of the finishing sequence, which is the most informative single statement of what wafer preparation actually consists of.

Lapping is mechanical grinding against an abrasive disc with water, performed on both faces. In the notes' cascade it takes the wafer from 914 µm to 814 µm, so it removes 100 µm in total — 50 µm from each face. It flattens the wafer but leaves its own damage: the abrasive fractures the silicon to a depth below the visible surface, a region of crushed and strained crystal called subsurface damage.

Chemical etching removes that damaged layer, taking 814 µm to 750 µm: 64 µm in total, or 32 µm per face. Because the etch is chemical it attacks the strained material without introducing new mechanical damage of its own. After it the roughness is about 2.5 µm, still far too rough for lithography but now free of subsurface damage.

The silicon etchant

The class gives the etchant as nitric acid with hydrochloric acid. Silicon etching by an acid mixture requires hydrofluoric acid, not hydrochloric: the nitric acid oxidises the silicon surface to SiO₂, and the HF dissolves that oxide so that fresh silicon is exposed and the cycle continues. Acetic acid is usually added as a diluent to moderate the rate. Hydrochloric acid does not dissolve SiO₂, so an HNO₃/HCl mixture would passivate after the first few atomic layers rather than etch.

The two-step oxidise-then-remove mechanism is worth holding onto, because it recurs exactly in the chemical mechanical polishing of section 6.4 and again throughout M13.

6.3 Edge contouring

The edge left by sawing is square and sharp. It is rounded on a diamond-coated lathe, a step called edge contouring, and the notes give two reasons.

The first is mechanical. Edge rounding substantially reduces the incidence of chipping during normal wafer handling, and an edge chip is not merely cosmetic: chip defect sites are known to nucleate plastic deformation, or slip, during subsequent thermal processing steps. A wafer heated to 1000 °C expands, and any stress concentration at a chipped edge can start a dislocation that then propagates inward across the wafer, through dies that were otherwise good.

The second concerns photoresist. Resist is applied as a liquid and spun to spread it into a uniform film. At a square edge the liquid piles up, forming a raised bead around the perimeter — the professor's term is a protrusion. In contact or proximity lithography that bead holds the mask off the wafer surface, introducing a gap that degrades resolution across the whole field, not only at the edge. A rounded edge lets the resist flow over the perimeter instead of accumulating at it. Edge rounding also minimises film build-up on the edge during epitaxial processes for the same reason.

6.4 Optical polishing by CMP

The final step produces a mirror surface — highly reflective, and free of scratches and damage. It is chemical mechanical polishing, CMP, and it is the same process, applied to bare silicon, that M13 and M14 treat as the planarisation technique for finished interconnect layers.

The wafer is mounted on a carrier and pressed against a rotating platen covered with a polishing pad, which is a polymer sheet or a fine cloth. The carrier and the platen rotate in opposite senses. A slurry is dripped onto the pad: a suspension of abrasive particles, typically about 10 nm across, in a chemically active liquid. For silicon polishing the abrasive is colloidal silica and the chemical is sodium hydroxide in water.

Neither component alone would work. The mechanism is chemical attack followed by mechanical removal of the product. Frictional heating from the sliding wafer drives the reaction between the sodium hydroxide and the silicon surface, converting it to a soluble silicate.

Si + 2NaOH + H₂O → Na₂SiO₃ + 2H₂
Si + 4NaOH → Na₄SiO₄ + 2H₂
(4.5)
The two silicate products the class gives: sodium metasilicate, Na₂SiO₃, and sodium orthosilicate, Na₄SiO₄. Which one dominates depends on the hydroxide concentration, and the class does not distinguish them. Either way the product is a soft surface film, far more easily abraded than the silicon beneath it.

The silica particles then abrade away the silicate film, exposing fresh silicon, which reacts again. The cycle repeats for the duration of the polish. This is why CMP removes material smoothly rather than scratching. Two things have to hold at once: the particle must be harder than the silicate film it is removing, and softer than the silicon underneath it. Colloidal silica satisfies both because it is amorphous, with a Knoop hardness around 600 against silicon's 1150 — crystalline quartz, chemically the same material, is hard enough to scratch silicon and is not used. The chemical step is what makes the removal possible at all, by converting the surface into something an abrasive that soft can take away. The roughness falls from about 2.5 µm before polishing to less than 100 Å after it.

Polishing was originally applied to one face only. Double-side polishing, used for 300 mm wafers, produces superior flatness than single-sided polishing, because clamping an unpolished back face against a chuck transfers its irregularities to the front.

CMP has a limit to the degree of flatness it can obtain, and it is a global process: it cannot correct flatness variation that differs from place to place across the wafer. To supplement and fine-tune the local flatness for lithography, plasma-assisted chemical etching, PACE, was developed. PACE is driven by chemically reactive neutral radicals rather than by accelerated ions: the wafer carries no applied bias, so nothing arrives with enough energy to displace silicon atoms, and the subsurface damage that reactive ion etching leaves does not appear. The class describes the working species as ions well below 1 eV; a plasma sheath sets a floor of tens of electronvolts on ion energy, so the damage-free behaviour comes from the absence of ion bombardment rather than from unusually slow ions. Because it is a localised process, the removal can be steered to the high points measured on an individual wafer.

Wafer cost per unit area rises by 50% from 200 mm to 300 mm. Why did the transition happen anyway, and which class of process step benefits least?

Because area rises by 125% against a 50% rise in cost per unit area. A step whose cost is per wafer — a furnace run, an implant, a lithographic exposure of a stepper field repeated across the wafer, a wet clean — is now shared among 2.25 times as many dies, so its contribution to the cost of a die falls by more than the wafer got expensive.

The steps that benefit least are those whose cost already scales with area rather than with wafer count. Lithography is the clearest case: a stepper exposes one field at a time and must perform 2.25 times as many exposures on a 300 mm wafer, so the exposure time per die is unchanged and only the overheads of loading and aligning the wafer are amortised. This is part of why lithography has become the dominant cost in a modern process, and why the arguments in M12 matter economically as well as physically.

Summary

  • SiO₂ + 2C → Si + 2CO in an arc furnace gives metallurgical-grade silicon at about 98% purity — polycrystalline and far too impure for devices.
  • Purification goes through a liquid: Si + 3HCl ⇌ SiHCl₃ + H₂ makes trichlorosilane, fractional distillation purifies it, and the reverse reaction in a Siemens reactor deposits electronic-grade polysilicon onto a heated silicon rod. The class gives the target as six nines; electronic grade is specified at nine to eleven.
  • Czochralski growth pulls a single crystal from a melt held just above 1414 °C. The seed is rotated for diameter uniformity, and a larger diameter requires a slower pull, so a larger wafer costs growth time directly — the notes put the pull rate as 1/D, the heat balance gives D−1/2. A 300 mm charge is about 150 kg and takes about 100 hours. The neck holds the crystal because it is cool and dislocation-free, not because hot silicon is strong.
  • The crucible is silica, so every Czochralski crystal contains oxygen. An anneal above 1200 °C out-diffuses it near the surface, leaving a denuded zone in which the devices are built; the precipitates left in the bulk are wanted, because they getter the transition metals that would otherwise reach the surface.
  • Float zone avoids the oxygen but does not reach 300 mm — the notes cap it at 100 mm, commercial material reaches 200 mm — so Czochralski is the route.
  • A 300 mm wafer has 2.25 times the area of a 200 mm wafer at 1.5 to 1.9 times the cost per unit area depending on which column of the notes' table is believed, and that gap is the whole economic argument. It is also 50 µm thicker and polished on both sides, and it obsoleted most 200 mm tooling.
  • Flats identify orientation and type up to 150 mm — (100) p-type has its secondary flat at 90° — and are replaced by a notch at 200 mm and above, because a flat costs die area.
  • The finishing sequence for a 200 mm wafer: saw to 914 µm at 76 µm roughness; lap to 814 µm at 12.5 µm, removing 50 µm a side; chemically etch to 750 µm at under 2.5 µm, removing 32 µm a side; contour the edge; polish to 725 µm at under 100 Å.
  • CMP works by chemical attack followed by abrasion of the soft reaction product: NaOH converts the surface to a sodium silicate, and amorphous colloidal silica — harder than the silicate, softer than silicon — removes it. PACE supplements it for local flatness, and leaves no subsurface damage because it is radical-driven with no applied bias.
