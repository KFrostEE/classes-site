Module 02

The Deep-Submicron MOSFET: A Structural Roadmap

Every part of the transistor that changed as the gate length fell from ten micrometres to one tenth of a micrometre, why each change was forced, and which later module treats it in full.

Prerequisites M01 Read time ~40 min Sources Class notes pp. 1–12 (Chapter 1); Lectures 1–4

The transistor described in M01 is the device as it existed around 1975: an aluminium gate over a thermally grown oxide, a uniformly doped body, and source and drain regions formed by diffusion. Almost none of that survives in a modern part. The gate is a silicide-capped polysilicon stack, the dielectric is a hafnium compound, the body carries a doping profile engineered in three dimensions, and the drain is built from two or three separately implanted regions. Each of those replacements was forced by a specific failure that appeared at a specific gate length, and each is the subject of a later chapter of this course.

This module is the map of those replacements. It states the scaling history that drove them, then walks the four structural axes along which the transistor was rebuilt — the gate stack, the gate dielectric, the channel doping profile, and the drain structure — followed by the substrate, the gate doping, and the isolation between devices. Every topic here is introduced to the depth needed to see why it exists and what it replaced; the mechanism, the process recipe and the numbers arrive in the module named alongside it. Read this page as the argument the rest of the course elaborates.

1 · The gate length history

The gate length is the distance, measured along the direction of current flow, over which the gate electrode controls the silicon surface beneath it. It sets the drain current through the factor Z/L of the drain-current expression, it sets the time a carrier needs to cross the channel, and it sets how many transistors fit on a die. Reducing it improves all three at once, which is why it has been reduced continuously since integrated MOSFETs were first manufactured.

The figures the notes give are 10 µm in 1970, 3–5 µm in 1980, and 2 µm in 1985, with gate lengths dropping below 1 µm in the late 1980s and reaching 0.5–0.35 µm by the late 1990s. The 2001–2002 generation spanned 0.25 µm to 0.13 µm, and the sequence continued through the 90 nm, 65 nm and 45 nm generations.

Below about 130 nm, the generation name is not the gate length

The number naming a technology generation and the physical gate length of the transistors built in it were the same quantity only down to the 0.35 µm generation. They began to separate at 0.25 µm, where the physical gate length was already about 200 nm, and by the 130 nm generation the gap was close to a factor of two — that generation shipped 70 nm transistors. The 90 nm generation shipped gate lengths near 50 nm, 65 nm shipped near 35 nm, and from the 22 nm generation the name became a marketing label with no dimension on the die corresponding to it. Physical gate length has stayed roughly in the 20–30 nm range since about 2011, while generation names continued to fall.

The history above is stated in the course's own terms, where the two are treated as one number. Keep the distinction in mind whenever a post-2005 figure is quoted: it is almost always a generation name, and the transistor is larger than the name suggests.

Reducing the gate length alone does not work. The gate controls the channel by competing with the drain for control of the potential in the silicon between source and drain, and shortening the channel strengthens the drain's share of that competition. Preserving gate control requires the oxide to thin, the source and drain junctions to become shallower, and the body doping to rise — each of which introduces a new failure of its own. The rest of this module is the catalogue of those failures and their fixes.

2 · Two transistors, twenty-five years apart

The clearest statement of what changed is a pair of cross-sections. The first is a 1.5 µm device: a rectangular gate on a thin oxide, with a source and a drain that are single uniformly doped regions reaching a junction depth xjc below the surface. Four dimensions describe it — the drawn gate length LL, the gate thickness tgate, the oxide thickness tox, and the junction depth.

The second is a 0.2 µm device. The gate has acquired an insulating sidewall spacer of thickness tsw — a wedge of deposited oxide standing against each vertical face of the gate, whose purpose is to hold a later implant a fixed distance away from the gate edge. The drain is now two regions: a shallow drain extension of depth xj that meets the channel, and a deeper heavily doped contact region of depth xjc that carries the metal contact. Beneath and around the extension sits a pocket implant, a locally heavier dose of the same dopant type as the body.

The notes organise the difference between these two figures into four axes — gate stack, gate dielectric, channel doping profile, drain structure — and then add three further structural changes that are not visible in a single cross-section: the substrate the wafer is cut from, the doping of the gate material itself, and the structure isolating one transistor from the next. The remaining sections take those in order.

3 · The gate stack

The gate electrode must do two things. It must hold a controlled potential over the channel, and it must carry that potential from wherever the signal originates to the transistor, which makes it a length of wire as well as a plate. The two requirements pull in different directions, and the history of the gate stack is the history of satisfying both.

3.1 Aluminium to polysilicon

The original gate material was aluminium. It was replaced by polycrystalline silicon — polysilicon, silicon deposited as a film of small randomly oriented crystal grains rather than as a single crystal — in the mid-1970s. Two properties drove the change. Polysilicon withstands the temperatures of the subsequent dopant-activation anneals, which aluminium, melting at 660 °C, does not; that thermal tolerance is what allows the gate to be patterned before the source and drain are formed, so that the gate itself masks the implant and the three regions self-align. The second property is the work function.

VT = φMS − QiCox − QdCox + 2φF
(2.1)
VT threshold voltage (V) · φMS gate-to-semiconductor work function difference (V) · Qi interface charge per unit area (C/cm²) · Qd depletion charge per unit area (C/cm²) · Cox oxide capacitance per unit area (F/cm²) · φF Fermi potential of the body (V). This is the professor's four-term form, with signs carried explicitly per device type. It is derived term by term in M03; it appears here because the first term is what decides the gate material.

The work function of a material is the energy needed to remove an electron from it to free space, and φMS is the difference between that quantity for the gate and for the silicon body. It enters the threshold voltage directly, volt for volt. Aluminium has one fixed work function, so an aluminium gate offers no adjustment. Polysilicon is silicon, and its work function moves with its doping, which converts φMS from a constant of nature into a process variable. That is the lever M03 pulls, and the reason the gate of a modern transistor is doped as heavily as the source and drain.

3.2 Polysilicon to polycide

Polysilicon is a poor conductor by the standards of a metal even when doped to its solubility limit. NMOS and CMOS technologies down to 0.35 µm used n⁺ polysilicon — polysilicon doped heavily with phosphorus — for the gate. Below 1.25 µm gate length the resistance of polysilicon lines became too large for them to serve as local interconnect, despite the heavy doping, because a line that is shorter in one dimension is also narrower in the others and its resistance rises as the cross-section falls.

The response was to cap the polysilicon with a metal silicide, a compound of a metal with silicon that conducts far better than doped silicon and tolerates the same process temperatures. The silicide used was tungsten silicide, WSix. The resulting two-layer gate — silicide over polysilicon — is called a polycide, and it reduced the resistance of the heavily doped layer to one fourth of its previous value. The polysilicon is retained beneath because the electrical behaviour of the gate depends on what touches the oxide, and a silicide in that position would forfeit the work-function control that motivated polysilicon in the first place.

3.3 Polycide to salicide

In deep-submicron CMOS the tungsten silicide was replaced by titanium disilicide, TiSi₂, or cobalt disilicide, CoSi₂, both of which have lower resistivities than WSix. The change brought a second benefit that is more important than the resistivity. Both of these silicides form by depositing the metal over the whole wafer and annealing: the metal reacts readily with silicon wherever it touches silicon, and reacts with oxide far more slowly. Since the gate is capped by exposed polysilicon and the source and drain are exposed single-crystal silicon, while the sidewall spacers and the isolation regions are oxide, one blanket deposition forms silicide on exactly the three places it is wanted, and the metal left lying over the oxide is then stripped away.

The anneal is performed in two stages rather than one, with the strip between them. The first anneal, at roughly 600–700 °C for titanium, forms a high-resistivity intermediate phase; the unreacted metal is then removed by a selective wet etch that attacks the metal and not the silicide; and a second anneal at 800–900 °C converts the intermediate phase into the low-resistivity one. Reaching the low-resistivity phase in a single high-temperature step is what the two-stage sequence exists to avoid, because silicon transported sideways through a metal film still lying over the spacer forms silicide bridges from the gate to the source and drain. The selectivity of the process is a matter of reaction rate rather than an absolute prohibition — good enough to build on, and the sidewall spacer is what makes it good enough.

A structure formed this way is a self-aligned silicide, contracted to salicide. No lithography step defines where the silicide goes; the existing geometry does. The notes give two benefits:

  • the polysilicon lines have lower resistivity because of the silicide on top of them, which is the polycide benefit carried over; and
  • the source and drain contact regions exhibit smaller contact resistance and smaller sheet resistivity than a contact without a silicide.

The second benefit addresses a problem that grows as the transistor shrinks. The resistance in series with the channel — through the contact, through the extension, and through the region beneath the spacer — does not fall as the channel resistance falls, so it becomes a larger fraction of the total. Silicide on the source and drain removes the largest component of it. M10 treats the full series-resistance budget and the silicide process.

4 · The gate dielectric

The gate dielectric is the insulating layer separating the gate from the channel. Its thickness sets Cox, the oxide capacitance per unit area, and therefore how much inversion charge a given gate voltage induces. Silicon dioxide served in this role from the beginning, for reasons treated in M05: it grows directly from the silicon it insulates, and the interface it forms carries remarkably few electrically active defects.

The oxide cannot stay the same thickness while the gate length falls. If it did, the drain would gain control of the channel potential at the gate's expense, and the transistor would conduct between source and drain regardless of the gate voltage. The notes state the relation the industry maintained as a single ratio.

LEtox ≈ 45
(2.2)
LE gate length (any length unit) · tox gate oxide thickness (the same unit). A dimensionless design rule, not a physical law: it records the ratio at which gate control over the channel stayed adequate. Both quantities must be expressed in the same unit before dividing.

The rule fixes the oxide thickness once the gate length is chosen. The notes give three points on it: a 1 µm gate needs tox ≈ 200 Å, a 0.18 µm gate needs 40 Å, and a gate shorter than 0.1 µm needs about 20 Å.

Twenty ångströms is 2 nm, which is ten to twelve atomic layers of SiO₂. At that thickness electrons pass through the oxide by tunnelling — the quantum mechanical process in which a particle crosses a barrier it does not have the energy to surmount, at a rate that falls exponentially with barrier thickness. The notes state the consequence precisely: at about 20 Å the gate-to-channel leakage current becomes comparable to the off-state source-to-drain leakage current, roughly 10 nA/µm of channel width. A gate that leaks as much as the channel it is supposed to be switching off has stopped being an insulated gate, and the notes conclude that building high-performance logic gates with SiO₂ is then no longer feasible.

The thickness that matters is electrical, not physical

What the transistor responds to is Cox = εrε0/tox, and εr is a property of the material. A dielectric with a relative permittivity higher than the 3.9 of SiO₂ therefore delivers the same capacitance at a greater physical thickness — and tunnelling depends on the physical thickness, not the capacitance. This is the entire argument for high-k dielectrics: a thicker layer that behaves electrically like a thin one, and so leaks far less. The notes make exactly this point at the end of §1.2. M07 states the argument quantitatively and surveys the candidate materials.

The same limit constrains storage as well as logic. DRAM cell capacitors and the oxides used in non-volatile memory face stringent thickness limitations for the same reason — in the memory case the requirement is more severe still, because a stored charge must survive for years rather than for one clock cycle.

5 · The channel doping profile

The channel is the region of the body directly beneath the gate oxide, and its doping concentration sets two things at once: the threshold voltage, through the depletion-charge term of equation (2.1), and the carrier mobility, because a carrier moving through the inversion layer scatters from the ionised dopant atoms around it. Raising the doping raises the threshold and lowers the mobility. The history of channel engineering is the history of getting the first without paying the second.

5.1 Uniform doping and the threshold implant

In early MOSFETs the channel doping was flat with depth below the Si/SiO₂ interface. In NMOS the flatness came directly from the starting wafer, which was uniformly doped throughout. In CMOS a well — a region of the opposite doping type to the substrate, in which transistors of one polarity are built — was introduced near the surface by a shallow implant followed by a drive-in anneal, a high-temperature step that lets the implanted dopant diffuse until its profile is approximately constant near the surface. Either route gives a body of one doping concentration from the surface down past the depletion region.

A uniform body fixes the threshold voltage at whatever value the substrate doping happens to give, which is rarely the value the circuit needs. The fix is a threshold-adjust implant: a low-dose ion implantation into the channel region that changes the doping in the first few tens of nanometres of silicon without disturbing the bulk. The notes record that boron is the species used for both NMOS and PMOS devices. Boron is an acceptor, so it makes the near-surface silicon of an n-channel device more strongly p-type, raising the n-channel threshold; in a p-channel device built in an n-type well, the same boron partially compensates the donor doping, which also moves the p-channel threshold in the direction wanted. One species and one implant step serves both. M09 works through the arithmetic and the placement of the implant peak.

5.2 Super-steep retrograde profiles

As the gate length falls, the depletion regions surrounding the source and drain junctions extend further into the channel relative to its length. When they meet, current flows from source to drain beneath the surface and the gate cannot stop it. This is punch-through. Raising the body doping shrinks those depletion regions and postpones it, but raising the doping everywhere degrades the mobility of the carriers in the inversion layer, which is exactly the region where mobility matters most.

The resolution is to make the doping profile non-uniform in depth: light where the carriers travel, heavy where the depletion regions need to be stopped. A profile that increases with depth rather than decreasing is called retrograde, and the version steep enough to serve at deep-submicron dimensions is super-steep retrograde, abbreviated SSR. The notes state the target it achieves: the correct value of VT without degradation of channel carrier mobility, by keeping the doping in the inversion layer below 5×10¹⁷ cm⁻³.

Holding a steep profile requires a dopant that does not diffuse away during the anneals that follow. The notes specify heavier dopant species than the usual ones: indium replaces boron for n-channel devices, and arsenic or antimony replaces phosphorus for p-channel devices. These species have smaller diffusivities in silicon, so the implanted profile does not spread during subsequent anneal cycles.

Low diffusivity is not the whole story for indium, and the two properties it lacks are what decide how far the technique scales. Indium is a deep acceptor, with a level about 0.16 eV above the valence band edge, so only a fraction of it is ionised at room temperature and its electrically active concentration is well below its chemical concentration — a fraction that itself varies with temperature, which appears directly as threshold-voltage temperature sensitivity. Indium also has a low solid solubility in silicon, of order 10¹⁸ cm⁻³, which caps how high the retrograde peak can be made. Together these are why indium alone stops being sufficient as a channel dopant at gate lengths near 60 nm and below: it cannot reach the concentration needed to hold off the leakage.

Low diffusivity is not a consequence of atomic mass

Dopant diffusion in silicon proceeds through point defects, and the mechanism differs by species. Boron and phosphorus move predominantly by an interstitial-mediated mechanism and are fast — phosphorus included, despite being a column-V donor like arsenic and antimony. Antimony is the one of the four that is genuinely vacancy-dominated, and it is slow. Arsenic sits between them, diffusing by interstitial and vacancy mechanisms in roughly equal measure, which is why it shows both oxidation-enhanced and nitridation-enhanced diffusion. That the slower species are also the heavier ones is a correlation, not the cause. The distinction matters later: transient enhanced diffusion, in which implant damage temporarily floods the lattice with interstitials and accelerates boron enormously, is unintelligible under a mass-based explanation. M09 treats it.

5.3 Halo and pocket implants

The profile can also be made non-uniform along the channel, from source to drain. Conventional MOSFETs used a blanket punch-through implant — blanket meaning applied uniformly over the whole wafer, so that the lateral doping concentration is the same everywhere along the channel. A blanket implant that is heavy enough to stop punch-through at the drain edge is heavier than the rest of the channel needs, and the notes state the consequence directly: it causes performance degradation, because the threshold voltage rises everywhere and the mobility falls everywhere.

The alternative is to introduce the dopant only where punch-through actually begins, at the edge of the drain-extension region, leaving the rest of the channel with the lighter doping a low threshold voltage requires. The extra doped region surrounds the edge of the drain extension, and from that geometry the structure takes the name halo. It is also called an anti-punch-through structure.

The implant is self-aligned to the gate, in the same way the source and drain are: the gate blocks the ions from the channel it covers, so the doped region places itself at the gate edge with no separate alignment step. The species is the same as the one already present in the well, so the halo reinforces the body doping rather than introducing a new junction. Its energy is somewhat higher than the energy used for the drain-extension implant, which means a greater range and a greater lateral straggle — the spread of implanted ions perpendicular to the beam — so the halo region extends beyond the extension it surrounds.

In more advanced technology the second implant is performed with the ion beam directed at a large angle to the wafer rather than perpendicular to it. Tilting the beam places the peak concentration at a precise depth and a precise lateral distance from the gate edge, because the gate now shadows a controlled width of channel. The structure is named a large-angle tilted implanted punch-through structure, LATIPS, and is also known as a pocket implant. It is the shaded region in the 0.2 µm cross-section above.

6 · The drain structure

For early MOSFETs with 10 µm channel lengths, the drain side of the pn junction at the end of the channel was a single region of one doping. Two things changed that, in sequence: first a reliability failure at 2 µm, then a resistance problem at 0.18 µm created by the fix for the first.

6.1 Hot carrier injection, and the lightly doped drain

As the channel length reduced to 2 µm, the electric field in the depletion region of the drain junction grew large enough to produce severe hot electron injection. The mechanism is as follows. The voltage between drain and source did not fall in proportion to the channel length, so the field along the channel rose. Electrons crossing the high-field region near the drain gain kinetic energy between collisions faster than they lose it, reaching energies well above the thermal average — they are "hot" relative to the lattice. A carrier with enough energy can surmount the energy barrier between silicon and silicon dioxide and enter the gate oxide, where it may be trapped. Trapped charge in the oxide shifts the threshold voltage by the term Qi/Cox of equation (2.1), and the shift grows with operating time. The notes record the consequence as long-term reliability degradation: a device that met specification when manufactured drifts out of specification in service.

Field is voltage gradient, so reducing the peak field means spreading the voltage drop over a longer distance. That is achieved by placing a lightly doped region between the channel and the heavily doped drain. A lightly doped region supports a wider depletion layer for the same voltage, so the same potential difference is distributed over more distance and the peak field falls. The structure is a two-region drain: the region connected to the channel is the lightly doped drain, LDD, at about 10¹⁷ cm⁻³, and the second region away from the gate, connected to the first, is heavily doped at about 10²⁰ cm⁻³.

Producing the two regions in the correct places requires the sidewall spacer. The notes give the process in four steps:

  1. After patterning the gate stack, the lightly doped drain region is implanted using a medium-current implanter. The gate itself aligns the drain dopant to the gate edge.
  2. A spacer is formed by chemical vapour deposition of SiO₂ over the whole wafer, followed by an anisotropic etch — one that removes material faster vertically than horizontally — which clears the flat surfaces and leaves oxide standing only against the vertical sidewalls of the gate.
  3. A high-current implanter introduces the dopants for the second, heavily doped contact region. The spacer now stands between the beam and the LDD region and prevents these species from entering it.
  4. An anneal activates the dopants implanted in both steps and drives the edge of the lightly doped region the proper distance beneath the edge of the gate.

The spacer is the entire mechanism. Its width sets how far the heavy implant stays from the gate, and therefore the length of the lightly doped region, without any lithography step defining it. M10 gives the deposition chemistry, the etch, and the reflow step.

6.2 Source–drain extension

For a 0.18 µm MOSFET the power supply voltage was reduced from 5 V to 2 V. Hot carrier injection depends on the field, and the field depends on the supply voltage, so at 2 V across a 0.18 µm channel the notes state that hot carrier injection no longer needs to be controlled by an LDD structure. What does need controlling is the resistance the LDD introduced: a lightly doped region is by definition resistive, and its contribution to the overall on-resistance of the MOSFET had risen to an unacceptably large degree.

The two-region drain is retained, but the near region is redefined. Its implant dose is raised from 1–5×10¹³ cm⁻² to 5–10×10¹⁴ cm⁻², roughly a factor of twenty, which produces a region of much lower resistivity. Because its peak doping is no longer lighter than that of the contact region, the name "lightly doped drain" no longer describes it, and the notes call it the source/drain extension, SDE. The peak doping concentration in the extension is as high as that of the contact region, but its junction depth is shallower. The second region is called the contact region.

The shallow junction is the point. A shallower junction is what limits how far the drain field reaches along the channel, which is the short-channel control the structure now exists to provide.

7 · Substrates for deep-submicron CMOS

The wafer itself changed. The notes specify two options: p⁺ substrates carrying a lightly doped p-type silicon epitaxial layer, or n⁺ substrates carrying a lightly doped n-type epitaxial layer. An epitaxial layer is single-crystal silicon grown on a single-crystal silicon surface, inheriting the orientation and lattice of the material beneath it, so that the boundary between the two is crystalline but the doping across it is a step.

The transistors are built entirely in the lightly doped epitaxial layer, which is where light doping is needed for mobility and threshold control. The heavily doped substrate beneath contributes a low-resistance path through the wafer. That path suppresses latch-up, a failure mode specific to CMOS in which the parasitic bipolar transistors formed by the n and p regions of adjacent devices turn each other on and conduct a large current from supply to ground until power is removed. Latch-up needs resistance in the substrate to develop the voltage that triggers it; a p⁺ substrate removes that resistance. M04 covers how wafers are produced, and M08 covers what the wells built into them do.

8 · Dual-doped polysilicon

Conventional CMOS at 0.35 µm used n⁺ polysilicon as the gate material for both the n-channel and the p-channel devices. For the n-channel device this is the right choice: it produces a surface-channel MOSFET, in which the inversion layer forms directly at the Si/SiO₂ interface where the gate controls it most strongly.

For the p-channel device, n⁺ polysilicon gives the wrong work function. The resulting φMS requires a threshold-adjust implant heavy enough that the conducting layer forms not at the surface but at some depth below it. Such a device is a buried-channel MOSFET. A buried channel is further from the gate than a surface channel, so the gate controls it less well, and the notes state the consequence: buried-channel PMOSFETs exhibit off-state leakage currents that are unacceptably large once MOSFETs are scaled to 0.25 µm gate lengths.

Building a surface-channel PMOSFET requires p⁺ polysilicon as the gate material, which means the two device types now need gates of opposite doping. That is dual-doped polysilicon, and it introduces additional process steps: the two gate regions must be doped separately and masked from each other.

Boron penetration through the gate oxide

The p-type dopant introduced into the p⁺ polysilicon is boron, and boron diffuses rapidly. Unless a sufficiently low thermal budget is used after the poly doping step, boron traverses the gate oxide and enters the silicon substrate beneath. The notes state the damage precisely: such penetration causes unacceptable variation in the threshold voltage of the PMOSFETs, because the boron that arrives in the channel changes its doping and therefore its VT. A thin oxide makes this worse, since the distance to be crossed is shorter — which is why boron penetration is one of the constraints on oxide scaling in M05.

A second failure follows from the same mobility, in a different direction. Boron also diffuses along a polysilicon line, and faster still through any silicide capping it, so boron implanted into a p⁺ gate can travel sideways into n⁺ polysilicon elsewhere on the same line and counter-dope it. That changes the gate doping, and with it the work function and the threshold voltage, of devices that were masked from the boron implant entirely. Dual-gate processes respond by keeping the n⁺/p⁺ transition away from active gates.

9 · Isolation: LOCOS to shallow trench

Neighbouring transistors on a die must not conduct into one another. The isolating structure between them is field oxide: a thick oxide, typically about one micrometre, lying over the silicon everywhere no transistor is being built, so that any metal line crossing that region is too far from the silicon to invert it.

The technology that produced it was LOCOS, local oxidation of silicon. A thin thermal oxide — the pad oxide, typically 10 to 50 nm — is grown first to decouple the silicon from the stress of the film above it; a silicon nitride film is patterned over that pad oxide across the regions that will become transistors; and the wafer is then oxidised. Oxide grows thickly everywhere the nitride is absent, because nitride is nearly impermeable to oxygen, and hardly at all where it is present. The notes record that LOCOS served NMOS and PMOS technologies until the gate length reached the 0.35 µm generation, and that it failed there for two reasons.

The first is the bird's beak, and the pad oxide is what causes it. Oxygen arriving at the edge of the nitride diffuses sideways through that pad oxide and oxidises the silicon underneath the nitride edge. Oxidation also consumes silicon and produces an oxide occupying roughly 2.2 times the volume of the silicon it replaced, and that expansion lifts the nitride edge, giving the growing wedge its characteristic taper. The field oxide therefore encroaches into the active areas of the chip, and the width lost is set by the oxidation, not by the lithography — which is why the beak is shortened by thinning the pad oxide or thickening the nitride, and not by anything the mask designer can do. At large dimensions that loss is tolerable; at 0.35 µm it is a substantial fraction of the device.

The second is topography. Because the field oxide is grown thermally and is only semi-recessed — partly below and partly above the original silicon surface — the wafer surface after LOCOS is not planar. Non-planar topography is a direct difficulty for lithography, which must bring a whole field into focus at once, and for the metal layers deposited over it.

Shallow trench isolation, STI, solves both. A trench is etched into the silicon where isolation is wanted, and filled with deposited oxide rather than grown oxide. Nothing is consumed, so there is no bird's beak and no encroachment; and the fill is polished back flat, so the surface is planar. M15 gives the full flow, and the polishing step it depends on is the subject of M13.

10 · Silicon on insulator

In bulk silicon technology the pn junctions separating one device from another are reverse-biased, and a reverse-biased junction conducts only a small leakage current. That is not the whole story. The notes state that current paths among devices may still be activated in the bulk region by such phenomena as latch-up or transient radiation effects — a burst of ionising radiation generates carriers throughout the silicon volume at once, and those carriers provide the conduction the reverse bias was supposed to prevent. Furthermore, the parasitic circuit elements associated with the CMOS structure, such as source-to-substrate and drain-to-substrate capacitances, become too large in structures using bulk silicon; those capacitances must be charged and discharged on every transition, so they cost speed and power.

Silicon on insulator removes the shared bulk. The transistors are built in a thin single-crystal silicon film sitting on a buried oxide layer, so no conduction path exists beneath them and the junction capacitances terminate on an insulator rather than on a substrate. The notes give this ten lines and no more, and the professor indicated it may become a project topic rather than a lecture; M17 holds the treatment.

11 · The scaling limits of the conventional structure

Chapter 1 of the notes closes with a table. Below 0.1 µm, the notes state, MOSFET devices need to adopt different structural changes — meaning that the modifications catalogued above run out, and each of six features reaches a value beyond which it cannot usefully go. The reason column is the important one: every limit is either a leakage current that grows or a resistance that grows.

FeatureLimitReason
Oxide thickness2 nmLeakage (Igate)
Junction depth30 nmResistance (RSDE)
Channel dopingVT = 0.25 VLeakage (Ioff)
SDE under-diffusion15 nmResistance (Rinv)
Channel length0.06 µmLeakage (Ioff)
Gate length0.10 µmLeakage (Ioff)

Read the table as a set of opposing constraints rather than a list, and read the channel-doping row as a floor rather than a ceiling. Subthreshold current rises exponentially as the threshold falls, and the rate of that rise can be no steeper than about 60 mV per decade at room temperature, so a threshold below 0.25 V multiplies the off-state leakage faster than the lower threshold buys drive current. The doping cannot be reduced past the value that produces that threshold. Neither can it be raised without limit, for the separate reason given in §5: heavier doping scatters the carriers in the inversion layer and costs mobility. Two different physical effects close on the same quantity from opposite sides.

The rest of the table has the same shape. The oxide cannot thin further because the gate leaks, and cannot thicken because gate control of the channel is already marginal. The extension junction cannot be made shallower because a shallower region is more resistive, and cannot be made deeper because the drain field then reaches too far along the channel. Every direction is blocked by something.

The notes list the responses on the same page: high-k materials for gate dielectrics, novel shallow ultra-low-resistivity junctions, advanced lithography, and silicon–germanium technology. The first three are the subject of the chapters that follow.

These are the limits of one structure, as assessed around 2002

Every entry in the table except the threshold-voltage floor was subsequently passed, and the table is best read as a statement of where the conventional planar bulk transistor with a pure SiO₂ gate dielectric ran out rather than where silicon ran out. Nitrided oxides and then high-k dielectrics with metal gates took the equivalent oxide thickness below 2 nm; strained silicon raised drive current without raising leakage; source–drain extension junctions went below 20 nm; and non-planar fin structures restored the electrostatic control a planar channel could no longer provide, which is why physical gate length continued below 0.10 µm instead of stopping there.

The threshold-voltage entry is the one that has held, and it held because it does not depend on the structure: the 60 mV per decade limit on subthreshold slope follows from the thermal distribution of carrier energies, so no change of geometry or material removes it.

The salicide process needs no mask to decide where silicide forms. What supplies that information instead, and what would go wrong if the sidewall spacers were absent when the metal was deposited?

The information is supplied by which surfaces are silicon and which are oxide. The metal reacts only with silicon, so silicide forms on the exposed polysilicon of the gate and on the exposed source and drain, and not on the spacers or the isolation oxide. Unreacted metal is then stripped.

Without spacers the gate sidewall would be exposed silicon continuous with the source and drain silicon at its base. Silicide would form across that path and short the gate to the source and drain. The spacer is therefore doing two separate jobs at two different stages: setting the extension length during implantation, and insulating the gate edge during silicidation.

Summary

  • Gate length fell from 10 µm in 1970 to the 45 nm generation, roughly a constant fraction per year. Below about 130 nm the generation name and the physical gate length are different numbers.
  • The gate stack went aluminium → n⁺ polysilicon (for thermal tolerance and work-function control) → polycide with WSix (resistance to one fourth) → salicide with TiSi₂ or CoSi₂ (lower resistivity, and silicide on the source and drain contacts with no extra mask).
  • Oxide thickness tracks gate length at LE/tox ≈ 45: 200 Å at 1 µm, 40 Å at 0.18 µm, 20 Å below 0.1 µm. At 20 Å the gate tunnelling current reaches the off-state leakage of about 10 nA/µm, which ends SiO₂ as a logic gate dielectric and forces high-k.
  • Channel doping went uniform → threshold-adjust boron implant (both device types) → super-steep retrograde with indium or antimony, keeping the inversion layer below 5×10¹⁷ cm⁻³ → halo and pocket implants placing the punch-through dose only at the drain-extension edge.
  • The drain went single-region → lightly doped drain at 10¹⁷ cm⁻³ beside a 10²⁰ cm⁻³ contact, to suppress hot carrier injection → source/drain extension at 5–10×10¹⁴ cm⁻² once the supply fell to 2 V and series resistance mattered more than hot carriers.
  • The sidewall spacer is the enabling structure for the two-region drain, for the pocket implant, and for the salicide. Its width is set by deposition and etch, not by lithography.
  • Substrates became heavily doped wafers with a lightly doped epitaxial layer; gates became dual-doped, n⁺ over the n-channel and p⁺ over the p-channel, to make both devices surface-channel. Boron penetration through the gate oxide is the cost.
  • LOCOS failed at 0.35 µm on bird's-beak encroachment and non-planar topography, and was replaced by shallow trench isolation.
  • Below 0.1 µm every remaining direction is blocked by either a growing leakage current or a growing series resistance.
