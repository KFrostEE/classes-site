Module 02

The Deep-Submicron MOSFET: A Structural Roadmap

Every part of the transistor that changed as the gate length fell from ten micrometres to one tenth of a micrometre, why each change was forced, and which later module treats it in full.

Prerequisites M01 Read time ~55 min Sources Class notes pp. 1–12 (Chapter 1) and p. 22; Lectures 1–4

The transistor described in M01 is the device of the first integrated-circuit generations: an aluminium gate over a thermally grown oxide, a uniformly doped body, and source and drain regions formed by diffusion. Almost none of that survives in a modern part. The gate is a silicide-capped polysilicon stack, the dielectric is a hafnium compound, the body carries a doping profile engineered in three dimensions, and the drain is built from two or three separately implanted regions. Each of those replacements was forced by a specific failure that appeared at a specific gate length, and each is the subject of a later chapter of this course.

This module is the map of those replacements. It states the scaling history that drove them, then walks the four structural axes along which the transistor was rebuilt — the gate stack, the gate dielectric, the channel doping profile, and the drain structure — followed by the substrate, the gate doping, and the isolation between devices. Two mechanisms are developed in full here because everything after them depends on them: punch-through, which sets how short a channel can be for a given body doping, and the buried channel, which forced the gate of the p-channel device to change. The remaining topics are introduced to the depth needed to see why each exists and what it replaced; the module named alongside each carries the rest.

1 · The gate length history

The gate length is the distance, measured along the direction of current flow, over which the gate electrode controls the silicon surface beneath it. It sets the drain current through the factor Z/L of the drain-current expression, it sets the time a carrier needs to cross the channel, and it sets how many transistors fit on a die. Reducing it improves all three at once, which is why it has been reduced continuously since integrated MOSFETs were first manufactured.

The figures the notes give are 10 µm in 1970, 3–5 µm in 1980, and 2 µm in 1985, with gate lengths dropping below 1 µm in the late 1980s and reaching 0.5–0.35 µm by the late 1990s. The 2001–2002 generation spanned 0.25 µm to 0.13 µm, and the sequence continued through the 90 nm, 65 nm and 45 nm generations.

From the 0.25 µm generation on, the generation name is not the gate length

The number naming a technology generation and the physical gate length of the transistors built in it were the same quantity only down to the 0.35 µm generation. They began to separate at 0.25 µm, where the physical gate length was already about 200 nm, and by the 130 nm generation the gap was close to a factor of two — that generation shipped 70 nm transistors. The 90 nm generation shipped gate lengths near 50 nm, 65 nm shipped near 35 nm, and from the 22 nm generation the name became a marketing label with no dimension on the die corresponding to it. Physical gate length has stayed roughly in the 20–30 nm range since about 2011, while generation names continued to fall.

The history above is stated in the course's own terms, where the two are treated as one number. Any figure quoted for a generation from 0.25 µm on is a generation name, and the transistor built in that generation has a shorter gate than the name suggests.

Reducing the gate length alone does not work. The gate controls the channel by competing with the drain for control of the potential in the silicon between source and drain, and shortening the channel strengthens the drain's share of that competition. Preserving gate control requires the oxide to thin, the source and drain junctions to become shallower, and the body doping to rise — each of which introduces a new failure of its own. The rest of this module is the catalogue of those failures and their fixes.

2 · Two transistors, fifteen years apart

The clearest statement of what changed is a pair of cross-sections drawn in the notes, one of a device from the late 1980s and one from 2001–2002. The first is a 1.5 µm device: a rectangular gate on a thin oxide, with a source and a drain that are single uniformly doped regions reaching a junction depth xjc below the surface and spreading a short distance under the gate edges. Four dimensions describe it — the drawn gate length LL, the gate thickness tgate, the oxide thickness tox, and the junction depth.

The second is a 0.2 µm device. The gate has acquired an insulating sidewall spacer of thickness tsw — a wedge of deposited oxide standing against each vertical face of the gate, whose purpose is to hold a later implant a fixed distance away from the gate edge. The drain is now two regions: a shallow drain extension of depth xj that reaches slightly under the gate edge and meets the channel, and a deeper heavily doped contact region of depth xjc, beyond the spacer, that carries the metal contact. Around the inner tip of each extension, beneath the gate edge, sits a pocket implant: a locally heavier dose of the same dopant type as the body.

The notes organise the difference between these two figures into four axes — gate stack and contact structure, gate dielectric, channel doping profile, and drain structure. They then name two structural aspects of the integrated circuit that lie outside the transistor altogether: the on-chip interconnect system, the stacked layers of metal wiring and insulating dielectric that connect the transistors to one another, whose planarization is the subject of M13 and M14; and the substrate and isolation structures, the wafer the transistors are built in and the insulating regions that keep neighbouring transistors from conducting into one another, treated here in §7 and §9. One further change, the doping of the gate material itself, belongs with the gate stack but became a separate problem once CMOS needed two gate materials; it is §8.

3 · The gate stack

The gate electrode must do two things. It must hold a controlled potential over the channel, and it must carry that potential from wherever the signal originates to the transistor, which makes it a length of wire as well as a plate. The two requirements pull in different directions, and the history of the gate stack is the history of satisfying both. The notes group the gate with the contacts to the source and drain, because the same silicide technology came to serve both.

3.1 Aluminium to polysilicon

The original gate material was aluminium. It was replaced by polycrystalline silicon — polysilicon, silicon deposited as a film of small randomly oriented crystal grains rather than as a single crystal — which the notes date to the mid-1970s. Beside that statement the notes write the threshold-voltage equation, because the first term of it is the professor's reason for the change.

VT = φMS − QiCox − QdCox + 2φF
(2.1)
VT threshold voltage (V) · φMS gate-to-semiconductor work function difference (V) · Qi interface charge per unit area (C/cm²) · Qd depletion charge per unit area (C/cm²) · Cox oxide capacitance per unit area (F/cm²) · φF Fermi potential of the body (V). This is the professor's four-term form, with signs carried explicitly per device type. It is derived term by term in M03; it appears here because the first term is what decides the gate material.

The work function of a material is the energy needed to remove an electron from it to free space, and φMS is the difference between that quantity for the gate and for the silicon body. It enters the threshold voltage directly, volt for volt. Aluminium has one work function, so an aluminium gate fixes φMS for a given body doping and offers no adjustment. Polysilicon is silicon, and its work function moves with its doping, between the conduction-band edge for heavy n-type doping and the valence-band edge for heavy p-type doping (M03 §1.2). Doping the gate therefore converts φMS from a fixed quantity into a process variable, and that is the reason the gate of a modern transistor is doped as heavily as its source and drain.

Polysilicon also made the self-aligned gate possible, and the historical record gives this reason and the work-function reason together. Lecture 3 drew the argument. In the aluminium-gate process the source and drain are formed first, by diffusing dopant through windows in a masking oxide at high temperature. Diffusion proceeds in three dimensions, so the dopant spreads sideways under the edge of the mask as well as downward. The aluminium gate is patterned afterwards, by a separate lithography step, and it must overlap the source and drain on both sides to be certain of covering the whole channel despite the alignment tolerance between the two masks. Wherever the gate lies over the heavily doped source or drain, the gate oxide separates two conductors: that overlap is a gate-to-source and a gate-to-drain overlap capacitance, which must be charged and discharged on every switching transition and so costs speed.

The self-aligned gate reverses the order. The gate oxide and a polysilicon gate are patterned first, and the source and drain are then introduced by ion implantation with the polysilicon gate itself as the mask. Ions reach the silicon only where the gate is absent, so the source and drain edges fall at the gate edges by construction, with no alignment step between them. The residual overlap is the lateral spread of the implant and of the activation anneal that follows, far smaller than the sideways reach of a diffused junction. The same implant dopes the polysilicon gate and raises its conductivity, so the lecture's point is that one step accomplishes both. Aluminium could not be used in this sequence: it melts at 660 °C, below the anneal that activates the implanted dopant, so an aluminium gate has to be put down after the source and drain are finished.

Both answers to why polysilicon replaced aluminium are therefore correct, and they do not compete. The adjustable work function is the reason the course gives, and it is the lever M03 §8 pulls. The first commercial silicon-gate process, at Fairchild in 1968, was developed for both at once: its heavily doped p-type silicon gate took about 1.1 V off the magnitude of the p-channel threshold of the time, and the gate served as its own source–drain mask. Self-aligned gate structures had been proposed and demonstrated a few years earlier (M03 §8 gives the sequence). The date the notes give for the change is later than the historical record, which the table at the end of this module records.

3.2 Polysilicon to polycide

Polysilicon is a poor conductor by the standards of a metal even when doped to its solubility limit. NMOS and CMOS technologies down to 0.35 µm used n⁺ polysilicon — polysilicon doped heavily with phosphorus — for the gate. Below 1.25 µm gate length the resistance of polysilicon lines became too large for them to serve as local interconnect, despite the heavy doping, because a line that is shorter in one dimension is also narrower in the others and its resistance rises as the cross-section falls.

The response was to cap the polysilicon with a metal silicide, a compound of a metal with silicon that conducts far better than doped silicon and tolerates the same process temperatures. The silicide used was tungsten silicide, WSix. The resulting two-layer gate — silicide over polysilicon — is called a polycide, and it reduced the resistance of the heavily doped layer to one fourth of its previous value. The polysilicon is retained beneath because the electrical behaviour of the gate depends on what touches the oxide, and a silicide in that position would forfeit the work-function control that motivated polysilicon in the first place.

3.3 Polycide to salicide

In deep-submicron CMOS the tungsten silicide was replaced by titanium disilicide, TiSi₂, or cobalt disilicide, CoSi₂, both of which have lower resistivities than WSix. The notes state the second property that mattered more: both can be formed on the source and drain contact regions at the same time as they are formed on the polysilicon lines. A structure made that way is a self-aligned silicide, contracted to salicide. No lithography step defines where the silicide goes; the existing geometry does.

Lecture 3 listed the metals whose silicides serve this role: tungsten, titanium, molybdenum and tantalum — refractory metals, the class of metals with very high melting points — and nickel, to which the notes add cobalt; nickel and cobalt are grouped as near-noble metals in the silicide literature. The process as taught has three steps. The metal is first deposited over the whole wafer by sputtering, a vacuum deposition in which ions from a low-pressure argon discharge strike a metal target and eject atoms that condense on the wafer. The wafer is then annealed: the metal reacts with silicon wherever the two touch — on the exposed polysilicon of the gate and on the exposed source and drain — and reacts with oxide far more slowly, so the sidewall spacers and the isolation oxide stay bare of silicide. Finally a selective wet etch removes the unreacted metal and leaves the silicide.

Lecture 3 described one anneal. Production titanium and cobalt salicide processes use two, with the strip between them. The first anneal, at roughly 600–700 °C for titanium, forms a high-resistivity intermediate phase; the unreacted metal is then removed by a selective wet etch that attacks the metal and not the silicide; and a second anneal at about 800 °C converts the intermediate phase into the low-resistivity one. Reaching the low-resistivity phase in a single high-temperature step is what the two-stage sequence exists to avoid, because silicon transported sideways through a metal film still lying over the spacer forms silicide bridges from the gate to the source and drain. The selectivity of the process is a matter of reaction rate rather than an absolute prohibition — good enough to build on, and the sidewall spacer is what makes it good enough.

The notes give two benefits of the salicide structure:

  • the polysilicon lines have lower resistivity because of the silicide on top of them, which is the polycide benefit carried over; and
  • the source and drain contact regions exhibit smaller contact resistance and smaller sheet resistivity than a contact without a silicide.

Lecture 3 gave the reason for the second benefit: the silicide–silicon interface is clean. Silicon exposed to air oxidises even at room temperature. A native oxide covers the surface to 0.5–1 nm almost at once and stops growing after a few hours at 1–2 nm, so a metal deposited directly on silicon, even after a hydrofluoric acid clean, lands on a film of oxide. A silicide forms instead by reaction with the silicon beneath the metal: 1 nm of titanium consumes 2.27 nm of silicon to form 2.51 nm of TiSi₂, and titanium reduces a thin native oxide in the process. The silicide–silicon interface therefore forms below the original surface, in silicon that was never exposed, and the contact made to it is correspondingly lower in resistance.

That benefit addresses a problem that grows as the transistor shrinks. The resistance in series with the channel — through the contact, through the extension, and through the region beneath the spacer — does not fall as the channel resistance falls, so it becomes a larger fraction of the total. Silicide on the source and drain removes the largest component of it. M10 treats the full series-resistance budget.

4 · The gate dielectric

The gate dielectric is the insulating layer separating the gate from the channel. Its thickness sets Cox, the oxide capacitance per unit area, and therefore how much inversion charge a given gate voltage induces. Silicon dioxide served in this role from the beginning, for reasons treated in M05a: it grows directly from the silicon it insulates, and the interface it forms carries remarkably few electrically active defects.

The notes give two reasons for thinning it continuously: to improve the drain current, and to control short-channel effects. The first follows from the charge: the inversion charge per unit area is Cox(VG − VT), so a thinner oxide delivers more carriers, and more current, at the same gate voltage. The second follows from the competition described in §1: a thinner oxide couples the gate more strongly to the silicon surface, so the gate keeps control of the channel potential against a drain that has moved closer. If the oxide stayed the same thickness while the gate length fell, the drain would take control and the transistor would conduct between source and drain regardless of the gate voltage. The notes state the relation the industry maintained as a single ratio.

LEtox ≈ 45
(2.2)
LE gate length (any length unit) · tox gate oxide thickness (the same unit). A dimensionless design rule, not a physical law: it records the ratio at which gate control over the channel stayed adequate. Both quantities must be expressed in the same unit before dividing.

The rule fixes the oxide thickness once the gate length is chosen. The notes give three points on it: a 1 µm gate needs tox ≈ 200 Å, a 0.18 µm gate needs 40 Å, and a gate shorter than 0.1 µm needs about 20 Å.

Twenty ångströms is 2 nm, and the notes (p. 22) describe a 2 nm gate oxide as only 10–12 atomic layers thick. Thermal oxide is amorphous and has no atomic planes, so a layer count depends on the length taken as one layer. The notes' figure corresponds to one Si–O bond length per layer: the notes give that bond as 1.62 Å on the same page, and 20 Å/1.62 Å ≈ 12. Counting from the oxide's density instead gives 8 to 11, depending on how the three atoms of each SiO₂ unit are counted; M05a §3 works through both counts.

At that thickness electrons pass through the oxide by tunnelling — the quantum mechanical process in which a particle crosses a barrier it does not have the energy to surmount, at a rate that falls exponentially with barrier thickness. The notes state the consequence precisely: at about 20 Å the gate-to-channel leakage current becomes comparable to the off-state source-to-drain leakage current, roughly 10 nA/µm of channel width. A gate that leaks as much as the channel it is supposed to be switching off has stopped being an insulated gate, and the notes conclude that building high-performance logic gates with SiO₂ is then no longer feasible. M06 treats the tunnelling mechanisms and the thickness floor they set.

The thickness that matters is electrical, not physical

What the transistor responds to is Cox = εrε0/tox, and εr is a property of the material. A dielectric with a relative permittivity higher than the 3.9 of SiO₂ therefore delivers the same capacitance at a greater physical thickness — and tunnelling depends on the physical thickness, not the capacitance. This is the entire argument for high-k dielectrics: a thicker layer that gives the same inversion-layer characteristics as a thin one, and so leaks far less. The notes make exactly this point at the end of their §1.2. M07 states the argument quantitatively and surveys the candidate materials.

The same limit constrains storage as well as logic. DRAM cell capacitors and the oxides used in non-volatile memory face stringent thickness limitations for the same reason — in the memory case the requirement is more severe still, because a stored charge must survive for years rather than for one clock cycle.

5 · The channel doping profile

The channel is the region of the body directly beneath the gate oxide, and its doping concentration sets three things at once: the threshold voltage, through the depletion-charge term of equation (2.1); how short the channel can be before the source and drain lose their separation, which is punch-through (§5.2); and the carrier mobility in the inversion layer, because heavier doping raises the electric field normal to the surface that the channel carriers experience (§5.2). Raising the doping raises the threshold, suppresses punch-through and lowers the mobility. The history of channel engineering is the history of getting the first two without paying the third.

5.1 Uniform doping and the threshold implant

In early MOSFETs the channel doping was flat with depth below the Si/SiO₂ interface. In NMOS the flatness came directly from the starting wafer, which was uniformly doped throughout. In CMOS a well — a region of the opposite doping type to the substrate, in which transistors of one polarity are built — was introduced near the surface by a shallow implant followed by a drive-in anneal, a high-temperature step that lets the implanted dopant diffuse until its profile is approximately constant near the surface. Either route gives a body of one doping concentration from the surface down past the depletion region.

A uniform body fixes the threshold voltage at whatever value the substrate doping happens to give, which is rarely the value the circuit needs. The fix is a threshold-adjust implant: a low-dose ion implantation into the channel region that changes the doping in the first few tens of nanometres of silicon without disturbing the bulk. The notes record that boron is the species used for both NMOS and PMOS devices, and they draw the result as a boron peak near 10¹⁷ cm⁻³ just below the surface, over a background near 10¹⁶ cm⁻³. Boron is an acceptor, so it makes the near-surface silicon of an n-channel device more strongly p-type, raising the n-channel threshold; in a p-channel device built in an n-type well, the same boron partially compensates the donor doping, which also moves the p-channel threshold in the direction wanted. One species and one implant step serves both. M03 §9 works through the arithmetic.

As the NMOSFET gate length approached 1 µm, the notes record a second implant: a blanket anti-punch-through boron implant, carried out after the threshold-adjust implant. Blanket means applied over the whole wafer with no mask, so the added doping is the same everywhere along the channel. Its peak sits deeper than the threshold implant's, below the surface layer where the channel carriers travel, at the depth where the source and drain depletion regions approach each other. Section 5.2 explains why that depth needs extra doping.

5.2 Punch-through

Each source and each drain forms a pn junction with the body. A pn junction is the boundary between an n-type and a p-type region, and around it lies a depletion region: a layer, stripped of mobile carriers, in which the ionised dopant atoms are left uncompensated and support the junction's electric field. The potential across it at zero applied bias is the built-in potential, Vbi; reverse bias — the n side made positive with respect to the p side, as the drain is with respect to the body of a conducting n-channel device — adds to that potential and widens the region.

Punch-through is the condition in which the depletion regions of the source and drain junctions merge beneath the gate. The body between them then holds no neutral region, the drain's potential reaches across to lower the barrier that holds the source's electrons out of the channel, and electrons injected from the source are swept through the depleted body to the drain. In a uniformly doped body the barrier is lowered first at the surface. Where the doping below the junction depth is lighter than at the surface, as beneath a diffused well, the regions also merge along a path below the surface, where the gate has least control, and that deeper path is what the blanket anti-punch-through implant of §5.1 closes. Either way the current grows steeply with drain voltage and the transistor can no longer be turned off. The professor's criterion for avoiding punch-through is a single inequality.

L > 2W
(2.3)
L channel length between the source and drain junctions (µm) · W depletion width of one source or drain junction into the body (µm, the same unit as L). The professor's condition (Lecture 3): the two depletion regions must not touch. Sze & Ng state it as the sum of the source and drain depletion widths approaching L; the drain junction carries the drain voltage and is the wider of the two, so taking W at the drain bias for both makes 2W an overestimate of that sum and the criterion errs on the safe side. A first-order rule: it ignores the two-dimensional field beneath the gate.

The source and drain are doped far more heavily than the body — 10²⁰ cm⁻³ in the notes, against 10¹⁵–10¹⁷ cm⁻³ — so each junction is one-sided. The depletion region must hold equal and opposite charge on its two sides, NAWp = NDWn, so the width on each side is inversely proportional to the doping on that side, and nearly all of the depletion region lies in the lightly doped body. Its width then depends on the body doping alone.

More body doping, less punch-through

The depletion width varies as NA−1/2. Heavier body doping gives each junction a narrower depletion region, the two regions separate, and a shorter channel works. Lighter body doping does the opposite. That is why the notes add boron beneath the channel as gate lengths approach 1 µm, and why every structure in the rest of this section is a way of putting more dopant where the depletion regions would meet.

Raising the body doping has a price, and Lecture 3 stated it immediately: the mobility falls. A carrier moving through silicon is scattered by the ionised dopant atoms, so more dopant means more scattering. Sze & Ng give the bulk electron mobility of silicon at room temperature as about 1400 cm²/V·s at 10¹⁵ cm⁻³, 1200 at 10¹⁶, 800 at 10¹⁷ and 300 at 10¹⁸ cm⁻³; Lecture 3 sketched a fall from about 1300 to about 200 cm²/V·s over the wider range 10¹⁵–10¹⁹ cm⁻³. The electrons of an inversion layer have a lower mobility still, roughly half the bulk figure. It is set largely by the field normal to the surface, which presses the carriers against the interface, where lattice vibrations and the roughness of the Si/SiO₂ interface scatter them; a heavily doped body raises that field through its larger depletion charge. Scattering from the ionised dopants adds to this at low field. The drain current is proportional to the mobility, so the current, and the transconductance — the change of drain current per unit change of gate voltage — fall with it. Heavier doping also raises the peak field at the drain junction, a separate problem taken up in §6.1.

The trade is between two places. The extra doping is needed where the depletion regions approach each other, below the surface; it is harmful where the carriers travel, at the surface. The blanket implant of §5.1 put boron under the whole channel at depth. The super-steep retrograde profile separates the two places vertically, and the halo and pocket implants separate them along the channel.

5.3 Super-steep retrograde profiles

A profile whose doping increases with depth rather than decreasing is called retrograde, and the version steep enough to serve at deep-submicron dimensions is super-steep retrograde, abbreviated SSR: light doping where the carriers travel, a steep rise to heavy doping where the depletion regions need to be held apart. The notes state what it achieves: the correct value of VT without degradation of channel carrier mobility, by keeping the doping in the inversion layer below 5×10¹⁷ cm⁻³. The need follows from punch-through: below about 0.13 µm the mean channel doping needed to control punch-through exceeds 5×10¹⁷ cm⁻³, so the only way to keep the surface below that value is to put the excess underneath it.

Holding a steep profile requires a dopant that does not diffuse away during the anneals that follow. The notes specify heavier dopant species than the usual ones: indium replaces boron for n-channel devices, and arsenic or antimony replaces phosphorus for p-channel devices. These species have smaller diffusivities in silicon, so the implanted profile does not spread during subsequent anneal cycles. Wolf adds a second reason: a heavier ion also has a smaller straggle — the spread of stopping depths about the mean — so its profile is steeper as implanted. Indium and arsenic implanted at 100–200 keV give about 5×10¹⁶ cm⁻³ at the oxide interface and a peak near 10¹⁸ cm⁻³ some 80 nm below it.

Implant energy, not ion mass, sets how deep the peak lies

Lecture 3 explained indium's use by its mass, as an ion that penetrates deeper at the same energy. At equal energy the reverse holds: a heavier ion loses its energy over a shorter path, stops at a shallower depth, and has a narrower distribution about that depth (Plummer, Deal & Griffin; Wolf states it for arsenic). The indium peak is placed at depth by choosing a higher implant energy, which is why Wolf's indium and arsenic profiles use 100–200 keV. The written sources give two reasons for the heavier species: smaller diffusivity, which is the notes' reason, and smaller straggle, which Wolf adds. The next box records how far the first one holds.

The notes' diffusivity reason holds for an undamaged lattice

The notes state that indium, arsenic and antimony diffuse more slowly than boron and phosphorus, so the implanted profile survives the anneals. Mass is not the mechanism. A dopant atom moves through silicon by pairing with a point defect — a silicon self-interstitial, an extra silicon atom lodged between lattice sites, or a vacancy, an empty lattice site — and each species has its own activation energy for the move. Boron and phosphorus move mainly by the interstitial route, antimony mainly by vacancies, and arsenic by both in roughly equal measure. Indium moves by the interstitial route, as boron does; it is slower than boron because its activation energy is higher, not because it uses a different defect.

The route decides which process steps speed a dopant up. Implant damage floods the lattice with interstitials and accelerates every interstitial diffuser for a short time, a burst called transient enhanced diffusion (M09). Griffin et al. measured indium's enhancement from implant damage as comparable to boron's, and concluded that indium is preferred as a channel dopant for its narrow implanted distribution rather than for its diffusion. Wolf states the opposite, that indium is less susceptible to the enhancement than boron. The straggle advantage is the one that survives either way.

Indium also has two limits. It is a deep acceptor, with a level about 0.16 eV above the valence band edge, so only a fraction of it is ionised at room temperature and its electrically active concentration is below its chemical concentration — a fraction that itself varies with temperature, which appears as threshold-voltage temperature sensitivity. And its solid solubility in silicon is low: Wolf puts the ceiling on indium peak doping at about 10¹⁸ cm⁻³. An Intel patent on the halo implants of §5.4, which place extra dopant only at the two ends of the channel, reports that solubility alone makes indium inadequate as the n-channel halo dopant at target gate lengths of 60 nm or less, because the doping needed to hold off the leakage, about 3×10¹⁸ cm⁻³ in its example, exceeds indium's solubility of about 2×10¹⁸ cm⁻³; the patent adds a boron halo to make up the difference.

5.4 Halo and pocket implants

The profile can also be made non-uniform along the channel, from source to drain. The blanket punch-through implant of §5.1 gives the same doping everywhere along the channel. A blanket implant heavy enough to stop punch-through at the drain edge is heavier than the rest of the channel needs, and the notes state the consequence directly: in deep-submicron devices it causes performance degradation, because the threshold voltage rises everywhere and the mobility falls everywhere.

The alternative is to introduce the dopant only where punch-through actually begins, at the edge of the drain-extension region, leaving the rest of the channel with the lighter doping a low threshold voltage requires. The extra doped region surrounds the edge of the drain extension much as a halo surrounds a head in a medieval painting, and the notes name the structure accordingly: a halo anti-punch-through structure.

The halo implant is self-aligned to the gate, in the same way the source and drain are: the gate blocks the ions from the channel it covers, so the doped region places itself at the gate edge with no separate alignment step. The species is the same as the one already present in the well — boron, for an n-channel device, as Lecture 3 stated. Its energy is somewhat higher than the energy used for the lightly doped drain implant (§6.1), which means a greater range and a greater lateral straggle, so the halo region extends beyond the extension it surrounds. In its original form the halo beam arrived normal to the wafer surface.

The pocket's doping has an upper limit, which Lecture 4 set at the order of 10¹⁷ cm⁻³ and never toward degenerate doping near 10¹⁹ cm⁻³. The reason is the junction the pocket forms with the heavily doped n⁺ extension: the field there is highest at the curved corners, and as both sides of a junction approach degenerate doping its breakdown voltage falls and its leakage rises (Sze & Ng ch. 2). The limit is set by that junction, not by punch-through, which heavier doping suppresses (§5.2).

In more advanced technology the second implant is performed with the ion beam directed at a large angle to the wafer. The gate masks the channel from the ions at any angle; what the tilt adds is lateral reach. Ions enter the exposed silicon beside the gate on a slanting path and come to rest beneath the gate edge, so the peak concentration can be placed at a precise depth and a precise lateral distance from the gate edge. The structure is named a large-angle tilted implanted punch-through structure, LATIPS, and is also known as a pocket implant. Wolf gives the practice: tilt angles of 20–45° from the wafer normal; the implant done after the source–drain extension implant and before the spacer is formed, so the pocket is self-aligned to the gate; and the dose split into four equal segments with the wafer rotated 90° between them — a quad implant — because gates on a chip run in two perpendicular directions and every gate edge must receive a beam tilted toward it. The pocket dopant is meant to lie just outside the final extension profile, around its tip.

6 · The drain structure

For early MOSFETs with 10 µm channel lengths, the drain side of the pn junction at the end of the channel was a single region of one doping. Two things changed that, in sequence: first a reliability failure at 2 µm, then a resistance problem at 0.18 µm created by the fix for the first. The source is built the same way, because the two are implanted together and a transistor must work in either direction.

6.1 Hot carrier injection, and the lightly doped drain

As the channel length reduced to 2 µm, the electric field in the depletion region of the drain junction grew large enough to produce severe hot electron injection. The mechanism is as follows. The voltage between drain and source did not fall in proportion to the channel length, so the field along the channel rose. Electrons crossing the high-field region near the drain gain kinetic energy from the field between collisions faster than they lose it, reaching energies well above the thermal average — they are hot carriers, hot relative to the lattice. A carrier with enough energy can surmount the energy barrier between silicon and silicon dioxide and enter the gate oxide. On the way it breaks bonds at the interface, creating interface traps, and some of the injected carriers are trapped in the oxide. Both charges collect near the drain, where the injection happens, and both grow with operating time: they lower the transconductance and the drive current and shift the threshold voltage. The shift is not the uniform Qi/Cox of equation (2.1). Turn-on is decided near the source end of the channel, so charge confined to the drain end moves the threshold less than a uniform sheet would, and moves it more when source and drain are exchanged. M05b follows the chain at the atomic scale, from a broken Si–H bond to a lower drain current. The notes record the consequence as long-term reliability degradation: a device that met specification when manufactured drifts out of specification in service.

The peak field sits at the drain junction, and the heavier body doping that punch-through control demands (§5.2) raises it. For a one-sided junction the field rises linearly across the depletion region to a maximum Emax = 2(Vbi + VR)/W, so a narrower region carrying the same voltage has a higher peak: with the numbers of the example in §5.2, 4.2×10⁴ V/cm at 10¹⁵ cm⁻³ and 4.3×10⁵ V/cm at 10¹⁷ cm⁻³. The field is highest where the junction curves, at the edge of the drain beneath the gate.

Field is voltage gradient, so reducing the peak field means spreading the voltage drop over a longer distance. That is achieved by placing a lightly doped region between the channel and the heavily doped drain. A lightly doped region supports a wider depletion layer for the same voltage, so the same potential difference is distributed over more distance and the peak field falls. The structure is a two-region drain: the region connected to the channel, and extending under the edge of the gate, is the lightly doped drain, LDD, at about 10¹⁷ cm⁻³, and the second region away from the gate, connected to the first, is heavily doped at about 10²⁰ cm⁻³.

Producing the two regions in the correct places requires the sidewall spacer. Lecture 4 gave the sequence in five steps, followed by the anneal that the notes list as their fourth; the notes compress the same sequence into four steps, taking the patterned gate as the starting point and treating deposition and etch as one step.

  1. Pattern the gate. The polysilicon is deposited over the gate oxide and patterned into the gate by lithography and etching.
  2. Implant the lightly doped drain. Phosphorus is implanted using a medium-current implanter, the machine suited to moderate doses — the notes give 1–5×10¹³ cm⁻² for this implant in §6.2. The gate itself aligns the LDD to the gate edge.
  3. Deposit the spacer oxide. A layer of SiO₂ is deposited over the whole wafer by low-pressure chemical vapour deposition, LPCVD, in which gases react at a heated surface under reduced pressure. The film is conformal — of the same thickness on vertical and horizontal surfaces — so along the gate sidewalls it is as thick horizontally as it is vertically everywhere else.
  4. Etch the spacer. Reactive ion etching, RIE, removes the film with a fluorine-based plasma. RIE is anisotropic: it removes material vertically and hardly at all sideways, so it clears the film from every flat surface and leaves it standing against the vertical sidewalls of the gate. A wet etch in buffered hydrofluoric acid would be isotropic and would undercut, which is why the lecture insisted on the dry process (M01 §7 gives the undercut arithmetic).
  5. Implant the contact region. A high-current implanter, the machine suited to heavy doses, introduces the n⁺ dopant for the second, heavily doped region. The spacer now stands between the beam and the LDD and prevents these ions from entering it.
  6. Anneal. A single anneal activates the dopants implanted in both steps and drives the edge of the lightly doped region the proper distance beneath the edge of the gate.

The spacer is the entire mechanism. Its width is set largely by the thickness of the deposited film, and it fixes how far the heavy implant stays from the gate, and therefore the length of the lightly doped region, without any lithography step defining it.

Three details of the spoken sequence that the written sources do not support

The rounded spacer is not made by reflow. Lecture 4 attributed the curved outer face of the spacer to an anneal at 800–900 °C that makes the oxide reflow, and gave the purpose as smooth coverage by the sputtered metal of the silicide step. Undoped deposited oxide flows far less than doped glass because of its higher viscosity; 800–900 °C is the reflow range of borophosphosilicate glass, a boron- and phosphorus-doped oxide, and phosphosilicate glass needs 950–1100 °C (Plummer, Deal & Griffin). The rounded outer corner is the shape of the conformal film at the top corner of the gate, which the anisotropic etch carries down unchanged. The benefit the lecture named — metal that covers a rounded profile evenly — is real, whatever produces the profile.

The spacer etch uses no mask. The lecture described patterning the oxide with a mask. Plummer, Deal & Griffin form the spacer by a blanket deposition followed by an anisotropic etch-back with no mask, and that is what lets the film thickness, rather than lithography, set the spacer width. The notes and Wolf describe the same deposition-and-etch sequence without mentioning a mask either way. Wolf calls the etch anisotropic; the notes' handwriting at that word reads as "an isotropic", which can equally be a split "anisotropic", and only an anisotropic etch leaves a spacer.

The deposition chemistry. The lecture named silane and nitrous oxide as the reactants. Plummer, Deal & Griffin give silane with oxygen at about 400 °C, or dichlorosilane with nitrous oxide at about 900 °C, for this deposition; no textbook source confirms the temperature the lecture gave for the silane–nitrous oxide pair, so none is stated here.

6.2 Source–drain extension

For a 0.18 µm MOSFET the power supply voltage was reduced from 5 V to 2 V. Hot carrier injection depends on the field, and the field depends on the supply voltage, so at 2 V across a 0.18 µm channel the notes state that hot carrier injection no longer needs to be controlled by an LDD structure. What does need controlling is the resistance the LDD introduced: a lightly doped region is by definition resistive, and its contribution to the overall on-resistance of the MOSFET had risen to an unacceptably large degree.

The two-region drain is retained, but the near region is redefined. Its implant dose is raised from 1–5×10¹³ cm⁻² to 5–10×10¹⁴ cm⁻², roughly a factor of twenty, which produces a region of much lower resistivity. Because its peak doping is no longer lighter than that of the contact region, the name "lightly doped drain" no longer describes it, and the notes call it the source/drain extension, SDE. The peak doping concentration in the extension is as high as that of the contact region, but its junction depth is shallower. The second region is called the contact region.

The shallow junction is the point. A shallower junction is what limits how far the drain field reaches along the channel, which is the short-channel control the structure now exists to provide.

7 · Substrates for deep-submicron CMOS

The wafer itself changed. The notes specify two options: p⁺ substrates carrying a lightly doped p-type silicon epitaxial layer, or n⁺ substrates carrying a lightly doped n-type epitaxial layer. An epitaxial layer is single-crystal silicon grown on a single-crystal silicon surface, inheriting the orientation and lattice of the material beneath it, so that the boundary between the two is crystalline but the doping across it is a step.

The transistors are built entirely in the lightly doped epitaxial layer, which is where light doping is needed for mobility and threshold control. The heavily doped substrate beneath contributes a low-resistance path through the wafer. That path suppresses latch-up, a failure mode specific to CMOS in which the parasitic bipolar transistors formed by the n and p regions of adjacent devices turn each other on and conduct a large current from supply to ground until power is removed. Latch-up needs resistance in the substrate to develop the voltage that triggers it; a p⁺ substrate removes that resistance. M04 covers how wafers are produced, and M08 covers what the wells built into them do.

8 · Dual-doped polysilicon

Conventional CMOS down to 0.35 µm used n⁺ polysilicon as the gate material for both the n-channel and the p-channel devices. For the n-channel device this is the right choice: it produces a surface-channel MOSFET, in which the inversion layer forms directly at the Si/SiO₂ interface, where the gate controls it most strongly. For the p-channel device the notes state the consequence in one line: n⁺ polysilicon makes the PMOSFET a buried-channel device. The mechanism runs as follows.

The work functions of heavily doped n⁺ and p⁺ polysilicon sit near the conduction and valence band edges respectively, so they differ by about the silicon band gap, 1.1 eV. On a p-channel device, n⁺ polysilicon therefore gives a φMS about 1.1 V more negative than p⁺ polysilicon would, and because φMS enters equation (2.1) volt for volt, the p-channel threshold is about 1.1 V more negative with the n⁺ gate. On an n-well doped 10¹⁶ cm⁻³ under a 10 nm oxide, and ignoring oxide charge, the two gates give about −1.05 V and +0.07 V (φF = −0.347 V, |Qd|/Cox = 0.14 V, and φMS = −0.21 V for n⁺ and +0.91 V for p⁺, from an electron affinity of 4.05 eV and a band gap of 1.12 eV; M03 §7 works the method). The n⁺-gate value is too negative, and it is brought back by the threshold-adjust implant of §5.1, which is boron for both device types. The implant has to supply the difference between the n⁺-gate threshold and the target: for a target of −0.5 V, about 0.55 V. With Cox = 3.45×10⁻⁷ F/cm² that needs a boron dose of about (0.55 V × 3.45×10⁻⁷ F/cm²)/(1.6×10⁻¹⁹ C) ≈ 1.2×10¹² cm⁻². In the n-type well the boron is an acceptor placed into donor-doped silicon, and it counter-dopes it: confined to the top 0.1 µm, 1.2×10¹² cm⁻² is about 1.2×10¹⁷ cm⁻³, twelve times the well's donor concentration, so a thin layer just beneath the oxide becomes p-type. A lighter well or a threshold nearer zero needs less boron, but any dose that shifts the threshold by a few tenths of a volt still outnumbers the donors of a well doped 10¹⁶ cm⁻³ or less.

That p-type layer is the channel. Near threshold the gate field depletes the top of it, and the holes that remain lie in its undepleted lower part, below the Si/SiO₂ interface; the p-channel device conducts through a layer buried beneath the surface. A buried channel is further from the gate, and the gate reaches it only through a depletion layer, so the gate's control over it is weaker than over a surface channel. The p layer is also depleted from below, by the pn junction it forms with the n-well, and the device turns off only when the gate's depletion reaches down to meet that junction depletion; if the layer is too thick or too heavily doped, the gate's depletion reaches its maximum width first and the device cannot be turned off. At short channel lengths the drain competes for control of that buried layer more effectively than for a surface channel, and the notes state the result: buried-channel PMOSFETs exhibit off-state leakage currents that are unacceptably large once MOSFETs are scaled to 0.25 µm gate lengths.

Building a surface-channel PMOSFET therefore requires p⁺ polysilicon as its gate material. The p⁺ gate moves φMS, and with it the threshold, about 1.1 V positive, so the threshold no longer has to be pulled back with boron; whatever adjustment remains keeps the surface n-type, and the channel forms at the surface. The n-channel and p-channel devices now need gates of opposite doping: that is dual-doped polysilicon. The notes list what it costs. First, additional process steps: the two gate regions must be doped selectively, which requires extra masking.

Boron penetration through the gate oxide

Second, the p-type dopant introduced into the p⁺ polysilicon is boron, and boron diffuses rapidly. Unless a sufficiently low thermal budget is used after the poly doping step, boron traverses the gate oxide and enters the silicon substrate beneath. The notes state the damage precisely: such penetration causes unacceptable variation in the threshold voltage of the PMOSFETs, because the boron that arrives in the channel changes its doping and therefore its VT. A thin oxide makes this worse, since the distance to be crossed is shorter — which is why boron penetration is one of the constraints on oxide scaling in M05.

Third, boron also diffuses along a polysilicon line, and faster still through any silicide capping it, so boron in a p⁺ gate can travel sideways into n⁺ polysilicon elsewhere on the same line and counter-dope it. That changes the gate doping, and with it the work function and the threshold voltage, of devices that were masked from the boron implant entirely. Dual-gate processes respond by keeping the n⁺/p⁺ transition away from active gates.

9 · Isolation: LOCOS to shallow trench

Neighbouring transistors on a die must not conduct into one another. The isolating structure between them is field oxide: a thick oxide, typically about one micrometre, lying over the silicon everywhere no transistor is being built, so that any metal line crossing that region is too far from the silicon to invert it.

The technology that produced it was LOCOS, local oxidation of silicon. A thin thermal oxide — the pad oxide, typically 10 to 50 nm — is grown first to decouple the silicon from the stress of the film above it; a silicon nitride film is patterned over that pad oxide across the regions that will become transistors; and the wafer is then oxidised. Oxide grows thickly everywhere the nitride is absent, because nitride is nearly impermeable to oxygen, and hardly at all where it is present. The notes record that LOCOS served NMOS and PMOS technologies until the gate length reached the 0.35 µm generation, and that it failed there for two reasons.

The first is the bird's beak, and the pad oxide is what causes it. Oxygen arriving at the edge of the nitride diffuses sideways through that pad oxide and oxidises the silicon underneath the nitride edge. Oxidation also consumes silicon and produces an oxide occupying roughly 2.2 times the volume of the silicon it replaced, and that expansion lifts the nitride edge, giving the growing wedge its characteristic taper. The field oxide therefore encroaches into the active areas of the chip, and the width lost is set by the oxidation, not by the lithography — which is why the beak is shortened by thinning the pad oxide or thickening the nitride, and not by anything the mask designer can do. At large dimensions that loss is tolerable; at 0.35 µm it is a substantial fraction of the device.

The second is topography. Because the field oxide is grown thermally and is only semi-recessed — partly below and partly above the original silicon surface — the wafer surface after LOCOS is not planar. Non-planar topography is a direct difficulty for lithography, which must bring a whole field into focus at once, and for the metal layers deposited over it.

Shallow trench isolation, STI, solves both. A trench is etched into the silicon where isolation is wanted, and filled with deposited oxide rather than grown oxide. Nothing is consumed, so there is no bird's beak and no encroachment; and the fill is polished back flat, so the surface is planar. M15 gives the full flow, and the polishing step it depends on is the subject of M13.

10 · Silicon on insulator

In bulk silicon technology the pn junctions separating one device from another are reverse-biased, and a reverse-biased junction conducts only a small leakage current. That is not the whole story. The notes state that current paths among devices may still be activated in the bulk region by such phenomena as latch-up or transient radiation effects — a burst of ionising radiation generates carriers throughout the silicon volume at once, and those carriers provide the conduction the reverse bias was supposed to prevent. Furthermore, the parasitic circuit elements associated with the CMOS structure, such as source-to-substrate and drain-to-substrate capacitances, become too large in structures using bulk silicon; those capacitances must be charged and discharged on every transition, so they cost speed and power.

Silicon on insulator removes the shared bulk. The transistors are built in a thin single-crystal silicon film sitting on a buried oxide layer, so no conduction path exists beneath them and the junction capacitances terminate on an insulator rather than on a substrate. The notes give SOI a short paragraph of overview; M17 holds the treatment.

11 · The scaling limits of the conventional structure

Chapter 1 of the notes closes with a table. Below 0.1 µm, the notes state, MOSFET devices need to adopt different structural changes — meaning that the modifications catalogued above run out, and each of six features reaches a value beyond which it cannot usefully go. The reason column is the important one: every limit is either a leakage current that grows or a resistance that grows.

FeatureLimitReason
Oxide thickness2 nmLeakage (Igate)
Junction depth30 nmResistance (RSDE)
Channel dopingVT = 0.25 VLeakage (Ioff)

Gate length0.10 µmLeakage (Ioff)

Read the table as a set of opposing constraints rather than a list, and read the channel-doping row as a floor rather than a ceiling. Subthreshold current rises exponentially as the threshold falls, and the rate of that rise can be no steeper than about 60 mV per decade at room temperature, so a threshold below 0.25 V multiplies the off-state leakage faster than the lower threshold buys drive current. The doping cannot be reduced past the value that produces that threshold. Neither can it be raised without limit, for the separate reason given in §5.2: heavier doping raises the field normal to the surface and lowers the inversion-layer mobility. Two different physical effects close on the same quantity from opposite sides.

The rest of the table has the same shape. The oxide cannot thin further because the gate leaks, and cannot thicken because gate control of the channel is already marginal. The extension junction cannot be made shallower because a shallower region is more resistive, and cannot be made deeper because the drain field then reaches too far along the channel. Every direction is blocked by something.

The notes list the responses on the same page: high-k materials for gate dielectrics, novel shallow ultra-low-resistivity junctions, advanced lithography, and silicon–germanium technology. The first three are the subject of the chapters that follow.

These are the limits of one structure, as assessed around 2002

Every entry in the table except the threshold-voltage floor was subsequently passed, and the table is best read as a statement of where the conventional planar bulk transistor with a pure SiO₂ gate dielectric ran out rather than where silicon ran out. Nitrided oxides and then high-k dielectrics with metal gates took the equivalent oxide thickness below 2 nm; strained silicon raised drive current without raising leakage; source–drain extension junctions went below 20 nm; and non-planar fin structures restored the electrostatic control a planar channel could no longer provide, which is why physical gate length continued below 0.10 µm instead of stopping there.

The threshold-voltage entry is the one that has held, and it held because it does not depend on the structure: the 60 mV per decade limit on subthreshold slope follows from the thermal distribution of carrier energies, so no change of geometry or material removes it.

A 1 µm n-channel gate is to run with 5 V on its drain, with source and drain doped 10²⁰ cm⁻³. What body doping keeps it clear of punch-through by the criterion L > 2W, and what does that doping cost?

The criterion needs W < 0.5 µm at the drain. Equation (2.4) gives 0.880 µm at 10¹⁶ cm⁻³, and W scales as NA−1/2, so the doping must rise by (0.880/0.5)² ≈ 3.1, to about 3.1×10¹⁶ cm⁻³. Recomputing with Vbi = 0.962 V at that doping gives W = 0.501 µm, which confirms it: the body needs at least about 3×10¹⁶ cm⁻³.

The cost is mobility. At 3×10¹⁶ cm⁻³ the bulk electron mobility is already below its 10¹⁶ cm⁻³ value of about 1200 cm²/V·s and falling toward 800 at 10¹⁷ cm⁻³; in the inversion layer the larger depletion charge also raises the normal field, which lowers the channel mobility further, and the drain current and transconductance fall with it. The peak field at the drain junction also rises, as the square root of the doping. Putting that doping only below the surface (SSR) or only at the channel ends (halo, pocket) avoids paying the mobility cost across the whole channel.

The salicide process needs no mask to decide where silicide forms. What supplies that information instead, and what would go wrong if the sidewall spacers were absent when the metal was deposited?

The information is supplied by which surfaces are silicon and which are oxide. The metal reacts with silicon and hardly at all with oxide, so silicide forms on the exposed polysilicon of the gate and on the exposed source and drain, and not on the spacers or the isolation oxide. Unreacted metal is then stripped.

Without spacers the gate sidewall would be exposed silicon continuous with the source and drain silicon at its base. Silicide would form across that path and short the gate to the source and drain. The spacer is therefore doing two separate jobs at two different stages: setting the extension length during implantation, and insulating the gate edge during silicidation.

Summary

  • Gate length fell from 10 µm in 1970 through 2 µm in 1985 to 0.25–0.13 µm in 2001–2002 and on to the 45 nm generation, roughly a constant fraction per year. From the 0.25 µm generation on, the generation name and the physical gate length are different numbers.
  • The gate stack went aluminium → n⁺ polysilicon → polycide with WSix (resistance to one fourth, below 1.25 µm) → salicide with TiSi₂ or CoSi₂. Polysilicon made φMS adjustable through the gate doping, and it also made the self-aligned gate possible, which removed the overlap capacitance of a separately aligned metal gate. The first production silicon-gate process, in 1968, was built for both reasons together.
  • Salicide: sputter the metal over everything, anneal so it reacts only with silicon, strip the unreacted metal (with a second anneal in production). It lowers the resistance of the poly lines and gives lower contact resistance, because the silicide–silicon interface forms by reaction, below the native oxide.
  • The oxide is thinned to raise drain current and to control short-channel effects, at LE/tox ≈ 45: 200 Å at 1 µm, 40 Å at 0.18 µm, 20 Å below 0.1 µm. A 2 nm oxide is 10–12 atomic layers. At 20 Å gate tunnelling reaches the off-state leakage of about 10 nA/µm, which ends SiO₂ as a logic gate dielectric and forces high-k.
  • Punch-through is the merging of the source and drain depletion regions under the gate; it is avoided while L > 2W. W falls as the body doping rises (2.77, 0.88 and 0.28 µm at 5 V for 10¹⁵, 10¹⁶ and 10¹⁷ cm⁻³), so heavier doping means less punch-through. The price is mobility.
  • Channel doping went uniform → boron threshold implant for both device types → blanket anti-punch-through boron implant for NMOS near 1 µm → super-steep retrograde, with indium replacing boron for n-channel and arsenic or antimony replacing phosphorus for p-channel, keeping the inversion layer below 5×10¹⁷ cm⁻³ → halo implants at the extension edge → tilted pocket implants (LATIPS), 20–45° from normal in four rotated segments.
  • The drain went single-region → lightly doped drain at 10¹⁷ cm⁻³ beside a 10²⁰ cm⁻³ contact, against hot carrier injection at 2 µm (pattern the gate; implant phosphorus with a medium-current implanter; deposit conformal LPCVD oxide; etch it anisotropically into spacers; implant the n⁺ contact with a high-current implanter; anneal) → source/drain extension at 5–10×10¹⁴ cm⁻² once the supply fell to 2 V and series resistance mattered more than hot carriers.
  • The sidewall spacer is the enabling structure for the two-region drain and for the salicide. Its width is set by deposition and etch, not by lithography.
  • Substrates became heavily doped wafers with a lightly doped epitaxial layer. n⁺ polysilicon on the p-channel device forces a boron counter-doping implant that buries its channel, and buried-channel PMOSFETs leak too much at 0.25 µm; p⁺ polysilicon makes both devices surface-channel. The costs are extra masking, boron penetration through the gate oxide, and boron counter-doping along poly lines.
  • LOCOS failed at 0.35 µm on bird's-beak encroachment and non-planar topography, and was replaced by shallow trench isolation.
  • Below 0.1 µm every remaining direction is blocked by either a growing leakage current or a growing series resistance.
