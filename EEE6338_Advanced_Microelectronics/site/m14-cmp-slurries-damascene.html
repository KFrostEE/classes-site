Module 14

CMP: Slurries, Damascene and Endpoint

The slurries that polish oxide and metal and why their chemistries differ; how a metal is polished; the tools, pads and pad conditioning; the tungsten-plug and copper damascene processes; dishing and erosion; how a polish is stopped at the right point; how the wafer is cleaned afterwards; and why the oxide between copper lines is being replaced by low-k dielectrics.

Prerequisites M13, M10 (sheet resistance), M11 (thin-film interference) Read time ~50 min Sources Class notes pp. 119–130 (Chapter 8); Lectures 22–24; Wolf Vol. 4 Chs. 8, 12, 14, 15, 16; Plummer Chs. 2, 11

M13 explained why a chip with several metal levels must be planarized and how chemical mechanical polishing (CMP) removes oxide: the slurry water hydrates the oxide surface, the abrasive particles plow the softened layer off, and a compliant pad presses harder on the high regions than on the low ones. That was the first of the two polishing jobs the notes distinguish (p. 119): planarizing a homogeneous film. This module takes up the second job, removing a deposited metal from the surface until it remains only in trenches and holes, which is how tungsten plugs and copper lines are made. Polishing a metal needs a different chemistry, so the module begins with the slurries (§1) and the mechanism by which a metal is polished (§2), then the tools and pads that carry the slurry (§3). The damascene processes follow (§4), with the dishing and erosion they suffer (§5), the methods that decide when to stop polishing (§6) and the cleaning that follows every polish (§7). The last section (§8) takes the thread Lecture 22 drew from CMP to interconnect delay: the capacitance of the oxide between lines, and the low-k dielectrics that replace it.

1 · Slurries

A slurry carries both halves of CMP: the abrasive that removes material mechanically and the chemistry that makes the surface removable. Its composition is chosen for the film being polished, and the difference between an oxide slurry and a metal slurry is the difference between the two removal mechanisms (M13 §6.3 and §2 below).

1.1 What a slurry is and what it must do

A CMP slurry consists of small abrasive particles, generally inorganic oxides 10–100 nm across, suspended in an aqueous solution (notes p. 124). The particles have roughly the same hardness as the film being polished. Reagents are added according to the material: acids, bases, surfactants, buffers, corrosion inhibitors and complexing agents. The polishing rate depends on the chemical composition and pH of the solution, on the concentration of solids in weight per cent, and on the shape, size and size distribution of the particles (Wolf §8.6.3).

The notes state four requirements (pp. 122, 124): a high removal rate, good planarity, local film uniformity and high selectivity. The first three are the metrics of M13 §5. Selectivity is new. It compares how fast the slurry removes the film being polished with how fast it removes another film that the polish exposes, usually the one beneath:

A slurry with high selectivity of the polished film to the film beneath lets the polish stop on the lower film.

The particles must also stay apart. Particles that stick together form agglomerates, and agglomerates produce microscratches in the polished film (Wolf §8.6.3.2). What keeps them apart is surface charge. A particle immersed in a liquid becomes charged by adsorbing ions from the solution, and an equal and opposite layer of ions gathers in the liquid next to it: an electric double layer. The potential associated with the adsorbed charge is the zeta potential (ZP). Because the adsorbed ions come from the solution, the zeta potential depends on its pH. Silica particles have a ZP of about −30 mV at pH 7, and it becomes strongly negative as the pH rises above 7; alumina particles have a positive ZP in acidic solution (Wolf §8.7.1.1). Particles whose zeta potentials have the same sign repel one another, but the repulsion must be strong enough to overcome the van der Waals attraction that would otherwise bind them. A silica slurry is therefore stabilized by raising its pH, for example with NH₄OH, which makes the zeta potential more strongly negative (Wolf §8.7.1.1).

1.2 Oxide slurries

The oxide slurry is the silica slurry of M13 §6.3: fumed silica suspended in an alkaline solution of potassium hydroxide (KOH) or ammonium hydroxide (NH₄OH) diluted in deionized water, with the pH held at 9–11 (notes p. 125). Wolf adds the detail. Fumed silica is made as a dry powder by oxidizing silicon tetrachloride (SiCl₄) in a flame, and must be dispersed and blended into the slurry. Colloidal silica is grown directly in solution by hydrolysis of sodium silicate, followed by ion exchange to remove the sodium; it is more stable, less prone to agglomeration and, with its more uniform particle size, less likely to scratch, and it has been replacing fumed silica in STI, ILD and polysilicon polishing. The solids content is 5–35 wt%. Holding the pH at 9–11 minimizes agglomeration (§1.1), and the alkaline chemical also raises the oxide removal rate. The rate increases with pH, with particle concentration and with particle size (Wolf §8.6.3.1). A buffering agent keeps the pH constant through the slurry volume and over time.

Two other abrasives appear in the notes (p. 125). Ceria (CeO₂) slurries have been introduced for STI and ILD polishing. Ceria removes oxide faster than silica and at a much lower pH, 6–7, and a ceria slurry polishes oxide up to 250 times faster than silicon nitride. That oxide-to-nitride selectivity is why ceria was adopted for STI, where the oxide filling the trenches is polished down to a nitride layer (M15). Ceria is difficult to make in small particles, and silica is sometimes blended in to keep the ceria particles suspended (Wolf §8.6.3.1). Zirconia (ZrO₂) slurries, harder than silica but softer than alumina, have been investigated for polishing low-k dielectrics (§8).

1.3 Metal slurries

A metal slurry has more constituents than an oxide slurry (notes p. 125; Wolf §8.6.3.2). It always contains:

  • an oxidizer, such as ferric nitrate (Fe(NO₃)₃) or hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂), which converts the metal surface into its oxide (§2);
  • a buffer agent, which holds the pH.

It may also contain:

  • a surfactant, which keeps the particles dispersed;
  • a complexing agent, a compound that binds dissolved metal ions and so helps carry the metal away from the surface;
  • a corrosion inhibitor, such as benzotriazole (BTA) in copper slurries, which protects the copper surface so that an acidic slurry can be used;
  • additives that change the selectivity to other films.

The abrasive is usually alumina (Al₂O₃). Alumina slurries give higher metal removal rates and higher selectivity to the oxide interlevel dielectric, more than 20:1 metal to oxide (Wolf). Their pH is low: 1–5 for tungsten slurries and 3–9 for copper slurries (notes p. 125). Alumina particles have traditionally shown poor colloidal stability and so form the agglomerates that scratch, and the oxidizer is often kept apart from the abrasive until use, for colloidal stability or shelf life (Wolf).

The two chemistries differ because the two removal mechanisms differ. Oxide is removed by hydration: water enters the strained oxide network and softens it, and the alkaline solution speeds the attack (M13 §6.3). A metal cannot be hydrated in that way. It is polished by converting its surface into an oxide that is softer than the metal and that the abrasive can remove, which needs an oxidizer and a pH at which that oxide forms and stays on the surface (§2). Wolf attributes the fairly high metal-to-oxide selectivity that can be reached, for example 120:1 for tungsten to SiO₂, to the larger part the slurry chemistry plays in metal CMP than in dielectric CMP (§8.5.4).

An automatic feed system delivers the slurry so that the pad is wetted uniformly (notes p. 124). The slurry dries quickly once a wafer leaves the pad, and dried slurry is very difficult to remove, so wafers are rinsed while still on the pad and moved at once into wet cleaning tanks (notes p. 124; Wolf §8.6.3). A metal polish removes two materials, the metal and the barrier beneath it, and one slurry cannot remove both without excessive dishing; production uses one slurry for each (Wolf §8.6.3.3), a point taken up in §5.

Lecture 24 named a specific copper slurry: hydrogen peroxide and glycine in water, with silica particles. The peroxide oxidizes the copper surface, the abrasive removes the oxide, and the fresh copper exposed is oxidized again, the cycle of §2. Glycine is an amino acid that binds copper ions in solution, a complexing agent in the sense of the list above; glycine–peroxide slurries for copper are documented in the literature, with alumina as the abrasive in the work cited (S. Aksu and F. M. Doyle, 201st Meeting of the Electrochemical Society, abstract 379; abstract read). For the tantalum barrier the lecture had the peroxide form an oxide, Ta₂O₅, that silica or alumina particles remove; Wolf regards tantalum, harder and more inert than copper, as removed mainly by abrasion (§5.2). The lecture's pH ranges, about 2–4 for tungsten and 3–6 for copper, lie inside the notes' 1–5 and 3–9. It gave two rules for every slurry: the particles should be similar in hardness to the film or harder, which agrees with the notes' "roughly the same hardness" (§1.1); and the chemicals should be of high purity, about 99.9 % in the lecture's figure. Its definition of a surfactant is the standard one, a substance that lowers the surface tension of the liquid in which it is dissolved.

Ceria is not harder than silica. Lecture 24 said, hesitantly, that ceria is harder than silicon dioxide. It is generally described as softer, which is one reason it scratches less (US Patent 6,645,265, background). Its higher removal rate on oxide is chemical: Cook traced it to the attraction between the ceria particle and the oxide surface (Wolf §8.5.2.1; M13 §6.3), not to hardness.

A ceria slurry clears a 400 nm oxide overburden from the nitride of an STI structure, and the polish runs on for a further 20 % of that clearing time to be sure every die is clear. How much nitride is lost during the overpolish? Why would a silica slurry at pH 10 be a poor choice for this step, and why is it held at pH 10 in the first place?

The overpolish lasts 20 % of the time needed to remove 400 nm of oxide, so at the oxide rate it would remove 0.20 × 400 = 80 nm of oxide. On the exposed nitride the rate is 250 times lower (eq. 14.1), so the nitride loss is 80/250 = 0.32 nm. A silica slurry polishes nitride far less selectively than ceria; the sources give ceria's 250:1 as the reason it was adopted for STI, so silica would thin the nitride stop layer much more during the same overpolish. Silica is held at pH 9–11 because at high pH its particles carry a strongly negative zeta potential and repel one another, which prevents the agglomerates that scratch, and because the alkaline solution raises the oxide removal rate.

2 · How a metal is polished

A metal film is polished by repeatedly converting its surface into a soft oxide and scraping the oxide off where the pad touches. The notes (p. 121) give the three-step model proposed by Kaufman, and Wolf (§8.5.4) gives the same model with the conditions that make it work. The same model explains why a metal slurry polishes the high regions of a metal film selectively and why a metal polish has a sharp end.

2.1 Kaufman's three steps

The notes write the reaction for tungsten as W, with an oxidizer such as Fe(NO₃)₃, giving WO₃ and H₂O. It is a schematic of the step, not a balanced equation. The three steps are these (notes p. 121; Wolf §8.5.4):

  1. Passivation. The oxidizing power of the solution forms a passivation layer, an oxide of the metal, on both the up-areas and the down-areas of the metal film.
  2. Abrasion of the up-areas. The freshly formed oxide is softer than the metal and is removed mechanically from the up-areas by the pad and abrasive. The down-areas are not touched, because the pad is only partly flexible (M13 §7.1).
  3. Regrowth. The corrosive slurry regrows the passive film on the up-areas, while the passivation film on the down-areas keeps the corrosive species away from the metal there.

The cycle repeats, so the up-areas are corroded and polished away while the down-areas stay protected, until the pad's flexibility lets it reach the down-areas. From that point the planarization depends on the hardness of the pad and the downforce on the wafer (notes p. 121).

2.2 The balance the slurry must strike

The model works only if the oxide has the right properties. It must be soft enough for the pad and abrasive to remove it, yet hard and impermeable enough to stop the slurry from etching the metal beneath; and the abrasive must be about as hard as the material it removes (Wolf §8.5.4). The slurry may also dissolve the metal oxide it forms, so oxidation and etching must be balanced. If etching dominates, the metal is etched isotropically, from the down-areas as fast as from the up-areas, and no planarization results.

Which oxide forms, and whether it stays on the surface or dissolves, depends on the pH of the solution and on its electrochemical potential, the tendency of the solution to remove electrons from the metal. A Pourbaix diagram maps the stable forms of a metal in water on these two axes. The diagram for tungsten shows that to form an undissolved, protective WO₃ layer the solution should be below about pH 4 and at a slightly positive potential, which is why most tungsten slurries run at pH 2–3 (Wolf §8.5.4), inside the 1–5 the notes give for alumina tungsten slurries. The condition is a design guide rather than a hard limit: tungsten slurries at IBM originally used potassium ferricyanide at a pH of about 5.0–6.5 (Wolf §8.11.2), outside that window; because it is highly toxic it has been replaced, most often by ferric nitrate, potassium iodate (KIO₃) or hydrogen peroxide (Wolf §8.11.2).

2.3 Selectivity and overpolish

Because the chemistry plays a larger part than in an oxide polish, a metal slurry can be made highly selective: for example 120:1 for tungsten against silicon dioxide (notes p. 122; Wolf §8.5.4). Selectivity matters because a metal polish, unlike an oxide polish, must go on past the moment the metal clears. An oxide polish removes a set thickness and stops with oxide still everywhere; a metal polish must remove every trace of metal from the field, the flat oxide surface between features, or neighbouring lines remain shorted. Some overpolish is therefore required, and the field oxide is exposed during it (notes p. 122). The challenge is to keep the overpolish long enough to clear the metal everywhere and short enough to limit the oxide lost (Wolf §8.5.4).

A metal polish is also easier to stop than an oxide polish. When the metal over the field clears, the oxide is exposed, and the reflectivity, the temperature and the friction at the wafer surface all change, so the end of a metal polish gives a clear signal (notes p. 122). A copper slurry removes Cu at 350 nm/min with a selectivity of 150:1 to the oxide beneath. How much oxide does a 45 s overpolish remove? A process engineer doubles the oxidizer concentration to raise the copper removal rate. What can go wrong, in terms of Kaufman's model?

RRox = 350/150 = 2.33 nm/min; 45 s = 0.75 min, so the oxide loss is 2.33 × 0.75 = 1.75 nm. What can go wrong is the balance between oxidation and etching on which Kaufman's model depends. More oxidizer does not by itself mean faster removal: whether the film formed protects the metal or dissolves depends on the pH and the complexing agent as well (the Pourbaix argument of §2.2). If the solution comes to dissolve the oxide, or the metal, faster than the passivating film re-forms, the down-areas are no longer protected, the copper is etched there as fast as on the up-areas, the etch is isotropic and the step is not planarized (Wolf §8.5.4). Wolf makes a related remark about the barrier step: an aggressive oxidizer chosen to attack the tantalum would risk polishing through the copper (§8.11.3).

3 · Tools, pads and pad conditioning

M13 §4 described the rotary polisher. A complete CMP process needs more than the polisher: the notes list seven kinds of tool and consumable (p. 123): the polishing tools, the slurries, the slurry distribution system, the pads, the endpoint detection systems (§6), the cleaning tools (§7) and the metrology tools. This section covers the polishers, the wafer carrier, the pads and the conditioning that keeps a pad working.

3.1 Polishers and carriers

Four configurations of polishing tool are in use (notes p. 123):

  • Rotary: the table (platen) and the carrier both rotate (M13 §4).
  • Orbital: the carrier rotates, and the table moves in an orbital path.
  • Linear: the carrier rotates, and the table, a continuous belt, moves in a straight line.
  • Fixed table: the table is stationary, and the carrier moves in an orbital path.

The notes name two design elements of improved wafer carriers (pp. 123–124). A retaining ring around the wafer keeps it from slipping out of the carrier pocket under high downforce and high speed. Wafer levelling is achieved by backing the wafer with a compressible film (a film-backed carrier), with a pneumatic bladder, or with an air bearing sealed by a lip. Each backing spreads the downforce over the back of the wafer. Uniform pressure matters because, by Preston's law (M13 §6.1), the removal rate at each point is proportional to the pressure there, so any local excess of pressure becomes a local excess of removal, a within-wafer non-uniformity (M13 §5.3).

3.2 Pads

Polishing pads are made of polyurethane in one of two forms (notes p. 126; Wolf §8.6.6):

  • Polyurethane-impregnated felts. The fibre structure of the felt makes them porous, so they carry a larger volume of water or slurry. They are soft and compressible. Rodel's Suba IV, a felt impregnated with polyurethane, is the standard example.
  • Closed-cell polyurethane, cast and sliced, with a filler to set the hardness. These pads are hard and have low compressibility. Rodel's IC-1000 is micro-porous, with pores about 30 % of its volume, and has been the most widely used pad for oxide CMP.

A hard pad bridges the down-areas and gives better planarity within a die; a soft pad follows the long-range shape of the wafer and gives more uniform removal across it. A hard IC-1000 pad mounted on a soft Suba IV pad, a stacked pad, is the usual compromise (Wolf §8.6.6; M13 §7.2). The pad surface is cut with concentric grooves or punched with perforations, 1 mm wide and 250 µm deep, that form channels to carry slurry between the pad and the wafer; the pores of the surface also carry slurry (Wolf).

3.3 Glazing and conditioning

The roughness and porosity of the pad surface determine three things (notes p. 126): how slurry reaches the wafer, how removed material is carried away from it, and how much of the pad touches the wafer. If the surface is not kept at its optimum roughness, the removal rate drifts. During polishing the pad surface deforms plastically, becomes smoother, and its pores fill with pad material. This is glazing. A glazed pad delivers less slurry to the wafer, so the polishing rate falls (notes p. 126). The fall is roughly logarithmic in time; Wolf gives an example in which a new pad's removal rate dropped from 210 nm/min to 75 nm/min after only 50 wafers (§8.6.7). Large waste particles can also become embedded in the pad and scratch the wafer.

Pad conditioning restores the surface. A disk embedded with diamond grit is swept back and forth across the rotating pad, and the micro-scratches it cuts reopen the pores (notes p. 126). Wolf gives the detail (§8.6.7). Hard cast pads such as the IC-1000 are conditioned with 80–120 grit diamonds set in a nickel plate; a nylon brush is enough for soft pads. Diamonds can fracture and break away; an 80-grit diamond is 100–200 µm across, so a loose one embedded in the pad scratches the wafer severely. Chemically bonding the diamonds, collecting loose ones in the pad grooves and washing the pad with a high-pressure spray between wafers reduce the problem. Many polishers condition the pad continuously during polishing, so that every wafer meets a pad in the same state, which makes the removal rate more uniform from wafer to wafer (WTWNU, M13 §5.3). A new pad also needs a break-in cycle on dummy wafers before its removal rate settles. The lectures gave two different conditioning frequencies (Lecture 21: every one or two wafers; Lecture 22: every three or four); the notes give none.

Slurry-free pads, also called fixed-abrasive pads, carry the abrasive in the pad itself, as a precisely micro-replicated abrasive surface on a two-component sub-pad, and are used with a particle-free solution (notes p. 127; Wolf §8.6.8): a simple basic solution for dielectrics, an oxidizing solution at pH 2–7 for metals. The notes list their benefits: a higher degree of planarization; a lower cost of ownership, since consumables cost less; no slurry handling and no slurry distribution system; and none of the problems of slurry stability and solution variation.

The pad is glazing: its surface deforms plastically, smooths and fills its own pores, so less slurry is carried to the wafer and less removed material is carried away, and the rate falls (Wolf's example: 210 to 75 nm/min in 50 wafers). Conditioning with a diamond-grit disk reopens the pores by cutting micro-scratches; doing it continuously in situ keeps the pad in the same state for every wafer, and a break-in cycle on dummy wafers brings a new pad to a steady rate before product is polished. The soft Suba IV underneath lets the hard pad follow the long-range shape of the wafer, which improves uniformity across the wafer, while the hard IC-1000 surface still bridges the down-areas within a die and keeps the planarization.

4 · Damascene: tungsten plugs and copper lines

Aluminium lines are made by depositing a blanket film and etching away what is not wanted, a subtractive process (Wolf §12.1.1). Copper conducts better than aluminium but is difficult to etch by plasma (Plummer Ch. 2), so copper lines are made the other way round: the pattern is etched into the dielectric, metal is deposited to overfill it, and CMP removes the metal from the surface so that it remains only in the pattern. This is the second polishing job of the notes (p. 119; M13 §7.3). It is called the damascene process, after the ancient practice of inlaying metal in wood or ceramics that originated in Damascus (Wolf §12.1.4). Elsewhere Wolf words it as "metal inlays, as developed by the artisans of Damascus in the Middle Ages" (§15.2), and damascening, in the reference definition, is the inlaying of one metal into another, typically gold or silver into darkened steel; "wood or ceramics" is the odd version. Lecture 24 derived the name from a rain-water drainage system in Damascus, which no reference supports.

4.1 Tungsten plugs

The tungsten plug is itself a damascene step (Wolf §12.1.4). Contact and via holes in the oxide are lined with a TiN/Ti film, filled with tungsten deposited by CVD, and the tungsten and liner are polished off the field, leaving a plug in each hole (Wolf §8.11.2). The aluminium lines above are still patterned by etching, so this W-plug/Al structure combines a single damascene step for the plugs with a subtractive etch for the lines (Wolf §12.1.4). Tungsten CMP replaced an earlier tungsten etchback for the same job; it requires a planar surface before the vias are formed, and Wolf reports higher yields for it, attributed to fewer defects and better plug shape.

Lecture 24 drew the contact plug in more detail. The contact window is opened in the oxide over an n⁺ source or drain and lined with titanium or titanium nitride (the lecture's alternatives; the stack above is Wolf's TiN over Ti). Where titanium touches the silicon at the bottom of the window, an anneal forms titanium silicide, a low-resistance contact; on the oxide sidewalls it does not react (Plummer Ch. 11). It is the reaction of the self-aligned silicide of M10 §4, confined here to the contact opening. Tungsten is deposited by CVD from WF₆ and polished back with enough overpolish to leave no Ti or TiN on the field. The lecture added that the contact openings are round, not square, and that in a copper-interconnect chip the tungsten contacts remain while every interconnect level above them is copper: the W-plug/Al structure above is the earlier generation.

4.2 Copper single damascene, step by step

Lecture 23 drew the copper damascene sequence at the board, and the notes (p. 129) draw a single damascene process. The steps:

  1. Trench. A trench is etched in the oxide where the line is to run, about 1 µm deep in the lecture's example. Lecture 23 added that if the oxide is a doped glass, its sharp corners can first be rounded by reflow, so that the barrier sputtered into the trench is continuous over them. That is limited to the dielectric under the first metal: reflow needs 850–900 °C, and once metal is on the wafer later steps stay far below that (M13 §3.1; Wolf classes reflow as a pre-metal technique). A copper trench lies over metal, so its corner profile has to come from the etch.
  2. Barrier. A thin barrier film of Ta, TaN, Ti or TiN is deposited by sputtering over the whole surface, trench walls and floor included; Lecture 23 put it at 200–300 Å. Lecture 24 gave 100–200 Å (Lecture 23's own words had spanned 100–300 Å), and about 1000 Å (0.1 µm) for the copper seed, as Lecture 23 did. The barrier has two jobs. Copper does not adhere well to the oxide (Lecture 23). And copper is a fast-diffusing contaminant that causes device failure even in trace concentrations in silicon, so it must be surrounded by a diffusion barrier on every side (Wolf §12.1.5).
  3. Seed. A thin copper seed layer, about 0.1 µm, is sputtered over the barrier (Lecture 23; Wolf: by PVD). Electroplating needs a conducting surface to carry the plating current, and plated copper does not grow well directly on titanium nitride (Lecture 23).
  4. Plate. Copper is electroplated to 1–2 µm, enough to fill the trench and overfill the field (Lecture 23). Sputtering a micrometre of copper would take a long time and might not fill the trench.
  5. Polish. CMP removes the copper from the field. When the copper is gone and the harder barrier is exposed, the slurry is changed, because the copper slurry does not remove the barrier, and the barrier is polished off the field (Lecture 23; §5). Copper, with its barrier, remains only in the trench.
  6. Cap. The whole surface is covered with a non-conducting diffusion barrier such as silicon nitride, which closes the copper in from above (Wolf §12.1.5).

4.3 Dual damascene

In the single damascene process each via level and each line level is filled and polished separately. The dual damascene process forms the lines and the vias beneath them at the same time (notes p. 130). The notes draw it on a stack built over a copper line of the level below: a silicon nitride (Si₃N₄) etch-stop layer, an insulator, a second Si₃N₄ etch stop, a second insulator and a top Si₃N₄ layer. An etch-stop layer is a film that the insulator etch removes much more slowly than the insulator, so an etch through the insulator ends on it at a known depth. Two masking and etch steps open a narrow via through the lower insulator down to the copper below and a wider trench through the upper insulator (Wolf §12.1.5). A TiN barrier and a copper seed are sputtered into both openings at once, copper is plated to fill them both, and one CMP step removes the overburden (notes p. 130; Wolf §12.1.5).

Wolf (§12.1.5) lists five advantages of the copper dual damascene structure over W plugs under etched aluminium lines:

  1. all-copper wiring has lower resistivity and better resistance to electromigration than the W-plug/Al-alloy system;
  2. it can be combined with low-k dielectrics, which reduces the capacitance and so the RC delay (§8);
  3. only one CMP step is needed per level, the copper and barrier stack, against two (the W plugs and the ILD oxide);
  4. the copper never has to be etched;
  5. each via has a single interface, the barrier against the copper below, instead of two, at the top and bottom of a W plug.

The costs are equally specific. The barrier must reach sufficient thickness everywhere in high-aspect-ratio trenches and vias, which becomes harder as they shrink; copper and barrier CMP is complex (§5); electroplating brings a new and potentially contaminating process into the fab; and the low-k dielectrics are hard to integrate (§8).

The second removes the first's complexity: two metals and two metal depositions, two CMP steps and a metal etch become one metal, one polish per level and no metal etch; it also lowers the resistance of the line-and-plug path and puts a single interface at each via instead of two, which helps electromigration (Wolf §12.1.4–12.1.5). Not having to etch the metal is what makes copper usable at all, since copper is difficult to plasma-etch. It brings copper diffusion, so every surface of the copper needs a barrier and the top a nitride cap, and it brings copper CMP with its dishing and erosion (§5). The seed is sputtered because electroplating needs a continuous conducting layer to carry the plating current, and a 0.1 µm film is quick to sputter; the 1–2 µm fill is plated because sputtering that thickness is slow and may not fill the trench.

5 · Polishing tungsten and copper: dishing and erosion

A damascene polish removes two materials, the metal and the barrier or liner beneath it, which polish at very different rates, and it must go on past the point where the metal clears. Both facts leave the metal in the trenches below the oxide surface. This section gives the rates, the multi-step processes built around them, and the two kinds of recess that result: dishing and erosion.

5.1 Tungsten and its liner

Tungsten is removed at more than 350 nm/min, but its TiN/Ti liner polishes three to eight times more slowly (Wolf §8.11.2). The liner must still be cleared from the field, and a slow liner removal both erodes the oxide beneath and recesses the plugs. Clearing it takes an overpolish, during which the tungsten in the plugs keeps being removed and the plug surface sinks below the oxide. Production therefore uses a three-step process on three platens: tungsten on the first; the liner on the second; and on the third, an oxide buff with a silica slurry that removes a little of the exposed oxide, repairs the oxide damage and brings the oxide surface back level with the recessed plugs (Wolf §8.11.2). A seam or void left in the tungsten fill is opened by the polish, traps slurry, and can be enlarged by the slurry chemistry into a hole down the plug, a defect called plug coring.

5.2 Copper and its barrier

Copper is soft and oxidizes easily in standard chemistries such as hydrogen peroxide; tantalum, its usual barrier, is very hard and chemically much more inert, and is thought to be removed mainly by abrasion (Wolf §8.11.3). The rates show the mismatch: copper at about 350 nm/min with a selectivity of 150:1 to the oxide, tantalum at only about 30 nm/min. A damascene polish needs the opposite of that mismatch: a selectivity near 1:1 between the copper and the barrier, so that while the barrier clears the copper in the trenches comes down with the field and is not recessed below it, and a selectivity above 100:1 between the copper and the insulator, so that the polish effectively stops on the insulator.

No single slurry gives both, so copper CMP is done in steps (Wolf §8.6.3.3, §8.11.3). The first slurry removes the bulk copper and stops on the barrier; the second removes the barrier from the field; a third step buffs the oxide. All the copper on the field must be cleared in the first step, because the second step's copper rate is deliberately kept very small. Lecture 23 described the same practice: when the copper is gone the slurry is changed, because the copper chemistry does not remove the much harder barrier. A further hazard appears near the end of the polish, when barrier metal is exposed on the field and copper in the trenches: two dissimilar metals in electrical contact in an electrolyte form a galvanic cell, and the resulting anodic corrosion can remove the copper from a trench, sometimes entirely (Wolf §8.11.3).

5.3 Dishing and erosion

Dishing is the thinning of a film that is polished until it remains only in recessed regions (Wolf §8.12.1; M13 §5.3). During the overpolish the layer beneath is exposed, and ideally it would act as a perfect polish stop. But the pad is not perfectly rigid. A perfectly rigid pad would exert no pressure on a recessed region and would leave the surface flat; a real pad sags into a wide recess and presses on its middle, so the soft film there keeps being polished away. Dishing therefore grows with the width of the feature. Wolf's example is from STI: in some processes the oxide in a field region wider than 100 µm loses more than 50 nm. Copper, soft and polished fast, dishes in the same way between harder oxide walls.

Erosion is the thinning of the dielectric itself during a metal polish: the difference between the ILD thickness before and after the metal CMP step (Wolf §8.12.1). It occurs because the dielectric is removed at a small but non-zero rate during the overpolish. It depends strongly on the density of the metal pattern and not on the line width. Wolf's reason: where the pattern is dense, the copper bearing the pad's load at the start of the polish carries a higher pressure and polishes faster, so the copper clears there first, and the dielectric there then receives a longer overpolish than in sparse regions. 6 · Endpoint detection

A polish must stop at the right point, and the notes (p. 127) distinguish two kinds of process with two different meanings of "right". A dielectric is polished to a known final thickness: nothing changes at the surface when that thickness is reached. An inlaid metal, usually several layers, is polished until it clears and the polish lands on the surrounding dielectric: the surface material changes. There are two ways to control them: in-line measurement, between polishes, and real-time in-situ detection, during the polish. In 2002 only the second kind of process, the metal one, could be controlled in situ (Wolf §8.6.9).

6.1 In-line endpoint: measure, calculate, polish, measure

For a dielectric, the thickness T0 of the film over the features is measured before polishing, over an area dense in features (notes p. 127; Wolf §8.6.9). The polishing time is calculated from calibration data, which give the removal rate for the pressure, the rotation speed and the average state of the pad:

tf = T0 − TfRR
(14.5)
tf polishing time (min) · T0 measured thickness before polishing · Tf required final thickness (both nm, or µm × 1000) · RR removal rate from the calibration (nm/min), the rate of M13 eq. 13.3 rearranged.

The thickness actually reached at tf varies, by as much as 20 % (1σ) during process development (Wolf). The two errors are not equally costly. A film left too thick can go back to the polisher; a film polished too thin must be scrapped or have more dielectric deposited. A typical manufacturing practice is therefore to underpolish on purpose, measure, and polish again for an adjusted time, repeating until the film is within specification. The cycle is often run on a look-ahead or pilot wafer from each lot, and the rest of the lot is polished for the time the pilot wafer gave (Wolf §8.6.9).

6.2 In-situ endpoint detection

In-situ detection identifies the end of a polish without taking the wafer off the tool. The notes list three methods (p. 128):

  1. Front-side laser interferometry or spectroscopy. A laser and a detector are mounted in the polishing table, and a clear window in the pad lets the laser reach the front of the wafer; Wolf gives the angle as 45°. Each time the laser and detector pass under the wafer, the reflected light is sampled, and its intensity is recorded against time (notes p. 128). In a transparent film, light reflected from the top surface interferes with light reflected from the bottom, as in an anti-reflective coating (M11 §8.3), so the reflected intensity rises and falls as the film thins. In a copper polish the signal is a different shape. The reflectance reaches a maximum on the copper, drops sharply when the polish breaks through to the semi-transparent tantalum, and settles at a small constant value from the copper left in the trenches (Wolf §8.6.9). Endpoint accuracies within 20 nm were reported in development. A spectrometer reading many wavelengths can follow a thin metal film and see the copper endpoint coming, so as the endpoint approaches the polish can be switched from a fast copper rate to a slower one, which clears the copper from the tantalum without eroding the copper in the trenches.
  2. Optical sensing at several points on the back of the wafer. Silicon is substantially transparent to infrared light, so infrared shone onto the back of the wafer passes through it and senses the thickness of the film on the front by the same interference (notes p. 128; Wolf §8.6.9). Two optical fibres, one to an infrared emitter and one to a detector, are mounted in the wafer carrier, and up to four such sensors can sit in one carrier, each reading a spot about 0.5 mm across. Metal on the front blocks some of the light, but there is usually enough space between features for light to pass.
  3. Monitoring the current supplied to the carrier motor. A current shunt or a Hall-effect probe measures the current to the motor that rotates the wafer carrier (notes p. 129). The carrier is driven at constant speed, so the drive current changes to compensate for any change in the load, and the load is the friction at the wafer surface (Wolf §8.6.9). The largest change comes when one material has been polished away and a layer with different polishing properties is exposed: tungsten clearing from oxide, copper clearing from tantalum, or STI oxide clearing from its nitride stop. The method is production-proven and simple to fit, but useless for an oxide polish, in which no layer is broken through.

The notes (p. 122) add a fourth signal for metal polishing, the change in surface temperature when the metal clears, and summarize the aim: a clear endpoint signal followed by a short overpolish, which removes metal left in dished areas or in scratches (Wolf §8.5.4). Lecture 23 described the motor method as detecting a change in motor speed when the polish reaches the harder barrier. In the method as Wolf describes it, the carrier is driven at constant speed and the current is what changes; the notes make the motor current (and the torque, p. 122) the monitored quantity; the friction change the lecture had in mind is the cause in both readings.

Lecture 24 went through the same methods with one change of label. It introduced the signals read during the polish, those of notes p. 122, as "in-line" detection: a change in surface temperature when the harder barrier is reached, a change in the torque of the platen or of the wafer carrier, and a change heard in the captions as "surface velocity" where the notes have surface reflectivity, presumably a misreading or a captioning error. In the notes and in Wolf, "in-line" is the measure–polish–measure procedure between polishes (notes p. 127), and every signal read during the polish belongs to real-time in-situ detection (p. 128), as on this page. For the laser method the lecture used the notes' term, "real-time": the beam reaches the wafer through an opening in the pad, the lecture's "hole" and the notes' clear window, and the reflectivity changes once the copper clears. Asked whether the signal came from one spot, the lecture agreed that it does not: wafer and platen both rotate, so successive passes cross different parts of the wafer, and the signal is in effect an average.

7 · Cleaning after CMP

CMP is an inherently dirty process. After polishing, the wafer surface carries slurry particles, abraded pad material and debris removed from the wafer (notes p. 129). Residual slurry brings foreign particles, metal contaminants and chemicals, all harmful to the steps that follow. Left on the wafer, particles can obstruct electrical connections, create voids in the next deposited film, or corrode exposed metal; the cleaning must also remove organic residues and trace metal ions, and control corrosion where tungsten or copper is exposed (Wolf §8.7). Every post-CMP clean has to overcome the forces that hold a particle on the surface without damaging the surface.

7.1 Why particles stick, and the three ways to remove them

A particle that comes close enough to a surface is captured by the van der Waals force and physisorbed (Wolf §8.7.1). Static charge and capillary adhesion, the pull of a liquid film between particle and surface, also hold particles, but van der Waals is usually the strongest. It can be strong enough to deform the particle, enlarging the contact area and so the binding. Wolf names three ways to remove such particles:

  1. Electrical repulsion between the particle and the surface, set by their zeta potentials (§1.1).
  2. Chemically assisted removal: dissolving the particle, oxidizing it and then dissolving it, or lifting it off by etching the surface slightly beneath it. The SC-1 step of the RCA clean does the last: it oxidizes and etches the surface at once, undercutting the particle and reducing its contact area.
  3. Mechanical dislodgement: a shear force slides or rolls the particle off. Smaller particles are harder to dislodge this way. The mechanical methods are brush scrubbing and megasonic (high-frequency vibrational) cleaning.

Immersion alone, in DI water or a wet chemical, does not remove slurry particles adequately (Wolf §8.7.1), because of the adhesion forces and the slow-moving boundary layer of liquid at the surface. The cleaning must also begin at once. Wafers are kept wet from the polisher to the cleaner: rinsed on the pad, sprayed as they are lifted off. A wafer left in air for one or two minutes before the spray becomes difficult and sometimes impossible to clean, and a metal slurry left on the wafer keeps reacting and removing material after the polish has stopped (Wolf §8.7).

Electrical repulsion decides the chemistry. Silica particles and an oxide surface both have negative zeta potentials at neutral pH, and both become more negative as the pH rises. After an oxide polish, a cleaning solution above pH 7 gives the silica particles more negative charge, which increases their repulsion from the oxide surface and makes them easier to remove; the drag of the liquid still does the removing (Wolf §8.7.1.1). Alumina particles from a metal slurry are positive below about pH 8; in an acidic cleaning solution they would be attracted to the negative oxide, which covers more than half of a wafer after a tungsten or copper polish. For surfaces with exposed metal, for alumina particles, and to stop particles re-adhering, Wolf specifies a cleaning solution above pH 8. Adding NH₄OH to the brush-cleaning water, which raises its pH, reduced the defects after both oxide and tungsten CMP by as much as 75 %. Cleaning chemistries are very dilute, around 1:50 chemical to DI water.

7.2 Brush scrubbing

The most widely used post-CMP clean is brush scrubbing while the wafer is sprayed with, or immersed in, the cleaning solution (Wolf §8.7.2). The removal force is the hydrodynamic drag of the liquid that the brush drives across the surface. The brush is a cylinder of polyvinyl alcohol (PVA): a soft, sponge-like, chemically resistant and highly compressible material that is pressed against the wafer and does not scratch it, unlike the rigid nylon brushes of the 1970s. Current tools scrub both faces of the wafer at once. Particles as small as 0.12 µm have been removed this way. Once dislodged, the particles stay suspended and are flushed over the wafer edge; keeping the pH above 8 stops them re-adhering to the wafer or the brush.

After an oxide polish with a silica slurry, the solution is DI water with NH₄OH, often with H₂O₂ (Wolf §8.7.2.1). After a metal polish the clean has three further requirements: it must leave the metal surface free of oxide, leave it smooth, and not attack it (§8.7.2.2). The scrubbers must also tolerate acidic chemistries, such as citric acid and HF, used to remove metals and mobile ions, and the alumina from tungsten slurries adheres more readily and is harder to remove than silica. One limit remains: particles inside recesses. Wolf shows a slurry particle left in a 0.7 µm contact hole of aspect ratio 3:1 after both brush and megasonic cleaning.

Below about pH 8 the alumina particles carry a positive zeta potential, while the oxide that makes up most of the polished surface is negative; the double layers attract, and the particles are held on the oxide (sketch: + particle on a − surface). Raising the pH above 8, for example with dilute NH₄OH, turns the alumina zeta potential negative so that particle and surface repel, and a PVA brush scrub then removes them by the drag of the liquid; re-adhesion is suppressed at the same pH. The wafer must arrive wet because slurry that dries on the surface becomes very difficult or impossible to remove, and a metal slurry left on it keeps reacting with the tungsten (Wolf §8.7).

8 · Low-k dielectrics and interconnect delay

Lecture 22 led from CMP to this topic. The oxide left over and between metal lines after polishing must be thick enough to limit the crosstalk and the parasitic capacitance between metal levels (notes p. 117; M13 §5.4), and that capacitance, with the resistance of the lines, sets their RC time constant; that, the lecture said, is where low-k dielectrics are used. A low-k dielectric is an insulator with a dielectric constant k below that of thermal silicon dioxide, 3.9; a high-k dielectric (M07) has one above that of silicon nitride, about 7 (Wolf Ch. 14). The notes have no section on low-k; this section follows the lectures for what was taught and Wolf for the physics, labelled.

The equation separates the two levers. The factor ρk is the materials' contribution, and it is a product: a lower-resistivity metal and a lower-k dielectric reduce the delay in the same proportion. The factor l²/p² is the geometry: at fixed length and aspect ratio, halving the pitch quadruples the RC delay, which is why interconnect delay, negligible beside gate delay in older technologies, became a significant part of the total at feature sizes of order 0.5 µm (Wolf Ch. 12). A tall line (A > 1) is dominated by the capacitance to its neighbours, a flat one (A < 1) by the capacitance to the levels above and below.

RC of a 1 mm line, aluminium and oxide against copper and low-k

Given a line 1 mm long at a pitch of 0.5 µm (w = s = 0.25 µm), 0.5 µm thick, with d = t, so A = 2. Pure aluminium, ρ = 2.7 µΩ·cm (Wolf §12.1.1), in SiO₂ with k = 3.9, the thermal-oxide value the lecture uses, so that only ρ changes between Al/SiO₂ and Cu/SiO₂ (Wolf §12.1.1 gives 4.2 for the deposited oxide under aluminium, which would raise the Al/SiO₂ result by 7.7 %, to 40.2 ps); copper, 1.7 µΩ·cm, in SiO₂, in fluorine-doped oxide at the lecture's k = 3.3, and in the BN/BCN films the lecture quoted at about 2.2. Find the RC time constant of each, and the reduction against Al/SiO₂.

Plan. Compute R and C separately for one case from (14.6) and (14.7) to see the sizes, then use (14.8), in which only ρk changes between the cases. Work in SI: 1 µΩ·cm = 10⁻⁸ Ω·m.

Resistance, copper. R = ρl/(wt) = 1.7×10⁻⁸ × 10⁻³ / (0.25×10⁻⁶ × 0.5×10⁻⁶) = 1.7×10⁻¹¹ / 1.25×10⁻¹³ = 136 Ω. For aluminium, 216 Ω.

Capacitance, SiO₂. w/d = 0.25/0.5 = 0.5 and t/s = 0.5/0.25 = 2.0, so the bracket is 2.5, 80 % of it from the neighbours. C = 2 × 3.9 × 8.854×10⁻¹² × 10⁻³ × 2.5 = 6.91×10⁻¹⁴ × 2.5 = 1.73×10⁻¹³ F = 173 fF.

Cu/SiO₂. RC = 136 × 1.73×10⁻¹³ = 2.35×10⁻¹¹ s = 23.5 ps. Check against (14.8): 8 × 1.7×10⁻⁸ × 3.9 × 8.854×10⁻¹² × (10⁻³/0.5×10⁻⁶)² × (1/2² + 1) = 4.70×10⁻¹⁸ × 4.00×10⁶ × 1.25 = 2.35×10⁻¹¹ s.

The other cases by ρk. Al/SiO₂: 2.7 × 3.9 = 10.53 → 37.3 ps. Cu/SiO₂: 1.7 × 3.9 = 6.63 → 23.5 ps. Cu/FSG: 1.7 × 3.3 = 5.61 → 19.9 ps. Cu/BCN: 1.7 × 2.2 = 3.74 → 13.2 ps (all ρ in µΩ·cm).

37.3, 23.5, 19.9 and 13.2 ps: copper alone cuts the delay by 37 %, copper with FSG by 47 %, copper with a k = 2.2 film by 64.5 %. Check: each ratio equals the ratio of ρk, e.g. 3.74/10.53 = 0.355.

The model gives the line's own RC. Wolf's fuller expression for the delay of a wire (eq. 12.1) is tint = R0C + 0.4[(RC)1.6 + (tof)1.6]1/1.6 + 0.7RCin, with R0 the output resistance of the driving transistor, tof the time of flight of the signal along the line and Cin the input capacitance of the next gate. The line's own RC enters as 0.4 RC, combined with the time of flight. ρ and k enter every term through the line's R and C, and which term dominates depends on the gate size, the line length and the geometry. At fixed length the RC term grows as 1/p² as the pitch shrinks (eq. 14.8).

8.2 How the dielectric constant is lowered

Silicon dioxide has k from 3.9 to 4.5 depending on how it is formed (Wolf Ch. 14). Thermal SiO₂ is relatively dense, about 2.3 g/cm³; no film as dense as thermal oxide reaches a k much below 3.5, and CVD oxides, though less dense, contain more silanol (Si–OH) groups, absorb more moisture, and the water raises their k. Wolf names three routes to a lower k:

  1. Lower density. A more open molecular structure, or pores a few nanometres across, replaces part of the solid with free space, whose k is 1. This is the most effective route, and below k ≈ 2 the only one; but less dense films are mechanically weaker, and the strength falls with the solid fraction.
  2. Less ionization: methyl groups replace oxygen, forming Si–CH₃ bonds, a smaller fraction of which ionize than Si–O bonds.
  3. Less polarization: fluorine replaces oxygen, forming Si–F bonds. Fluorine holds its valence electrons more tightly, so the Si–F bond is less polarizable than the Si–O bond it replaces; fluorine atoms also repel one another more strongly than oxygen atoms do, which lowers the density as well.

The second and third routes lower k only modestly. Wolf groups the materials in three ranges: a first generation, k = 2.8–3.5; a second generation, 2.5–2.8; and ultra-low-k, below 2.0.

8.3 The materials the lectures named

Lecture 22 gave three routes from the oxide's 3.9. Fluorine-doped oxide, fluorinated silicate glass (FSG), the lecture put at 3.3. FSG is deposited by PECVD or HDP-CVD from the usual oxide chemistry with a fluorine source added, such as SiF₄, CF₄, C₂F₆ or NF₃. Wolf gives a minimum k of about 3.5 for the FSG films used in ICs, with the fluorine content limited to about 6 %: above that the film is unstable, releases fluorine, and the fluorine forms HF with water and corrodes aluminium lines. In copper wiring the fluorine attacks the tantalum barrier, forming volatile tantalum fluorides and weakening adhesion. FSG is attractive nonetheless because it is a doped CVD oxide much like those already in use, and IBM integrated it at 0.35, 0.25 and 0.18 µm (Wolf §14.4.3). Wolf adds that FSG for copper damascene, deposited on a flat surface with no gap to fill, can take more fluorine, and that its k is 0.2–0.3 lower than that of FSG used with aluminium. Read with the 3.5, that puts damascene FSG at about 3.2–3.3, so the lecture's 3.3 agrees with Wolf for the copper case; Wolf does not say outright which case his 3.5 refers to. Boron- and phosphorus-doped oxides, the lecture said, lower k only slightly, to perhaps 3.8. Boron nitride and boron carbonitride (BN, BCN) films reached k ≈ 2.2 in the lecturer's own group's work (Lecture 22); reported values for these films depend strongly on composition and deposition method, and 2.2 is at the low end, below the 2.5–2.8 of Wolf's second generation and above his ultra-low-k range.

Lecture 24 repeated the argument from the metal side. Aluminium was used first because it was easy to deposit, with no polishing problem in those days; copper came in when RC delay mattered, because a chip carries interconnect whose total length can run to miles, so a lower resistivity pays everywhere. Copper oxidizes in air and its resistance rises, the lecture said, and aluminium does not; aluminium's oxide is hard and adheres tightly to the metal (Wolf §8.11.4), and copper is capped with silicon nitride (§4.2). The lecture's resistivities were aluminium 2.3, copper 1.8 and silver 1.2 µΩ·cm. Handbook values for the pure metals at 20 °C are 2.65, 1.68 and 1.59 µΩ·cm (CRC Handbook of Chemistry and Physics, 84th ed., as reproduced), and Wolf gives 2.7 and 1.7 (§16.1.1), so silver's advantage over copper is about 5 %, not a third. Silver was rejected, the lecture said, for its cost and because it "oxidizes worse than copper"; the tarnish on silver is mainly silver sulfide, formed with sulfur compounds in air, not an oxide. For the dielectric, the lecture put fluorinated oxide at "around three", a "25 % drop" from 3.9 rounded to 4 (from 3.9 the drop is 23 %), against Lecture 22's 3.3, which this section uses; it named Teflon and other polymers at 2.6–2.8, which are hard to polish and for which zirconia abrasives have been investigated (§8.4); and it gave the lecturer's BCN films as 2.2–2.6. Wolf's Table 14-2 lists Teflon films at 1.9–2.1 and most other organic polymer candidates at 2.5–2.9, polyimides higher at 3.0–3.6, so the lecture's 2.6–2.8 describes the polymers as a group but not Teflon.

8.4 Polishing low-k dielectrics

A low-k film in a damascene structure has to survive CMP (Wolf §8.11.7). Most schemes avoid exposing it, capping it with silicon oxide or nitride and polishing the cap. The cap must be thick enough that the polish never reaches the low-k layer, and its erosion must be less than its thickness; but the cap's higher k raises the effective k of the stack. The films raise three problems of their own: adhesion to the cap and to the layer below under the shear of polishing; compressibility, which lets the stack flex, crack the rigid cap and delaminate; and low thermal conductivity, so frictional heating can form hot spots and locally cure the film further. Fluorine-doped oxide adds a fourth: it is softer and more reactive than undoped oxide and is polished faster both mechanically and chemically, as doping softens oxide in general (M13 §6.4). Special slurries have been tried for direct polishing: zirconia (notes p. 125; §1.2), and manganese oxide, which reduced the depth of the deep scratches left by a silica slurry from 20 nm to 2 nm.

A designer replaces an aluminium line in oxide, 1 mm long, by a copper line in a k = 2.2 dielectric of the same cross section, but the route now needs 2 mm. Is the new line faster or slower than the old one, and by what factor? If the example's 1 mm copper line in SiO₂ were made in FSG (k = 3.3) instead, how much would that change its delay?

By eq. (14.8), RC ∝ ρkl² at fixed pitch and aspect ratio. The materials factor falls by (1.7 × 2.2)/(2.7 × 3.9) = 3.74/10.53 = 0.355; doubling the length multiplies by 2² = 4. Net 0.355 × 4 = 1.42: the new line is 42 % slower (53.0 ps against 37.3 ps in the example's geometry), because the delay grows with the square of the length and the materials gain only a factor of 2.8. In FSG the delay scales with k: 3.3/3.9 = 0.846, so 15.4 % less, 19.9 ps against 23.5 ps.

Summary

  • Slurry: 10–100 nm inorganic-oxide abrasive about as hard as the film, in a reagent solution; needs high RR, planarity, local uniformity and selectivity S = RRA/RRB. Particles stay apart by zeta-potential repulsion. Oxide: fumed or colloidal silica in KOH/NH₄OH, pH 9–11, 5–35 wt%; ceria for STI and ILD (pH 6–7, oxide:nitride up to 250:1); zirconia tried for low-k. Metal: oxidizer (ferric nitrate, H₂O₂), buffer, surfactant, complexing agent, inhibitor (BTA for Cu); alumina, > 20:1 to oxide; pH 1–5 W, 3–9 Cu.
  • Metal CMP (Kaufman): oxide forms everywhere → abraded from up-areas → regrows; down-areas stay passivated. The oxide must be soft enough to abrade, dense enough to stop etching. W slurries pH 2–3 (WO₃ stable below pH 4). W:SiO₂ e.g. 120:1. Metal CMP needs overpolish; its endpoint is sharp.
  • Tools: rotary, orbital, linear, fixed table; retaining ring; film, bladder or air-bearing backing. Pads: felt (soft, porous, Suba IV) or cast closed-cell (hard, IC-1000), stacked. Glazing lowers RR (210 → 75 nm/min in 50 wafers); diamond-grit conditioning reopens pores. Slurry-free fixed-abrasive pads.
  • Damascene: W plugs (TiN/Ti liner, three-step W CMP with oxide buff). Cu: trench → barrier Ta/TaN/Ti/TiN (200–300 Å, adhesion and diffusion barrier) → Cu seed ≈ 0.1 µm sputtered → Cu plated 1–2 µm → CMP Cu, change slurry, CMP barrier → SiN cap. Dual damascene: via and trench together over Si₃N₄ etch stops, one fill, one polish.
  • Cu 350 nm/min, 150:1 to oxide; Ta ≈ 30 nm/min; needed: Cu:barrier ≈ 1:1, Cu:insulator > 100:1, hence two or three slurries. Dishing grows with width (pad sag), erosion with density (longer overpolish); tm = h − (D + E); Db = tb(SCu:b − 1). Recess and barrier raise R = ρL/A.
  • Endpoint: dielectric to a thickness (in-line, tf = (T0 − Tf)/RR, deliberate underpolish on a pilot wafer) or metal to a stop (in situ). In situ: laser through a pad window (interference; Cu reflectance drops at Ta), backside IR through the silicon, carrier motor current at constant speed (friction change at a layer change; useless for oxide-only polishes).
  • Cleaning: van der Waals adhesion; remove by repulsion (pH > 7 for silica on oxide; > 8 with metal exposed or alumina), chemistry (SC-1 undercut) and mechanics (PVA brush, double-sided; megasonic). Keep wafers wet; immersion alone fails.
  • RC = 8ρkε0(l/p)²(1/A² + 1): materials factor ρk, geometry (l/p)². Low-k: < 3.9 (SiO₂ 3.9–4.5). Routes: density (pores), ionization (Si–CH₃), polarization (Si–F). FSG: lecture 3.3; Wolf min ≈ 3.5, 0.2–0.3 lower in Cu damascene (≈ 3.2–3.3); ≤ 6 % F; BN/BCN ≈ 2.2 (lecturer's group). Low-k under CMP: caps, adhesion, compressibility, heat.
