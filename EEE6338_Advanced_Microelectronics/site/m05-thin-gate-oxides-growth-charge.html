Module 05

Thin Gate Oxides: Roles and Requirements

Everything silicon dioxide does in a process, why the gate oxide must keep thinning and what that costs, and the ten requirements a deep-submicron gate dielectric has to satisfy at once.

Prerequisites M03 Read time ~30 min Sources Class notes pp. 17–22, 29 (Chapter 3); Wolf Vol. 4 §3.1; Lectures 5, 6, 8

Silicon is not the best semiconductor available by any transport measure. Gallium arsenide has an electron mobility several times higher, germanium has a higher mobility for both carrier types, and both were investigated seriously before silicon became dominant. Silicon won because of its oxide. Silicon dioxide grows directly from the silicon it insulates by simple thermal reaction with oxygen; it is a stable, hard, chemically inert solid with a band gap near 9 eV; it can be patterned selectively; and — decisively — the interface it forms with silicon carries so few electrically active defects that a field-effect device built across it actually works. No competing semiconductor has a native oxide with that combination.

This module is about that oxide in its most demanding application, as the gate dielectric. It begins with the full catalogue of what SiO₂ does in a process, because the gate dielectric is only one of ten roles and the thicknesses those roles need span more than two orders of magnitude. It then states why the gate oxide must keep thinning, what that buys and what it costs, and the ten requirements a deep-submicron gate dielectric has to satisfy simultaneously. Several of those requirements are decided by the bonding of individual atoms, and the two modules that follow take them to that scale: M05a sets out the bonding of silicon, of silicon dioxide and of the interface between them, and M05b the defects in that bonding and the charge they carry.

The notes open Chapter 3 with the issues a thin gate oxide raises. The list is the plan for this module and the four after it, and each item is answered where shown.

  1. The structure of SiO₂ and Si/SiO₂ interface. M05a for the structure; M05b for the defects and charge at the interface.
  2. Dielectric breakdown and defects in SiO₂. M06 §5 for breakdown; M05b for the defects.
  3. Leakage current in SiO₂ films. M06 §1–2, and §4 for the leakage that stress creates.
  4. Models and processes of growth of ultra-thin gate oxides. M06 §8.
  5. Strengthening SiO₂ films through the addition of nitrogen (oxynitrides). M06 §9.
  6. Boron penetration through gate oxides. §3.2 and requirement 9 of §4 below; M06 §9.1 for the nitrogen that blocks it.
  7. Technology of growing ultra-thin gate oxides on 200–300 mm Si wafers. §3.3 below for the uniformity demand; M06 §8.2 for rapid thermal oxidation.
  8. Limits of SiO₂ as a gate dielectric material. M06 §7, then M07.

Reading the list in Lecture 6, the professor appended one further item: the need for high-k dielectrics, such as hafnium oxide and hafnium silicate, which is the subject of M07.

1 · What silicon dioxide is used for

The notes name the gate dielectric in the chapter heading and list nine other roles beneath it, ten in all. They are collected here before any of them is treated in depth, because several later arguments depend on knowing that the same material is doing a different job a few micrometres away.

  1. Gate dielectric. The insulator between the gate electrode and the channel. This is the role the rest of the module concerns.
  2. Diffusion mask. Dopants diffuse through SiO₂ several orders of magnitude more slowly than through silicon — for boron at 1100 °C the diffusivity ratio is of order 10⁻⁶ — so an oxide layer patterned with a window confines a subsequent diffusion to the window. The ratio has to be that large for masking to work at all: a few hundred nanometres of oxide must hold back a diffusion that is driving a micrometre into the exposed silicon beside it. The classic three-mask process in M01 uses it this way.
  3. Surface passivation. A layer deposited over the finished chip at the end of the process, protecting the surface from dust, humidity and mechanical contact. Silicon nitride is now also used in this role. This is mechanical and chemical protection; the electrical sense of passivation is requirement 10 in §4.
  4. Field oxide. The thick oxide lying between devices so that a metal line running from one to another is too far from the silicon to invert it. The lecture gives about 1 µm, which is the micron-era figure; the thickness table below gives 200–400 nm, which is the range that applies once LOCOS reached sub-half-micron design rules.
  5. Interlevel dielectric. The insulator separating one metal wiring layer from the next, with openings etched through it where the two must connect. A modern part has at least six levels of metal interconnection.
  6. Screen oxide. A thin layer left on the surface during ion implantation, whose main job is to suppress channelling — the tendency of an ion to travel abnormally far down an open crystallographic direction. Arriving through an amorphous film deflects the ion so that it enters the silicon at an effectively random angle. The screen also protects the bare surface from the beam. Because silicon and silicon dioxide stop ions at comparable rates, the ion spends part of its range in the oxide and the implanted peak ends up shallower below the silicon surface by roughly the screen thickness, which the implant energy is raised to compensate.
  7. Pad oxide. As the class describes it, the oxide through which a contact window is opened so that a metal line can reach a diffused region beneath. In the wider literature "pad oxide" means something else: the thin thermal oxide grown under a silicon nitride film in LOCOS or STI, whose job is to keep the nitride's intrinsic stress from generating dislocations in the silicon. It is also the path along which oxidant diffuses sideways under the nitride edge to form the bird's beak of M02 §9. The contact-level film described in class is usually called the pre-metal dielectric.
  8. Trench liner in shallow trench isolation. A very thin oxide grown on the walls of an etched trench before the trench is filled with a thicker deposited oxide. Growing the liner rather than depositing it produces a clean interface on the trench sidewall. M15 treats it.
  9. Tunnel oxide. A very thin oxide in non-volatile memory, thin enough that charge deliberately tunnels through it to program and erase a cell — the one application where tunnelling is the intended behaviour rather than a failure.
  10. Sidewall spacers. The deposited oxide standing against the vertical faces of the gate, which sets the source–drain extension length and insulates the gate edge during silicidation. See M02 §6.

The notes then give the thickness each application of thermally grown oxide requires. The range is the point: the same material, grown by the same reaction, is used at thicknesses from 1.5 nm to 400 nm.

Four entries in the table do not appear in the list of ten.

  • Reoxidation of etched gate-stack sidewall damage repairs the silicon exposed at the foot of the gate after the etch that defined it, which is plasma-damaged and would otherwise sit directly in the path of the channel.
  • Inter-polysilicon dielectric is the insulator between two polysilicon layers: in an EEPROM or flash cell it separates the floating gate, which stores the programmed charge, from the control gate above it, and in a capacitor built from two polysilicon plates it is the capacitor's dielectric.
  • Sacrificial oxide is grown and then removed: its purpose is to consume the top few nanometres of silicon, carrying away surface damage and contamination with it, so that the real gate oxide is grown on a clean surface.
  • Capacitor dielectric in DRAM circuits is the insulator of the storage capacitor in a dynamic memory cell, which holds one bit as the presence or absence of charge on that capacitor; a thinner dielectric stores more charge per unit area at the same voltage.

Conversely, four roles in the list have no row in the table: surface passivation, interlevel dielectric, pad oxide and sidewall spacers. The table is restricted to thermally grown oxide, and the passivation layer, the interlevel dielectric and the spacer are normally deposited films.

2 · Why the oxide must keep thinning

M02 established the rule the industry maintained, LE/tox ≈ 45, and the physical reason for it: as the gate shortens, the gate must be brought closer to the channel to retain control of it against the drain. The notes list three benefits under the heading "Benefits of SiO₂ scaling". Only the first comes from the thinner oxide itself; the second and third come from the lateral shrink that the thinner oxide makes possible, and Wolf, which the notes follow, lists all three as benefits of scaling the gate length.

ID = ZL µnCox [(VG − VT)VD − ½VD2]
(5.1)
ID drain current (A) · Z channel width (cm) · L channel length (cm) · µn electron mobility in the channel (cm²/V·s) · Cox oxide capacitance per unit area (F/cm²) · VG, VT, VD gate, threshold and drain voltages (V). Valid below saturation, where VD < VG − VT. The professor writes the channel width as Z; most texts write it as W. This is the notes' form. Taur & Ning carry a body-effect factor m on the last term, −(m/2)VD2, with m between 1.1 and 1.4 in a well-designed device; the notes' form sets m = 1.
  1. Drain current rises. Cox appears as a direct factor in equation (5.1) and varies as 1/tox, so halving the oxide thickness doubles the current available to charge the next stage. More current into the same load is a shorter delay.
  2. Gate area falls, so input capacitance falls. The gate capacitance of the transistor is Cox times the gate area Z×L. When the whole device is scaled, both Z and L shrink, so the area falls as the square even though Cox per unit area has risen. The capacitance that must be charged when logic levels are switched therefore falls, and the RC delay falls with it. This is why clock frequencies moved from around 100 MHz in the 1990s into the gigahertz range.
  3. Device density rises, which is the reason the scaling is pursued at all.

A fourth consequence follows from the second. Dynamic power dissipation in CMOS is the energy needed to charge and discharge the capacitances at each transition, so reducing the gate area reduces the power dissipated per device.

3 · What thinning costs

Reducing the gate length raises the off-state leakage, and reducing the oxide thickness is part of the remedy for that but introduces failures of its own. Both effects must be stated before the requirements of section 4 make sense.

3.1 Subthreshold current and drain-induced barrier lowering

A MOSFET does not switch off abruptly at VG = VT. Below threshold a small drain-to-source current continues to flow, falling exponentially as the gate voltage falls further — by one decade for every 70–100 mV of gate voltage in a typical device, a figure called the subthreshold swing. This is the subthreshold current IDST, and it is one component of the total off-state leakage current of the chip. It is unwanted: in a circuit with a hundred million transistors, most of which are off at any instant, the sum of their subthreshold currents is a continuous power drain that no switching activity accounts for.

In deep-submicron devices, reducing the gate length increases IDST. The notes name the short-channel effect responsible: drain-induced barrier lowering, DIBL. The source-to-channel junction presents an energy barrier that a carrier must surmount to enter the channel, and in a long channel the gate voltage alone sets its height. In a short channel the source–drain distance is comparable to the depth of the depletion region under the gate, so the drain potential penetrates along the channel and shares control of the source barrier with the gate. Raising the drain voltage lowers the barrier, more carriers cross it, and the subthreshold current rises significantly as the gate length is reduced.

The notes give two measures employed to prevent it.

  1. The junction depth xj of the source and drain extension regions must be made shallower. The extension is the shallow part of the two-part drain that reaches under the gate edge, drawn in M02 §2 and treated in M02 §6.2; the notes' margin sketch marks xj there, just under the gate edge. A shallow junction presents less sidewall to the channel, so less of the drain's field is directed along it toward the source.
  2. The depth of the channel depletion region must be made shallower. This is achieved by increasing the doping concentration in the channel with a heavier-dose threshold-adjust implant — for an n-channel device, a heavier boron implant, which raises the acceptor concentration near the surface of the channel (M03 §9). The maximum depletion width Wm varies as NA−1/2 (M03 §4), so quadrupling the channel doping roughly halves the depletion depth.

Both measures keep the electric field of the drain from penetrating along the channel toward the source. The second, however, raises the threshold voltage if the oxide thickness is left unchanged: more boron near the surface increases the depletion charge term QD/Cox of the threshold equation. That increase is counteracted by reducing the gate oxide thickness, which divides the same depletion charge by a larger Cox. Thinning the oxide acts only on that term. The two other terms that depend on the channel doping move in opposite directions: 2φF rises by 2ΔφF, and φMS falls by ΔφF, because the work function of the p-type silicon rises with φF while that of the gate does not (M03 §1.2). Their net rise is ΔφF, which is (kT/q) ln 4 ≈ 36 mV when the doping is quadrupled, and a marginally thinner oxide absorbs it as well. The chain closes on itself: the oxide must thin to permit the doping that permits the short channel. The notes add that the thinner oxide further raises the on-state drain current, through benefit 1 of §2.

3.2 The negative factors of a very thin oxide

Thinning the oxide answers the short-channel problem of §3.1 and creates problems of its own. The notes state them as four negative factors:

  • Thinner oxide films cannot tolerate the increased gate electric fields; smaller power supply voltages are needed.
  • Tunnelling leakage through the oxide increases.
  • The oxide is more prone to penetration by the poly dopant (boron).
  • Defects and oxide-layer thickness non-uniformities become bigger problems when a thinner oxide is manufactured.

Beneath the list the notes draw a single gate stack carrying five labels, after Wolf's Figure 3-3: dielectric breakdown, tunnelling current, penetration of gate dopant, defect and uniformity of film, and reliability of charge injection. Four of the labels are the four bullets; the fifth, charge injection, appears only in the drawing. They are numbered here in the order of the bullets, with charge injection last. Lecture 6 numbered them the same way, drawing 2 to 5 on the stack and treating non-uniformity separately, as §3.3 does.

  1. Dielectric breakdown. A thinner oxide across the same supply voltage carries a higher electric field, and a thin film cannot tolerate it. The supply voltage must therefore be reduced — section 4.2 works the numbers — and Wolf adds that the smaller supply voltage itself reduces the on-state drain current, giving back part of what thinning gained. The breakdown mechanism belongs to M06 §5.
  2. Tunnelling current. Electrons cross the oxide by tunnelling at a rate that rises exponentially as the barrier thins. The notes place direct tunnelling in oxide thinner than about 4 nm and state that below 3 nm the direct tunnelling current becomes very large; Lecture 6 described the gate current appearing at 20 to 30 Å. M06 §1 treats the mechanism.
  3. Penetration of gate dopant. Boron in p⁺ polysilicon diffuses through the oxide into the silicon, changing the channel doping and therefore the threshold voltage of the p-channel device. Wolf's version of the notes' bullet reads "especially boron", and Lecture 6 added that phosphorus does not cause the same problem, so the failure belongs to the p⁺ half of the dual-doped gate of M02 §8.
  4. Defect and uniformity of film. A defect of fixed size — a local thinning, or a cluster of broken bonds — that is a small fraction of a 200 Å film is a large fraction of a 20 Å film, so the same absolute defect density produces far more failures. The same holds for thickness variation: a 1 Å variation is 0.5% of 200 Å and 5% of 20 Å. Section 3.3 takes up uniformity.
  5. Reliability of charge injection. Charge can be injected from the silicon through the thin oxide into the gate, and the injected charge causes reliability problems by modifying the oxide over time. Unlike the tunnelling current of item 2, which a fresh film passes at once, this is a consequence of what operation does to the film, and it is one route to the dielectric breakdown of item 1; M06 §3 follows the trapping it produces.

3.3 Thickness uniformity

A gate oxide specification is not only a thickness; it is a thickness held across every device on the wafer, across every wafer in a batch, and across every batch the tool runs. Any variation in thickness changes Cox, and through it the threshold voltage and the drain current of the affected devices. Gate oxides are grown by dry oxidation, because dry oxide is the highest quality. For the thick oxides of an earlier generation that meant a furnace at 1000–1100 °C. It cannot mean that here: at 1100 °C a 5 nm film is grown in about two minutes, so its thickness would be set by how fast the furnace ramps rather than by the recipe, and none of the uniformity below would be reachable. Ultrathin gate oxides are grown either in a furnace at reduced temperature, where the rate is slow enough to control, or by rapid thermal oxidation for seconds — and the fact that the low-temperature route degrades oxide quality is precisely the tension that made rapid thermal oxidation necessary. M06 §8 takes it up.

Three separate uniformity requirements apply, and failing any one of them is fatal:

  • Across the wafer. A 5 nm specification means 5 nm everywhere on a 300 mm disc, not 4.9 nm in one place and 5.1 nm in another. The devices at the two places would have measurably different characteristics.
  • Wafer to wafer. Oxidation is performed on a batch: the wafers stand in a quartz boat — about twenty at a time in the lecture's description — and the boat is pushed into the furnace. Every wafer sees the same time and the same nominal temperature, and they can still emerge with different thicknesses, because the gas flow and the radiative environment differ along the length of the boat.
  • Run to run. The same recipe run on two different days must give the same thickness. A process that is uniform within a run but drifts between runs cannot be used in manufacturing, because the correction can only ever be applied after the fact.

4 · The ten requirements of a gate dielectric

The notes state what a gate dielectric for deep-submicron MOSFETs has to satisfy. Each item is a constraint that a candidate material must meet simultaneously with all the others, and the list is the standard against which the high-k materials of M07 are later judged. The first sentence of each item follows the notes; what follows it is commentary.

  1. It must be possible to continue to decrease the thickness of the oxide to the degree specified in accordance with the scaling of the gate length, LE/tox ≈ 45. The notes then state the consequence of failing it: "at some point, if SiO₂ cannot be scaled further, another material with higher reliability will be needed." That material is the high-k dielectric of M07.
  2. The thickness must match the design specification closely, with uniformity across the entire wafer, from wafer to wafer, and from run to run.
  3. The oxide film and the Si/SiO₂ interface must maintain stable electrical characteristics and exhibit small values of interface charge — low Qf, Dit, Qot and Qm. M05b explains what each of those is and where it sits.
  4. It must have a high dielectric breakdown strength, above 8 MV/cm — the notes write "BDV", breakdown voltage, but V/cm is a field, and §4.2 turns it into a voltage — and the film must be free of pinholes and contain a negligible number of defects. A pinhole is a conducting path straight through the film.
  5. The film must exhibit a long lifetime under normal operation.
  6. The oxide must be compatible with the other materials of the MOSFET structure, and be thermally, electrically and chemically stable under the processes used to fabricate integrated circuits.
  7. The leakage current through the gate oxide must be sufficiently small to meet the off-current leakage requirement of the circuit in which it is used.
  8. The oxide should exhibit high resistance to hot carrier damage, so that device degradation is low. Hot carriers are electrons or holes that have gained kinetic energy far above the thermal value from a strong electric field — in a MOSFET, chiefly the lateral field near the drain (M02 §6.1).
  9. If the oxide is to be used in asymmetric CMOS technology, in which both p⁺ and n⁺ polysilicon are used, the film must be resistant to boron penetration at the process temperatures used after gate doping. The technology meant is dual-doped polysilicon gates, n⁺ on the n-channel device and p⁺ on the p-channel device (M02 §8); the box below the list takes up the word "asymmetric". The boron is the p⁺ gate's dopant, factor 3 of §3.2.
  10. The oxide must passivate the silicon surface. Passivation here is electrical, not the protective overcoat of role 3 in §1: the oxide must bond to the surface silicon atoms so completely that few electrically active states remain at the interface. The effect was found in 1958, when a Bell Telephone Laboratories group led by Atalla grew a thin oxide over the surface where a pn junction emerged and found the junction leakage reduced by a factor of 10 to 100; it was later understood that the oxide removes or stabilises most of the interface traps. M05b gives the bond-level account.

The notes' "asymmetric CMOS" is the literature's "symmetric CMOS"

Requirement 9 in the notes reads "asymmetric CMOS technology (in both p⁺ and n⁺ poly Si is used)". Wolf, which the notes follow, prints "a symmetric CMOS technology (i.e., in which both p⁺ and n⁺ poly are used)", and symmetric CMOS is the name the literature gives to dual-doped polysilicon gates. With n⁺ polysilicon on the n-channel device and p⁺ on the p-channel device, both transistors are surface-channel devices and can be made alike in channel doping, junction depth and threshold-voltage magnitude, which is the symmetry the name refers to. The notes' single word most likely runs Wolf's two words together. The parenthesis fixes the meaning in both versions: the requirement concerns dual-doped polysilicon gates.

4.1 Requirements 5 and 6, made concrete

"Long lifetime" means twenty years. A chip bought today and failing in a month is not acceptable, and the failure mode is real rather than hypothetical: the oxide degrades under sustained operation, through the field it carries and the charge that passes through it. The lecture put the demand as surviving the enormous number of on–off cycles in twenty years of switching, and the quality of the oxide has to be such that it survives all of them.

Requirement 6 is a related demand on a different timescale. The transistors are built first, and everything else — the multilevel interconnect, the intervening dielectrics, the anneals, the depositions, many of which occur at high temperature — is built on top of them afterwards. The gate oxide must pass through all of that thermal, chemical and electrical processing without change.

4.2 Requirement 4, made concrete

A high-quality dry-grown oxide has a breakdown field in the range 8 to 10 MV/cm. That number is large enough to be misleading when quoted on its own, because the voltage it corresponds to depends on a thickness that is now measured in nanometres.

Eox = Vtox
(5.2)
Eox oxide electric field (V/cm) · V voltage across the oxide (V) · tox oxide thickness (cm). The uniform-field approximation for a parallel-plate structure. Convert the thickness to centimetres first: 5 nm = 50 Å = 5×10⁻⁷ cm, and 1 MV/cm = 10⁶ V/cm.
Supply voltage set by the breakdown field

A 5 nm oxide with 5 V applied. Eox = 5 V / (5×10⁻⁷ cm) = 1×10⁷ V/cm = 10 MV/cm. That sits at the very top of the range a high-quality oxide can survive, with no margin at all.

The same oxide reduced to 2 nm. At a breakdown field of 10 MV/cm the maximum voltage is V = Eoxtox = 10⁷ V/cm × 2×10⁻⁷ cm = 2 V. Anything above 2 V breaks the oxide down.

Allowing margin. A design cannot be run at the breakdown limit, because the oxide is not perfect everywhere and the limit is a mean rather than a guarantee. Allowing roughly 30%, the usable supply is 0.7 × 2 V ≈ 1.4 V. Both steps above take 10 MV/cm, the optimistic end of the 8–10 MV/cm range: at the low end the same 2 nm oxide caps at 8×10⁶ V/cm × 2×10⁻⁷ cm = 1.6 V, and 1.1 V with the same margin.

A 2 nm gate oxide constrains the supply to roughly 1.4 V, and a lower-quality oxide breaking down at 1 MV/cm would not survive at any useful voltage.

Gate oxides of 2 nm are commonplace, so this calculation is one of the reasons supply voltages fell from 5 V to 2 V and below. It was not the only one: hot-carrier damage in the drain field and the dynamic power of switching capacitances pushed in the same direction.

The notes add an operating-point figure: "20 years life time can be achieved if the oxide field remains below 8 MV/cm. For CMOS logic and memory chips the typical field is restricted to 5 MV/cm range." Lecture 8 repeated both numbers.

The notes use 8 MV/cm in two roles that cannot both hold

Requirement 4 makes 8 MV/cm the minimum breakdown strength; the lifetime sentence makes it the field below which an oxide operates for twenty years. Time to breakdown falls by roughly a decade for every 0.3 to 0.5 MV/cm of additional field, so an oxide whose breakdown strength is 8 MV/cm, run at 7.9 MV/cm, survives seconds or hours, not decades. The consistent reading is the notes' second number: 8 MV/cm is the minimum breakdown strength a film must demonstrate to be accepted, and about 5 MV/cm is the field it is then allowed to see in service, which is also the operating field the reliability literature assumes for logic.

Chips are additionally subjected to burn-in tests to screen out those containing oxides that would fail under normal operating conditions during their service life. As Lecture 8 described it, the test is applied to finished, encapsulated chips: they are held at elevated temperature, their electrical properties are measured again after cooling, and the parts that have changed are discarded. Burn-in as standardised in MIL-STD-883 (Test Method 1015) also holds the parts under electrical bias throughout. A weak oxide that would have failed in the field months later fails in the factory instead. The mechanism of breakdown, and why it has two stages, belongs to M06 §5.

A process offers a 1.2 V supply, and the gate oxide may see at most 5 MV/cm in service. What is the thinnest gate oxide that supply permits, and what must that film withstand to meet requirement 4?

Rearranging (5.2), tox ≥ V/Eox = 1.2 V / (5×10⁶ V/cm) = 2.4×10⁻⁷ cm = 2.4 nm, or 24 Å.

Requirement 4 asks that the same film break down only above 8 MV/cm, which at 2.4 nm is 8×10⁶ V/cm × 2.4×10⁻⁷ cm ≈ 1.9 V. The film must therefore survive 1.9 V momentarily in order to be trusted with 1.2 V for twenty years; the factor of 1.6 between the two fields is the margin the lifetime rests on.

5 · From requirements to bonds

Section 4 states the requirements as properties of a film: a charge density, a breakdown strength, a resistance to hot carriers, a passivated surface. Each of them is decided at the scale of individual atoms — whether every silicon atom at the interface has four bonded partners, whether every oxygen bridges two silicon atoms, which bonds are strained or broken, and which foreign atoms sit among them. That is where the next two modules go.

  • Requirement 3, low Qf, Dit, Qot and Qm, is decided by which defects and ions the film contains, and where. M05b.
  • Requirement 4, breakdown strength and few defects, by how completely the oxide network is linked, and where it is broken or strained. M05a and M05b; breakdown itself in M06 §5.
  • Requirement 8, hot-carrier resistance, by whether the bonds that passivate the interface survive energetic carriers. M05b.
  • Requirement 9, boron penetration, by the chemistry of the film, and nitrogen in it. M06 §9.
  • Requirement 10, a passivated surface, by whether each interface silicon has all four bonds satisfied. M05a and M05b.

M05a sets out the bonding itself: the silicon crystal, the tetrahedral unit of silicon dioxide and how tetrahedra link through bridging oxygen, how the oxide grows into the silicon it consumes, and how the bonding changes across the Si/SiO₂ interface. M05b then takes the departures from that bonding — dangling, stretched and broken bonds, and foreign atoms — and the four charges they produce, the hydrogen anneal that neutralises most interface traps, and the hot-carrier process that undoes it.

Summary

  • The notes open Chapter 3 with eight issues: oxide and interface structure; breakdown and defects; leakage; growth of ultra-thin oxides; oxynitrides; boron penetration; growth on 200–300 mm wafers; the limits of SiO₂. M05 to M07 answer them.
  • SiO₂ serves ten roles in a process. As thermal oxide it spans 1.5 nm (gate) to 400 nm (LOCOS field oxide). Four table entries are not in the list of roles: sidewall re-oxidation, inter-poly dielectric, sacrificial oxide and DRAM capacitor dielectric.
  • Thinning the gate oxide raises drain current through Cox, reduces input capacitance through the reduced gate area, and raises device density; reduced gate area also reduces power.
  • Shortening the gate raises the subthreshold current through drain-induced barrier lowering. The remedies are a shallower extension junction xj and a shallower channel depletion region, from a heavier boron threshold-adjust implant in the n-channel device; that raises VT, which a thinner oxide counteracts.
  • The notes' four negative factors of thinning: the field forces a lower supply, tunnelling leakage rises, boron penetrates from p⁺ poly, and defects and non-uniformity matter more. Their diagram labels five concerns: dielectric breakdown, tunnelling current, gate-dopant penetration, defect and uniformity of film, and reliability of charge injection.
  • Thickness uniformity is required in three independent senses: across the wafer, wafer to wafer within a batch, and run to run.
  • Of the ten requirements, number 9's "asymmetric CMOS" (the notes' word; Wolf and the literature say "symmetric CMOS") means dual-doped polysilicon gates, and number 10's passivation is electrical — few interface states — not the protective overcoat.
  • Breakdown field is 8–10 MV/cm for a high-quality dry oxide. At 2 nm that caps the applied voltage at 2 V taking the top of the range, 1.6 V taking the bottom, and about 1.4 V with a 30% margin. 8 MV/cm is a minimum acceptable breakdown strength; parts are run near 5 MV/cm.
