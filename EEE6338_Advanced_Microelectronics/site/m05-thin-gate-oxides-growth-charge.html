Module 05

Thin Gate Oxides: Roles, Requirements and Charge

Everything silicon dioxide does in a process, what a gate dielectric must satisfy, the atomic structure of a thermally grown oxide, and the four kinds of charge that live in it and at its interface with silicon.

Prerequisites M03 Read time ~45 min Sources Class notes pp. 17–28 (Chapter 3); Lectures 5, 6

Silicon is not the best semiconductor available by any transport measure. Gallium arsenide has an electron mobility several times higher, germanium has a higher mobility for both carrier types, and both were investigated seriously before silicon became dominant. Silicon won because of its oxide. Silicon dioxide grows directly from the silicon it insulates by simple thermal reaction with oxygen; it is a stable, hard, chemically inert solid with a band gap near 9 eV; it can be patterned selectively; and — decisively — the interface it forms with silicon carries so few electrically active defects that a field-effect device built across it actually works. No competing semiconductor has a native oxide with that combination.

This module is about that oxide in its most demanding application, as the gate dielectric, and about the ways it falls short. It begins with the full catalogue of what SiO₂ does in a process, because the gate dielectric is only one of ten roles and the thickness required spans more than two orders of magnitude across them. It then states why the gate oxide must keep thinning, what that buys and what it costs, and the ten requirements a deep-submicron gate dielectric has to satisfy simultaneously. The second half is physical: the atomic structure of a thermally grown oxide, and the four distinct kinds of charge found in it and at its interface — where each comes from, which are controlled and which are not, and how each moves the threshold voltage.

1 · What silicon dioxide is used for

The notes list ten roles. They are collected here before any of them is treated in depth, because several later arguments depend on knowing that the same material is doing a different job a few micrometres away.

  1. Gate dielectric. The insulator between the gate electrode and the channel. This is the role the rest of the module concerns.
  2. Diffusion mask. Dopants diffuse through SiO₂ several orders of magnitude more slowly than through silicon — for boron at 1100 °C the diffusivity ratio is of order 10⁻⁶ — so an oxide layer patterned with a window confines a subsequent diffusion to the window. The ratio has to be that large for masking to work at all: a few hundred nanometres of oxide must hold back a diffusion that is driving a micrometre into the exposed silicon beside it. The classic three-mask process in M01 uses it this way.
  3. Surface passivation. A layer covering the finished chip, protecting the surface from dust, humidity and mechanical contact. Silicon nitride is now also used in this role.
  4. Field oxide. The thick oxide lying between devices so that a metal line running from one to another is too far from the silicon to invert it. The lecture gives about 1 µm, which is the micron-era figure; the thickness table below gives 200–400 nm, which is the range that applies once LOCOS reached sub-half-micron design rules.
  5. Interlevel dielectric. The insulator separating one metal wiring layer from the next, with openings etched through it where the two must connect. A modern part has at least six levels of metal interconnection.
  6. Screen oxide. A thin layer left on the surface during ion implantation, whose main job is to suppress channelling — the tendency of an ion to travel abnormally far down an open crystallographic direction. Arriving through an amorphous film deflects the ion so that it enters the silicon at an effectively random angle. The screen also protects the bare surface from the beam. Because silicon and silicon dioxide stop ions at comparable rates, the ion spends part of its range in the oxide and the implanted peak ends up shallower below the silicon surface by roughly the screen thickness, which the implant energy is raised to compensate.
  7. Pad oxide. As the class describes it, the oxide through which a contact window is opened so that a metal line can reach a diffused region beneath. Note that in the wider literature "pad oxide" means something else: the thin thermal oxide grown under a silicon nitride film in LOCOS or STI, whose job is to keep the nitride's intrinsic stress from generating dislocations in the silicon. That is the layer §9 of M02 identifies as the cause of the bird's beak. The contact-level film described here is usually called the pre-metal dielectric.
  8. Trench liner in shallow trench isolation. A very thin oxide grown on the walls of an etched trench before the trench is filled with a thicker deposited oxide. Growing the liner rather than depositing it produces a clean interface on the trench sidewall. M15 treats it.
  9. Tunnel oxide. A very thin oxide in non-volatile memory, thin enough that charge deliberately tunnels through it to program and erase a cell — the one application where tunnelling is the intended behaviour rather than a failure.
  10. Sidewall spacers. The deposited oxide standing against the vertical faces of the gate, which sets the source–drain extension length and insulates the gate edge during silicidation. See M02 §6.

The thicknesses these roles require differ enormously, and the table below is worth reading as a single statement of the range the same material must cover.

A sacrificial oxide is grown and then removed: its purpose is to consume the top few nanometres of silicon, carrying away surface damage and contamination with it, so that the real gate oxide is grown on a clean surface. Reoxidation of etched gate-stack sidewall damage repairs the silicon exposed at the foot of the gate after the etch that defined it, which is plasma-damaged and would otherwise sit directly in the path of the channel.

2 · Why the oxide must keep thinning

M02 established the rule the industry maintained, LE/tox ≈ 45, and the physical reason for it: as the gate shortens, the gate must be brought closer to the channel to retain control of it against the drain. The notes state three separate benefits of scaling the oxide down, and they are distinct from each other.

ID = ZL µnCox [(VG − VT)VD − ½VD2]
(5.1)
ID drain current (A) · Z channel width (cm) · L channel length (cm) · µn electron mobility in the channel (cm²/V·s) · Cox oxide capacitance per unit area (F/cm²) · VG, VT, VD gate, threshold and drain voltages (V). Valid below saturation, where VD < VG − VT. The professor writes the channel width as Z; the notes and most texts write it as W. Z/L is typically in the range 5 to 10.
  1. Drain current rises. Cox appears as a direct factor in equation (5.1) and varies as 1/tox, so halving the oxide thickness doubles the current available to charge the next stage. More current into the same load is a shorter delay.
  2. Gate area falls, so input capacitance falls. The gate capacitance of the transistor is Cox times the gate area Z×L. When the whole device is scaled, both Z and L shrink, so the area falls as the square even though Cox per unit area has risen. The capacitance that must be charged when logic levels are switched therefore falls, and the RC delay falls with it. This is why clock frequencies moved from around 100 MHz in the 1990s into the gigahertz range.
  3. Device density rises, which is the reason the scaling is pursued at all.

A fourth consequence follows from the second. Dynamic power dissipation in CMOS is the energy needed to charge and discharge the capacitances at each transition, so reducing the gate area reduces the power dissipated per device.

3 · What thinning costs

Reducing the gate length raises the off-state leakage, and reducing the oxide thickness is the remedy for that but introduces failures of its own. Both effects must be stated before the requirements of section 4 make sense.

3.1 Subthreshold current and drain-induced barrier lowering

A MOSFET does not switch off abruptly at VG = VT. Below threshold a small drain-to-source current continues to flow, falling exponentially as the gate voltage falls further. This is the subthreshold current IDST, and it is one component of the total off-state leakage current of the chip. It is unwanted: in a circuit with a hundred million transistors, most of which are off at any instant, the sum of their subthreshold currents is a continuous power drain that no switching activity accounts for.

In deep-submicron devices, reducing the gate length increases IDST. The mechanism has a name: drain-induced barrier lowering, DIBL. The source-to-channel junction presents an energy barrier that a carrier must surmount to enter the channel, and the gate voltage controls its height. When the channel is long, the drain is far away and affects that barrier negligibly. When the channel is short, the drain's depletion region reaches close enough to the source that the drain voltage lowers the barrier directly, without the gate's involvement. More carriers then cross it, and the subthreshold current rises significantly as the gate length is reduced.

The notes give two measures employed to prevent it, and both are geometric rather than electrical.

  1. The junction depth xj of the source and drain extension regions must be made shallower. A shallow junction has a smaller depletion volume and its field reaches a shorter distance along the channel.
  2. The depth of the channel depletion region must be made shallower, which is achieved by increasing the doping concentration in the channel — a heavier-dose threshold-adjust implant.

Both measures keep the electric field of the drain from penetrating along the channel toward the source. The second, however, raises the threshold voltage, because a higher channel doping increases the depletion charge term of the threshold equation. That increase is counteracted by reducing the gate oxide thickness, which divides the same depletion charge by a larger Cox. The chain closes on itself: the oxide must thin to permit the doping that permits the short channel.

3.2 The five failures of a very thin oxide

The notes list five negative factors, and the professor drew all five on a single gate stack. They are collected in the same way here.

  1. The field the oxide must withstand rises. A thinner oxide across the same supply voltage carries a higher electric field, and a thin oxide film cannot tolerate it. The consequence is that the power supply voltage must be reduced — section 4.2 works the numbers.
  2. Tunnelling leakage increases. Electrons cross the oxide by tunnelling at a rate that rises exponentially as the barrier thins. At 20 to 30 Å the gate current becomes significant. M06 treats the mechanism.
  3. The oxide is more prone to penetration by the gate dopant. Boron in p⁺ polysilicon diffuses through the oxide into the silicon, changing the channel doping and therefore the threshold voltage of the p-channel device. Phosphorus does not have this problem, so the failure is specific to the dual-doped gate of M02 §8.
  4. Defects matter more. A pinhole or a cluster of oxide defects that is a small fraction of a 200 Å film is a large fraction of a 20 Å film, and the same absolute defect density produces far more failures.
  5. Charge injection from the silicon into the gate becomes possible, and the injected charge causes reliability problems by modifying the oxide over time.

3.3 Thickness uniformity

A gate oxide specification is not only a thickness; it is a thickness held across every device on the wafer, across every wafer in a batch, and across every batch the tool runs. Any variation in thickness changes Cox, and through it the threshold voltage and the drain current of the affected devices. Gate oxides are grown by dry oxidation, because dry oxide is the highest quality. For the thick oxides of an earlier generation that meant a furnace at 1000–1100 °C. It cannot mean that here: at 1100 °C a 5 nm film is grown in about two minutes, so its thickness would be set by how fast the furnace ramps rather than by the recipe, and none of the uniformity below would be reachable. Ultrathin gate oxides are grown either in a furnace at reduced temperature, where the rate is slow enough to control, or by rapid thermal oxidation for seconds — and the fact that the low-temperature route degrades oxide quality is precisely the tension that made rapid thermal oxidation necessary. M06 takes it up.

Three separate uniformity requirements apply, and failing any one of them is fatal:

  • Across the wafer. A 5 nm specification means 5 nm everywhere on a 300 mm disc, not 4.9 nm in one place and 5.1 nm in another. The devices at the two places would have measurably different characteristics.
  • Wafer to wafer. Oxidation is performed on a batch — the wafers are stacked vertically in a quartz boat, typically around twenty at a time, and the boat is pushed into the furnace. Every wafer sees the same time and the same nominal temperature, and they can still emerge with different thicknesses, because the gas flow and the radiative environment differ along the length of the boat.
  • Run to run. The same recipe run on two different days must give the same thickness. A process that is uniform within a run but drifts between runs cannot be used in manufacturing, because the correction can only ever be applied after the fact.

4 · The ten requirements of a gate dielectric

The notes state what a gate dielectric for deep-submicron MOSFETs has to satisfy. Each item is a constraint that a candidate material must meet simultaneously with all the others, and the list is the standard against which the high-k materials of M07 are later judged.

  1. It must be possible to continue to decrease the thickness of the oxide to the degree specified by the scaling of the gate length, LE/tox ≈ 45.
  2. The thickness must match the design specification closely, with uniformity across the entire wafer, from wafer to wafer, and from run to run.
  3. The oxide film and the Si/SiO₂ interface must maintain stable electrical characteristics and exhibit small values of interface charge — low Qf, Dit, Qot and Qm. Section 6 explains what each of those is.
  4. It must have a high dielectric breakdown strength, above 8 MV/cm — the notes call this the breakdown voltage, but V/cm is a field, and §4.2 turns it into a voltage — and the film must be free of pinholes and contain a negligible number of defects.
  5. The film must exhibit a long lifetime under normal operation.
  6. The oxide must be compatible with the other materials of the MOSFET structure, and be thermally, electrically and chemically stable under the processes used to fabricate integrated circuits.
  7. The leakage current through the gate oxide must be sufficiently small to meet the off-current leakage requirement of the circuit in which it is used.
  8. The oxide should exhibit high resistance to hot carrier damage, so that device degradation is low.
  9. If the oxide is to be used in a CMOS technology in which both p⁺ and n⁺ polysilicon are used, the film must be resistant to boron penetration at the process temperatures used after gate doping.
  10. The oxide must passivate the silicon surface.

4.1 Requirement 5, made concrete

"Long lifetime" means twenty years. A chip bought today and failing in a month is not acceptable, and the failure mode is real rather than hypothetical: the oxide degrades under the repeated field reversals of normal switching. Twenty years of operation is an enormous number of cycles, and the quality of the oxide has to be such that it survives all of them.

Requirement 6 is a related demand on a different timescale. The transistors are built first, and everything else — the multilevel interconnect, the intervening dielectrics, the anneals, the depositions, many of which occur at high temperature — is built on top of them afterwards. The gate oxide must pass through all of that thermal, chemical and electrical processing without change.

4.2 Requirement 4, made concrete

A high-quality dry-grown oxide has a breakdown field in the range 8 to 10 MV/cm. That number is large enough to be misleading when quoted on its own, because the voltage it corresponds to depends on a thickness that is now measured in nanometres.

Eox = Vtox
(5.2)
Eox oxide electric field (V/cm) · V voltage across the oxide (V) · tox oxide thickness (cm). The uniform-field approximation for a parallel-plate structure. Convert the thickness to centimetres first: 5 nm = 50 Å = 5×10⁻⁷ cm.
Supply voltage set by the breakdown field

A 5 nm oxide with 5 V applied. Eox = 5 / (5×10⁻⁷) = 1×10⁷ V/cm = 10 MV/cm. That sits at the very top of the range a high-quality oxide can survive, with no margin at all.

The same oxide reduced to 2 nm. At a breakdown field of 10 MV/cm the maximum voltage is V = 10⁷ × 2×10⁻⁷ = 2 V. Anything above 2 V breaks the oxide down.

Allowing margin. A design cannot be run at the breakdown limit, because the oxide is not perfect everywhere and the limit is a mean rather than a guarantee. Allowing roughly 30%, the usable supply is about 1.4 V. Note that both steps above take 10 MV/cm, the optimistic end of the 8–10 MV/cm range: at the low end the same 2 nm oxide caps at 1.6 V, and 1.1 V with the same margin.

A 2 nm gate oxide constrains the supply to roughly 1.4 V, and a lower-quality oxide breaking down at 1 MV/cm would not survive at any useful voltage.

Gate oxides of 2 nm are commonplace, so this calculation is the reason supply voltages fell from 5 V to 2 V and below. The reduction was not a power-saving choice made independently; it was forced by the dielectric.

The notes add an operating-point figure: a twenty-year lifetime can be achieved if the oxide field remains below 8 MV/cm, and for CMOS logic and memory chips the typical field is restricted to the 5 MV/cm range. Those two numbers do different jobs and only the second is an operating limit. Time to breakdown falls by roughly a decade for every 0.3 to 0.5 MV/cm of additional field, so an oxide run at 7.9 MV/cm — just inside a breakdown strength of 8 — survives for seconds or hours, not decades. Read 8 MV/cm as the minimum breakdown strength a film must demonstrate to be accepted, and ~5 MV/cm as the field it is then allowed to see in service. Chips are additionally subjected to burn-in tests — operation at elevated voltage and temperature before shipping — to screen out those containing oxides that would fail under normal operating conditions during their service life. The mechanism of breakdown, and why it has two stages, belongs to M06.

5 · The structure of a thermally grown oxide

Understanding where charge comes from requires knowing what the oxide is made of. The basic unit of SiO₂ is a tetrahedron: each silicon atom, which contributes four valence electrons and acts as Si⁴⁺, is bonded to four oxygen atoms arranged at the corners of a tetrahedron around it.

The arrangement is three-dimensional and does not survive being drawn flat. Four points equidistant from a centre and as far from each other as possible cannot lie in a plane; they form a tetrahedron, with every O–Si–O angle equal to 109.5°. Drawn on paper, one of the four oxygen atoms necessarily projects forward or backward out of the page, and in a two-dimensional sketch of the network that fourth bond is the one that disappears.

How those tetrahedra connect determines whether the material is crystalline or amorphous. In crystalline SiO₂ every oxygen atom belongs to two tetrahedra and is thus bonded to two silicon atoms. Such an oxygen is a bridging oxygen: it links two tetrahedra into a continuous network. In amorphous SiO₂ — fused silica, which is what a thermally grown gate oxide is — some of the vertices of the tetrahedra carry non-bridging oxygen atoms, bonded to one silicon atom only, with the other bond terminated by something else or left unsatisfied.

The ratio of the two governs the mechanical and electrical integrity of the film: the greater the ratio of bridging to non-bridging oxygens, the greater the cohesiveness of the SiO₂ structure. A non-bridging oxygen is a place where the network is interrupted, and interruptions are where charge is trapped and where breakdown begins.

The interatomic distances the notes give are Si–O 1.62 Å, O–O 2.27 Å and Si–Si 3.12 Å, with the Si–O–Si bond angle varying from 110° to 180° and the most probable value being 144°. (The measured distribution in vitreous silica runs from about 120° to 180°.) It is the angular freedom of that bridging bond — a bond that can be bent across a 60° range at little energy cost — that allows SiO₂ to form an amorphous network rather than being forced into a crystal.

One of those three distances does not fit the other two

The numbers can be checked against each other. The Si–Si distance follows from the Si–O length and the bridging angle: 2 × 1.62 × sin(144°/2) = 3.08 Å, which agrees with the quoted 3.12 Å. The O–O distance is the edge of a tetrahedron of side Si–O, which for the tetrahedral angle of 109.5° is 2 × 1.62 × sin(109.5°/2) = 2.65 Å, not 2.27 Å — and 2.65 Å is exactly the value measured by X-ray diffraction in vitreous silica, so the check closes on the published number. Going the other way, an O–O distance of 2.27 Å with Si–O of 1.62 Å would require an O–Si–O angle of 89°, which a tetrahedron cannot have.

Use the notes' figures for anything assessed. The check is worth doing because it is the kind of internal consistency test that costs one line and catches a transcription error in any table of this sort.

One consequence of these dimensions deserves stating on its own. A 2 nm gate oxide is only 10 to 12 atomic layers thick. At that point the film is no longer a bulk material with two surfaces; it is almost entirely surface, and the interface regions at the top and bottom occupy most of it. This is why the properties discussed in the rest of this module — interface states, non-uniformity, defects — dominate the behaviour of a thin oxide in a way they never did for a thick one.

6 · The four charges

The Qi/Cox term of the threshold voltage equation, introduced in M03, is not one physical quantity. It is the sum of four, distinguished by where the charge sits, what produced it, and whether it can move.

Qi = Qf + Qit + Qot + Qm
(5.3)
Qf fixed oxide charge (C/cm²), also counted as Nf in charges/cm² · Qit interface trapped charge (C/cm²), counted as Nit in charges/cm² or as Dit in charges/cm² per eV · Qot oxide trapped charge (C/cm²), counted as Not · Qm mobile charge (C/cm²), counted as Nm. All four are areal densities, and all four must be as small as possible.

The four divide cleanly by how much attention each needs today. The professor's summary is that Qm and Qf are now well controlled, and their impact does not become more severe as oxides are scaled; whereas reliability involves Qit and Qot, and work on them is ongoing. The two that remain difficult are the two that keep being generated — by the process itself, and then again throughout the operating life — rather than being set once and left.

6.1 Fixed oxide charge, Qf

Fixed oxide charge sits very close to the interface, within about two nanometres of it, and does not move or change its charge state with gate voltage. Its origin is the oxidation reaction itself. Oxidation proceeds by oxygen reaching the silicon surface and bonding to it, and at the moment growth stops there is a last layer of silicon atoms that are partly oxidised — bonded to some oxygen but not fully coordinated. Those incompletely bonded silicon atoms near the interface carry the fixed charge, and it is positive.

Because it is established by the final moments of the oxidation, Qf depends on the oxidation conditions and on the crystal orientation of the silicon, and is fixed thereafter. It is the component that was brought under control first.

6.2 Interface trapped charge, Qit

Interface trapped charge is charge localised at sites at the silicon surface. Those sites arise from energy states that exist in the forbidden gap of the silicon — extra allowed energy states present at the semiconductor surface but not within the bulk.

Their origin is the termination of the crystal. In bulk silicon every atom has four neighbours and all four of its bonds are satisfied. At a surface the lattice stops, and the atoms in the last plane have bonds pointing outward with nothing to bond to. These are dangling bonds. A silicon atom with a dangling bond has only three bonded neighbours and can be regarded as a trivalent silicon atom.

Because the energy field of the crystal at such an atom is one-sided — the electrons in the surface region are bonded only from the side directed toward the bulk — the dangling bond gives rise to an additional allowed electronic state, and an electron occupying it is localised, confined to a restricted region of space centred on that atom. A localised state in the band gap is a trap: it captures a free carrier from the channel and holds it.

Thermal oxidation ties up these surface silicon bonds along a nearly perfect Si/SiO₂ interface, which is precisely why silicon works as a MOS material. But oxidation does not tie up all the bonds. A small fraction is left unsatisfied, and a significant number of surface states can still exist. The notes make this quantitative, and the calculation is worth working because it shows how small a fraction is still too many.

How many unsatisfied bonds are too many

A (100) silicon surface has 6.8×10¹⁴ silicon atoms per cm².

Suppose oxidation leaves one in a thousand of those bonds dangling, and each gives rise to one surface state carrying one electronic charge. The interface trapped charge density is then 6.8×10¹⁴ / 1000 = 6.8×10¹¹ cm⁻².

For a 20 nm gate oxide, Cox = 3.9 × 8.85×10⁻¹⁴ / 2×10⁻⁶ = 1.73×10⁻⁷ F/cm², and the charge is Qit = 6.8×10¹¹ × 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ = 1.09×10⁻⁷ C/cm².

The threshold shift is Qit/Cox = 1.09×10⁻⁷ / 1.73×10⁻⁷.

ΔVT = 0.63 V

A defect rate of one bond in a thousand — a 99.9% perfect interface — shifts the threshold voltage by more than a third of a modern supply voltage. This is the measure of how good the Si/SiO₂ interface has to be, and of how remarkable it is that it can be made that good.

Dangling bonds are not the only source. Crystal defects near the surface, and foreign atoms bonded at the surface, are other interface perturbations that produce surface states. So do stretched bonds: the notes distinguish weak Si–Si and Si–O stretch bonds at the interface, where the lattice mismatch between crystalline silicon and amorphous oxide forces bonds away from their equilibrium length and angle.

The two kinds are geometrically distinct. A Si–Si stretch bond is what remains where an oxygen atom is missing from the network altogether — an oxygen vacancy. Across an intact bridging oxygen two silicon atoms sit about 3.06 Å apart. Remove the oxygen and they relax toward each other and bond directly, at about 2.46 Å, against 2.35 Å for an ordinary Si–Si bond in crystalline silicon. The surrounding network resists that contraction, so the bond is held strained: its bonding level is pushed up out of the SiO₂ valence band, which makes this the easiest site in the oxide for a hole to localise on.

What happens when a hole arrives is worth following, because the product has a name the literature uses throughout. The vacancy is bistable. Neutral, it is the Si–Si dimer just described; on capturing a hole that dimer bond breaks and the structure relaxes asymmetrically, one silicon puckering through the plane of its three oxygens toward a back oxygen while the unpaired electron stays on the other. The resulting positively charged, paramagnetic defect is the E′ centre, and it is the dominant hole trap in thermal SiO₂ — the same defect that appears in §6.5 as oxide trapped charge Qot, and the one measured by electron spin resonance. Because the structure relaxes rather than merely ionising, the trapping is largely irreversible at room temperature, which is why removing it takes a deliberate anneal.

A Si–O stretch bond has its oxygen present and bonded, but displaced from the atomic position the network wants, with the silicon shifted with it. Nothing is missing and no bond is broken; the bond is merely strained. Where such a bond sits decides what kind of level it produces, and the rule is the same for both bond types — the list below gives it.

Where a defect sits determines the kind of level it produces:

  • A trivalent silicon atom at the interface introduces a deep trap level near the silicon midgap. Midgap is the worst possible position for a trap, and the reason is emission rather than capture. How readily a trap captures a carrier depends on its capture cross-section and on how many carriers are available, not on where its level sits. What depends steeply on the level position is how quickly the captured carrier is thermally re-emitted back to the band it came from. A shallow trap captures an electron and returns it to the conduction band long before a hole arrives, so no recombination is completed; a midgap trap holds the electron long enough for a hole to be captured too. Minimising re-emission is what makes a midgap level the most efficient recombination centre.
  • The same trivalent silicon in the oxide bulk introduces a deep trap level in the SiO₂ band gap instead.
  • Weak Si–Si and Si–O stretch bonds at the interface introduce a continuum of deep trap levels — not a single energy but a distribution, which is why interface traps are described by Dit, a density per unit energy, rather than by a single count.
  • The same stretched bonds in the bulk of the oxide produce shallow trap levels.
  • A dangling silicon bond tied up with an impurity, mostly hydrogen or a hydroxyl group, produces an electron trap.
  • An oxygen vacancy, equivalently a weak Si–Si stretch bond, produces a hole trap.

6.3 Passivating the interface with hydrogen

The remaining dangling bonds are removed chemically, and the step is deliberately the last thermal step in the whole process. The wafer is annealed in a hydrogen ambient at 400–450 °C after metallization — which is why it is variously called the post-metallization anneal, the final anneal, the sinter or the alloy anneal, and why 450 °C is the ceiling: aluminium is already on the wafer. The placement is not arbitrary. Everything that happens after the oxide is grown — implantation, plasma etching, plasma deposition, metallization — generates interface traps, so an anneal performed at oxidation would be undone before the wafer was finished. The anneal drives hydrogen into the structure, where it reaches the interface and bonds to the unsatisfied silicon bonds that thermal oxidation did not tie up. A silicon atom terminated by hydrogen has four satisfied bonds and no longer contributes a level in the silicon band gap, which is why the anneal drops Dit by orders of magnitude. A dangling bond terminated this way is passivated; one that is not is unpassivated, and it is the unpassivated population that the arithmetic above counts. Hydrogen and hydroxyl species left in the bulk of the oxide are a separate population at a separate location, and those are the ones associated with electron traps in the list in §6.2.

The ambient is either 100% H₂ or a dilute mixture of a few percent hydrogen in an inert carrier, the mixture being called forming gas. Dilution is a safety measure, and the numbers are chosen precisely. Hydrogen is flammable in air between about 4% and 75% by volume, an unusually wide range. A mixture holding less than about 4% hydrogen therefore cannot produce a flammable atmosphere however it escapes and however it mixes with air, which is why the dilute mixtures used for this anneal sit just under that limit and are classified as non-flammable. Pure hydrogen at this temperature is a different matter and demands correspondingly careful handling.

Which inert gas dilutes the hydrogen

Strictly, forming gas denotes hydrogen diluted in nitrogen, and nitrogen is the usual carrier: it is far cheaper than argon and unreactive with silicon and its oxide at 450 °C, molecular N₂ not nitriding either appreciably below roughly 1000 °C. Compositions from about 3% to 10% H₂ are all in ordinary use — this course's own textbooks give 5% and 10% — and the low end is chosen for the reason above, since below about 4% the mixture cannot burn.

Argon-diluted hydrogen is also standard wherever nitrogen itself must be excluded, and it is sold commercially in exactly the 3%-in-argon composition. Nothing in the passivation physics turns on the choice: the carrier dilutes the hydrogen and sweeps the tube, and takes no part in the reaction at the interface.

Hydrogen is not the only species that will terminate a dangling bond. A hydroxyl radical, OH, is also available — the wafer meets water at every clean — and it bonds to a trivalent silicon atom in the same way. It is not a substitute, but the reason is not that the bond is easier to break: releasing a hydroxyl means breaking a Si–O bond, which at roughly 4.8 eV is considerably stronger than the 3.3 eV of Si–H. What disqualifies it is the electrical result. A dangling bond terminated by hydrogen has four satisfied bonds and no state in the gap; a dangling bond terminated by a hydroxyl group leaves an electron trap, as the list in §6.2 records. Hydroxyl removes the midgap level and substitutes a trap of its own, so the site is quieter but not silent. Passivation is therefore described in terms of hydrogen throughout.

The passivation is not permanent, and this is the origin of one of the most important reliability mechanisms in MOS devices. Si–H and Si–OH bonds can be broken easily by injected hot electrons. A carrier arriving at the interface with a few electronvolts of energy can transfer enough to break the bond, releasing the hydrogen and restoring the dangling bond — and with it the interface trap. Interface traps are therefore not a fixed property of a finished device; they are generated progressively throughout its operating life, wherever hot carriers reach the interface.

Hydrogen both fixes the interface and makes it fragile

The notes state the trade explicitly: hydrogen introduced in the post-anneal can increase hot electron degradation, and the ideal gate dielectric should contain as little hydrogen as possible. Read literally that is in tension with the paragraph above, which needs hydrogen to passivate the interface in the first place. The resolution is that what matters is not how much hydrogen is present but how tightly it is held: the same hydrogen that passivates a dangling bond today is the species released when a hot carrier breaks that bond, and once released it migrates and breaks others. Excess hydrogen beyond what the interface needs is therefore a liability.

The cleanest demonstration is isotopic. Annealing in deuterium rather than hydrogen passivates exactly the same bonds in the same number, and extends hot-carrier lifetime by one to two orders of magnitude — because a deuterium atom is twice the mass and the bond's vibrational modes couple far less efficiently to the energy an arriving carrier delivers. Coverage was not what changed; the ease of removal was.

This is why hot carrier injection appears twice in this course with different consequences. In M02 §6 it was a reason to redesign the drain; here it is the mechanism that generates interface traps over time. Both consequences follow from the same carriers.

6.4 What interface traps do

The notes give four effects.

  1. They change the surface potential. The charge present in the interface traps interacts with the charge in the silicon near the surface, altering the silicon charge distribution and therefore the surface potential — which is the quantity that defines threshold.
  2. They act as generation–recombination centres, giving rise to leakage currents. A trap at midgap provides a two-step path across the band gap that is far more probable than the single-step transition, so carriers are generated at the interface that would not be generated in the bulk.
  3. They add a capacitance. The density of interface trap charge changes with the surface potential, which means charge flows into and out of the traps as the gate voltage changes. That is a capacitance, Cit, and it appears in parallel with the depletion capacitance of the silicon — the two together then sitting in series with Cox. The distinction is what the measurement sees: because the trap capacitance is in series with the oxide, no density of interface traps can push the measured capacitance above Cox. Interface traps therefore reveal themselves by stretching a capacitance–voltage curve along the voltage axis rather than by raising it.
  4. They weaken the oxide, causing second-stage breakdown in the gate oxide.
ΔVT = − QoxCox
(5.4)
ΔVT threshold voltage shift (V) · Qox effective oxide charge per unit area (C/cm²) · Cox oxide capacitance per unit area (F/cm²). This is the Qi/Cox term of the threshold equation, written as a shift because it is what changes when the oxide charge changes. Positive oxide charge gives a negative shift. Effective is doing work in that gloss. A charge at distance x from the gate inside an oxide of thickness tox shifts the threshold by only the fraction x/tox of what the same charge would do at the silicon interface, so what enters is the charge weighted by its distance from the gate — its centroid. Substituting the bare sum of equation (5.3) is exact for Qit, which sits at the interface, and close enough for Qf, which sits within about 2 nm of it. For Qot distributed through the bulk and for Qm, which moves, the bare sum is an upper bound — and §6.6 is precisely the argument that position matters.

Equation (5.4) closes the loop on the degradation mechanism. Hot electron injection produces interface traps by breaking Si–H bonds and increasing the number of silicon dangling bonds. That increases the total Qox, which shifts VT, which reduces the drain current of equation (5.1), since the current depends on VG − VT. If the drain current degradation becomes severe enough, the device eventually fails to operate.

One refinement applies to Cit. Only those interface traps that can be filled and emptied at a rate faster than the capacitance measurement signal can contribute to it; traps too slow to follow the signal do not. Cit is therefore a function of three things: the interface trap density, the MOS capacitor gate voltage — which controls the surface potential and hence the probability of occupancy of the traps — and the frequency of the capacitance measurement. Measuring the same device at two frequencies gives two different answers, and that frequency dependence is how interface traps are distinguished experimentally from the other three charges.

To minimise the trap density the notes give two measures: use (100)-oriented silicon, and perform a post-anneal in hydrogen at the end of the fabrication process. The orientation matters, but not for the reason a bond count suggests. A (100) plane does carry the fewest silicon atoms per unit area of the common orientations — 6.8×10¹⁴ cm⁻² against 7.8×10¹⁴ for (111) — yet each atom on an ideal (100) surface carries two outward bonds where a (111) atom carries one, so (100) actually presents the larger density of bonds to leave unsatisfied. It is nonetheless the low-defect plane, by roughly a factor of three in both interface-trap density and fixed charge, and the ordering (111) > (110) > (100) tracks the density of the particular trivalent-silicon defect that survives oxidation rather than the density of bonds available beforehand. What matters is how many dangling bonds the oxidised interface is left with, which is a question about the chemistry and strain of that interface, not about arithmetic on the bare surface. It is why every wafer size in M04 is specified as (100).

6.5 Oxide trapped charge, Qot

Oxide trapped charge is due to holes or electrons trapped in the bulk of the oxide, and can therefore be either positive or negative — the only one of the four that is not of a definite sign. The carriers become trapped at trap centres, which are microscopic defects in the oxide structure within the bulk of the layer.

Those defects may be caused by ionising radiation, by hot-carrier injection, or by high currents through the oxide due to Fowler–Nordheim tunnelling, or by other reactions that bend or break Si–O bonds in the oxide. All of these are processes that deposit energy in the oxide network, and the damage they leave is a site capable of holding charge.

Qot arrives by two routes, and both matter. Three specific process steps introduce it during manufacture — ion implantation, sputter deposition and plasma etching — and what they have in common is energetic particles or ultraviolet photons striking the wafer. It is then generated again in service, by hot carriers, by tunnelling current and by ionising radiation. The process-induced part is largely removed by the back-end anneal described next; the operational part accumulates and cannot be.

Qot can be annealed out by heat treatment above 550 °C — low temperature by the standards of oxidation and implant activation, though well above the 400–450 °C alloy anneal that ends a process — and for that reason the notes rate it less important than the other oxide charges. The qualification matters, though: not all of it may be completely annealed out at the end of the fabrication process, and what remains may result in long-time reliability problems.

Where this connects to the radiation-effects course

Qot and Qit are the same two quantities that total ionising dose effects in EEE 5416 are written in terms of. The physics is identical; only the source of the energy differs. This course explains how processing puts charge into the oxide, and how the interface is passivated in the first place. The radiation course explains how ionising radiation puts far more charge there, and separates the two contributions by their different signatures in the measured threshold shift.

6.6 Mobile charge, Qm

Mobile charge consists of ionic impurities that entered the oxide during processing: sodium, potassium and lithium, present as Na⁺, K⁺ and Li⁺. They are distinguished from the other three components by one property — they move. Under an applied gate voltage they drift through the oxide, toward the gate under one polarity and toward the silicon under the other.

That mobility is what makes them intolerable. A charge whose position changes changes its influence on the channel: the same ion contributes a different amount to the threshold voltage depending on how close it currently is to the interface, so a device biased one way for a day has a different threshold from the same device biased the other way. The characteristics drift with the operating history, which no circuit design can compensate.

The sources are the chemicals of the process itself. Sodium, potassium and lithium are present as major impurities in water, in process chemicals and in solvents; ordinary water additionally carries iron, magnesium and zinc. Controlling Qm therefore means controlling the entire chemical supply: low-sodium reagents, high-purity potassium and lithium-free chemicals, and deionised water throughout — which is why the heavy-metal specification for a ULSI wafer in M04 reaches 0.001 ppb.

People are a source as well. Process engineers and technicians wear masks in a fabrication area, and the reason is not only particles: the human body is full of sodium, and breathing over a wafer transfers enough of it into a film to affect the device.

The historical consequence is on record. The professor gives it as p-channel MOSFET thresholds of six to eight volts, routine in the early 1970s and 1980s, with mobile charge as the principal cause. Two refinements are worth carrying alongside that. Thresholds of several volts belong to the metal-gate era, the mid-1960s to the early 1970s; by the 1980s silicon-gate NMOS and CMOS ran thresholds near 1 V from a 5 V rail. And the literature attributes the magnitude chiefly to fixed oxide charge, which was about three times larger on the (111) wafers universally used then, divided by the small Cox of a 100–150 nm oxide — that combination alone accounts for several volts. Mobile ions are held responsible for the instability: the drift of the threshold under bias and temperature, and the unpredictability that followed. That is the same distinction the paragraph above draws. Following the sign analysis of M03 §6, positive ionic charge drives the threshold of a p-channel device further negative, adding to three other terms that are already negative. Bringing the chemical supply under control removed several volts from that number, and it is one of the largest single improvements in the history of the technology.

Two nominally identical MOS capacitors are measured. One shows a threshold that shifts by 0.4 V after a week of positive gate bias at 150 °C and returns after the opposite bias; the other shows a threshold that shifts progressively during electrical stress and does not return. Which charge dominates in each?

The first is mobile charge, Qm. The signature is a shift that is reversible with bias polarity and accelerated by temperature, because the ions drift through the oxide and temperature raises their mobility. Bias–temperature stress is the standard test for exactly this reason.

The second is interface trapped charge, Qit, with a contribution from Qot. Electrical stress injects hot carriers, which break Si–H bonds and create new dangling bonds. The bonds do not re-form when the bias is removed, so the shift is progressive and permanent. Distinguishing the two in practice also uses the frequency dependence of section 6.4: Qit contributes a measurable Cit that varies with measurement frequency, while drifted ions do not.

Summary

  • SiO₂ serves ten roles in a process, from a 1.5 nm gate dielectric to a 400 nm field oxide — a thickness range of more than two orders of magnitude in the same material.
  • Thinning the gate oxide raises drain current through Cox, reduces gate capacitance through the reduced area, raises device density and reduces power. It also forces the supply voltage down, raises tunnelling leakage, admits boron penetration, amplifies the effect of defects, and permits charge injection.
  • Shortening the gate raises the subthreshold current through drain-induced barrier lowering. The remedies are a shallower extension junction and heavier channel doping; the second raises VT, which is counteracted by thinning the oxide.
  • Thickness uniformity is required in three independent senses: across the wafer, wafer to wafer within a batch of about twenty, and run to run.
  • Breakdown field is 8–10 MV/cm for a high-quality dry oxide. At 2 nm that caps the applied voltage at 2 V taking the top of the range, 1.6 V taking the bottom, and about 1.4 V with margin — which is a large part of why supply voltages fell. 8 MV/cm is a minimum acceptable breakdown strength, not an operating field; parts are run near 5 MV/cm, because time to breakdown falls about a decade per 0.3–0.5 MV/cm.
  • SiO₂ is a network of Si–O tetrahedra. Bridging oxygens join two tetrahedra; non-bridging oxygens interrupt the network, and a higher bridging fraction means a more cohesive film. Si–O is 1.62 Å, Si–Si 3.12 Å, and the Si–O–Si angle is most probably 144°. A 2 nm oxide is 10–12 atomic layers.
  • Qi = Qf + Qit + Qot + Qm. Qf and Qm are now well controlled; Qit and Qot are the reliability problem, because they are generated during operation.
  • Interface traps come from dangling bonds at the terminated lattice — trivalent silicon. A (100) surface has 6.8×10¹⁴ atoms/cm²; leaving one bond in a thousand dangling gives 6.8×10¹¹ cm⁻² and shifts VT by 0.63 V through a 20 nm oxide. (100) is the low-defect plane empirically, by about a factor of three, not because it offers fewer bonds — it offers more.
  • A hydrogen or forming-gas anneal passivates the remaining bonds, but Si–H is broken easily by hot electrons, so interface traps are generated throughout the operating life. The ideal gate dielectric contains as little hydrogen as possible.
  • ΔVT = −Qox/Cox, with Qox weighted by how far each charge sits from the gate. Traps also cause leakage by generation–recombination, add a frequency-dependent Cit in parallel with the depletion capacitance, and weaken the oxide against second-stage breakdown.
  • Qot is positive or negative, arrives both from processing (implantation, sputtering, plasma etching) and from operation (hot carriers, tunnelling, radiation), and the process-induced part anneals out above 550 °C. Qm is Na⁺, K⁺ and Li⁺ from water, chemicals, solvents and people; the professor credits it with the six-to-eight-volt p-channel thresholds of the early era, while the literature credits fixed charge on (111) silicon with the magnitude and mobile ions with the drift.
