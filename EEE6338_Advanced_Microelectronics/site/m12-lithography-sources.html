Advanced Lithography II: Sources and Tricks

The hardware that prints below the wavelength: the KrF, ArF and F₂ excimer lasers and what their pulsed, narrow-line output demands of the exposure tool; the lens materials that survive deep-ultraviolet light; the resolution-enhancement techniques — off-axis illumination, optical proximity correction and phase-shift masks — that lowered k1; the limits of optical lithography and the water immersion that moved them; and the non-optical candidates the notes list: X-ray proximity printing, extreme-ultraviolet reflective projection, electron-beam direct writing and electron-beam projection (SCALPEL).

Prerequisites M11 Read time ~50 min Sources Class notes pp. 96–105 (Chapter 7); Lectures 19–20; Wolf Vol. 4 Ch. 7; Plummer, Deal & Griffin §5.2.4, §5.6

M11 set the two limits every projection tool works against: the feature size F = 0.5λ/NA and the depth of focus DOF = 0.6λ/NA² of eq. (11.2), with λ the exposure wavelength and NA the numerical aperture, the sine of the half-angle of the cone of light the lens delivers to the wafer. M11 also introduced the general form, R = k1λ/NA, in which the factor k1 collects everything other than wavelength and aperture: the resist, the illumination and the mask. That form leaves three levers on the resolution R, and this module, Chapter 7 of the notes (pp. 96–105), takes them in turn. The wavelength is set by the source, so §1 describes the excimer lasers that produce 248, 193 and 157 nm light, and §2 the lens materials that transmit it. The factor k1 is lowered by tricks of illumination and mask design, §§3–4, of which the notes treat the phase-shift mask in detail. The aperture is limited by geometry, which §5 shows was the limit that water immersion moved. When all three are exhausted, the remaining routes leave light behind: X-rays, extreme ultraviolet and electrons, §§6–9. Lecture 20 covered X-ray proximity printing, electron-beam writing and ion beams from outside the notes, and §10 collects the two calculations of these lectures.

1 · Excimer lasers: the deep-UV source

A mercury arc lamp, the source of the g-line (436 nm) and i-line (365 nm), emits only weakly in the deep ultraviolet (DUV) and radiates in all directions. A laser emits a collimated beam, so it delivers far more usable power into the narrow cone an illuminator accepts: Wolf puts its radiance several orders of magnitude above the arc lamp's (p. 261). The lasers that emit at 248, 193 and 157 nm are excimer lasers, and the notes open Chapter 7 with them (p. 96).

1.1 What an excimer is, and why it lases

An excimer ("excited dimer") is a molecule of one noble-gas atom and one halogen atom, krypton and fluorine in KrF, that is bound only while one of its atoms is electronically excited (Wolf pp. 259–260). Ground-state Kr and F do not react. In the laser, a capacitor charged to about 12 kV discharges in about 75 ns through a high-pressure mixture of the gases; the discharge produces excited and ionized atoms, which combine into KrF* (the asterisk marks the excited state). KrF* returns to the ground state by emitting a 248 nm photon, and the ground-state molecule, being unbound, dissociates at once into Kr and F. The lower level of the laser transition therefore never accumulates molecules. Wolf's reason for the ease of the population inversion a laser needs, more molecules in the upper state than in the lower, is the other half of the picture: the excimer's spontaneous lifetime is relatively long, nanoseconds to microseconds, so the discharge can build up the excited population (p. 260). A photon emitted by one KrF* stimulates the decay of others; mirrors at the ends of the cavity return the light through the gas, and the stimulated emission builds until almost all the excimers have decayed. The result is a short pulse, after which the capacitor must recharge before the next. Replacing krypton by argon gives ArF* and 193 nm; the F₂ laser emits at 157 nm. Wolf notes that a KrF laser becomes an ArF laser by changing only the gas and the cavity mirrors (p. 259).

The notes assign each laser to its technology generations: KrF to 0.35, 0.25 and 0.18 µm CMOS, ArF to 0.13 and 0.1 µm, and F₂ to below 0.1 µm (p. 96, where "0.13, 0.1 nm" and "below 0.1 nm" are slips for micrometres). Wolf gives the KrF nodes in micrometres and, in 2001, forecast ArF between 130 and 100 nm and F₂ below 100 nm (p. 259).

1.2 Pulsed output: power, dose and servicing

A lithographic KrF laser delivers 2–20 W of average power as pulses at 200–2500 Hz, each 5–20 ns long (notes p. 96) and carrying about 10 mJ (Wolf p. 261). Because the energy arrives in short pulses separated by long gaps, the power within a pulse is far above the average, and that peak power can damage optical coatings and materials where the beam is concentrated, as in the stepper lens (Wolf p. 261). The exposure dose is controlled by counting energy: the tool integrates the measured pulse energies and stops the laser when the required dose is reached (Wolf p. 263). The rule in the notes and Wolf is that at least about 100 pulses are needed for 1 % dose control (p. 96; Wolf p. 263). One reading of it: a dose assembled from N pulses can overshoot by up to one pulse, a relative error of 1/N, which is 1 % at N = 100. Wolf also ties dose control to the uniformity of the pulse energies (pp. 261, 265).

Peak power. 10 × 10⁻³ J / 10 × 10⁻⁹ s = 1.0 × 10⁶ W = 1.0 MW. The laser emits for 2000 × 10 ns = 2 × 10⁻⁵ of each second, so the peak is 5 × 10⁴ times the average.

20 W average, 1.0 MW peak, 50 ms per field at 2000 Hz (100 ms at 1000 Hz, 25 ms at 4000 Hz). Doubling the repetition rate halves the exposure time, as Wolf records for the step from 1000 to 2000 Hz (p. 263). Check: a 5 ns pulse doubles the peak to 2.0 MW and a 20 ns pulse halves it to 0.50 MW, which is why lengthening the pulse is the ArF designer's remedy for optical damage (§1.4).

The gas is costly and toxic, and the discharge cavity is not sealed, so it is refilled about every 5 days (notes p. 96; Wolf pp. 263–264). The pulses also sputter material onto the cavity's mirrors and windows. Wolf's 1998 figures, which the notes repeat, are cleaning after 10⁹ pulses and cavity replacement after 2.5 × 10⁹, the largest cost of operating the laser. In production a laser fires about 2 × 10⁹ pulses a year (Wolf p. 264).

How often is a production KrF laser cleaned, and how often is its cavity replaced, by Wolf's 1998 figures? At 2000 Hz, for what fraction of the year is the laser actually firing?

Cleaning: 10⁹ pulses / (2 × 10⁹ pulses per year) = 0.5 year, every 6 months. Replacement: 2.5 × 10⁹ / 2 × 10⁹ = 1.25 years, about 15 months; Wolf rounds to "about once a year". Firing time: 2 × 10⁹ / 2000 s⁻¹ = 1.0 × 10⁶ s = 11.6 days, 3.2 % of a year. The laser spends most of its life waiting for wafers to move, align and step, so the service interval is set by pulse count, not calendar time.

1.3 Line narrowing: why the bandwidth must be below a picometre

A refractive lens bends each wavelength by a different amount, because the refractive index of its material changes with wavelength (dispersion), and the change is large in the DUV. A lens made of one material cannot correct for it, and a lens whose focal length depends on wavelength focuses a broad-band source at a range of depths: chromatic aberration. The free-running 248 nm laser has a spectral width of about 300 pm (0.3 nm), and the uncorrected DUV stepper lens requires less than 1 pm (Wolf pp. 261–262). Prisms and gratings inside the cavity narrow the line, at some cost in power and stability, and a feedback loop holds the centre wavelength to a similar accuracy, because for each 1 pm the wavelength drifts, the focus of the lens moves by about 0.15 µm (Wolf p. 262).

What a picometre of bandwidth costs in focus

Given a focus shift of 0.15 µm per pm of wavelength (Wolf p. 262); a KrF stepper with NA = 0.6. Find the spread of focus for a 300 pm free-running line, a 1 pm line and the 0.5 pm line of a 2000 laser, and compare each with the course's depth of focus.

Plan. The spread of focal positions is the bandwidth times 0.15 µm/pm; the depth of focus is eq. (11.2).

Depth of focus. NA² = 0.36; DOF = 0.6 × 0.248 µm / 0.36 = 0.413 µm, that is ±0.207 µm.

Free-running line. 300 pm × 0.15 µm/pm = 45 µm, a hundred times the depth of focus.

Without narrowing, the image would be smeared over 45 µm of depth; at 1 pm the bandwidth alone consumes about a third of the focus budget, which is why Wolf records a move to below 0.5 pm, and below 0.4 pm for the generation after 180 nm (p. 262).

1.4 ArF and F₂ lasers

The ArF laser must deliver more power at a higher repetition rate with a narrower line; the notes give 20 W average at 4 kHz (p. 97), the figures of Cymer's 2002 Nanolith-7000 (Wolf p. 265). That is 20 W / 4000 s⁻¹ = 5 mJ per pulse. Optical materials degrade faster under 193 nm light, and the degradation is smaller when the peak power is lower, so the pulse is lengthened to spread the same energy over a longer time (notes p. 97; Wolf p. 265).

The F₂ laser emits two lines, at 157.63 nm and 157.52 nm, with 87 % of the energy in the longer one (notes p. 97; Wolf p. 265). Lecture 19 gave the split the other way round; the notes' version is used here. The two lines are 0.11 nm = 110 pm apart, 550 times the 0.2 pm bandwidth Wolf expects a 157 nm lens to need, so a refractive tool cannot accept both: the 1999 prototype Wolf describes was run on the 157.63 nm line alone, the line that carries 87 % of the energy (p. 265).

An ArF laser delivers 20 W at 4 kHz. What is the energy per pulse? If the pulse is stretched from 10 ns to 20 ns, what happens to the peak power and to the average power, and why does the designer want the first change?

20 W / 4000 s⁻¹ = 5.0 mJ per pulse. Peak power: 5 mJ / 10 ns = 0.50 MW, and 5 mJ / 20 ns = 0.25 MW, halved. The average power is unchanged at 20 W, because the energy per pulse and the pulse rate are unchanged, so the exposure time is unchanged. The lens degrades less at the lower peak power (notes p. 97; Wolf p. 265), so the stretched pulse buys lens lifetime at no cost in throughput.

2 · Lens and mask materials for the deep UV

A refractive element must transmit the exposure light, keep its shape and index under intense pulses, and refract the same amount in every direction. Ordinary optical glass fails the first below about 350 nm, where it begins to absorb (Lecture 19; Wolf Fig. 7-8). Fused silica, amorphous SiO₂ of high purity, transmits through 248 nm and 193 nm, and the notes call it the "quartz optics" of 248 and 193 nm tools (p. 97). Crystalline quartz is not used for lenses: a crystal's refractive index can depend on the direction of propagation and polarization, birefringence, and quartz has it, while the amorphous fused silica has very little (Wolf p. 271).

Two problems appear at shorter wavelengths. Under intense excimer light fused silica compacts, becoming denser and more absorbing, which degrades the image and the light reaching the wafer; at 193 nm the compaction is severe enough that 193 nm lenses use at least some calcium fluoride (CaF₂) elements, placed where the intensity is highest (Wolf p. 270). And at 157 nm fused silica absorbs too strongly for lenses, and CaF₂ is "virtually the only" transmissive material (Wolf p. 270; notes p. 97). CaF₂ is a single crystal, which does not compact, but it must be extremely pure and homogeneous to resist laser damage, and it is hygroscopic, soft and prone to chipping, so a CaF₂ element takes 50–100 % longer to make than a glass one (notes p. 97; Wolf p. 271). In May 2001 crystalline CaF₂ was found to have an intrinsic birefringence at 157 nm, a further burden on lens design (Wolf p. 271). Lecture 19 added other fluorides used as DUV optics: MgF₂, AlF₃, LaF₃ and LiF. At 157 nm the whole optical path is purged with nitrogen (notes p. 97; Wolf p. 269); the notes give the reason in the resist chapter, that at 157 nm even a monolayer of oxygen or water on the lens changes its transmission (p. 93; M11 §6). A 157 nm scanner was estimated at $12–20 M (notes p. 97; Wolf p. 269). Its optics were expected to be catadioptric, combining mirrors with lenses, because such designs are about a third the size and weight of an all-refractive one and need fewer and smaller elements (Wolf p. 269); at 193 nm, SVG's catadioptric scanners needed 1.7 kg of CaF₂ where a refractive system needed 48 kg for its chromatic correction (Wolf p. 268).

Masks have their own constraint. The reticle is written by an electron beam that heats it, and a pattern placed on a substrate that expands with temperature is placed in the wrong position. Fused silica expands 0.5 ppm/K and CaF₂ 19 ppm/K, which is one reason Wolf concluded that 157 nm would be the shortest optical wavelength: below it fused silica stops transmitting and a CaF₂ reticle would be needed (pp. 298–299). At 157 nm itself the reticle can still be fused silica, made with little OH and some fluorine to raise its transmission to 82 % (Wolf p. 272).

A 193 nm projection lens built entirely of fused silica performs well when new and degrades over months of use. Name the mechanism, say which elements suffer first, and give the material change that addresses it. Why can the same remedy not be applied to the reticle at 126 nm?

Compaction: under intense excimer pulses fused silica densifies, which changes its index and raises its absorption, so the image degrades and less light reaches the wafer. The elements where the intensity is highest suffer first, the illuminator and parts of the projection optics, and those are made of single-crystal CaF₂, which does not compact (Wolf p. 270). At 126 nm the reticle itself must be CaF₂, because fused silica stops transmitting a few nanometres below 157 nm, and a CaF₂ reticle expands 38 times more than fused silica when the mask writer heats it, so its patterns are misplaced (Wolf pp. 298–299).

3 · Lowering k1: off-axis illumination and proximity correction

In R = k1λ/NA (Wolf eq. 7.1), k1 is the measure of how aggressive a process is. Without help, a production process needed k1 above about 0.75, because the image degraded unacceptably below it; the resolution-enhancement techniques (RETs) — anti-reflective coatings and top-surface imaging (M11), off-axis illumination, optical proximity correction and phase-shift masks — brought it toward 0.35, halving the printable feature at the same wavelength and aperture (Wolf pp. 274–275). The notes treat only the phase-shift mask (§4); the other two are taken from Wolf, who presents the three together as the resolution-enhancement techniques of the period.

3.1 Off-axis illumination

A grating of lines and spaces with pitch p (one line plus one space) diffracts the light passing through it into discrete beams, the diffraction orders. The undiffracted zero order continues along the direction of the illumination, and the first orders, m = ±1, leave at angles given by sin θ1 = λ/p (Wolf p. 276). The zero order alone carries no information about the pattern, only its average brightness; an image of the lines forms only if the lens also collects at least one first order, which interferes with the zero order to recreate the periodic intensity. With the illumination along the axis, the zero order enters the centre of the lens pupil and the two first orders lie at ±λ/p in units of sin θ; both fall inside the pupil, whose radius is NA, only if λ/p ≤ NA. The smallest pitch is λ/NA, a half-pitch of 0.5λ/NA: the course's feature size, k1 = 0.5 (Wolf p. 276).

Off-axis illumination (OAI) tilts the illuminating beam so that the zero order enters the pupil near one edge. Then one first order can enter near the opposite edge while the other falls outside, and two-beam imaging forms the pattern from a pitch at which on-axis illumination collects neither first order. In the limit the zero and first orders sit at opposite edges of the pupil, 2NA apart, and the smallest pitch halves to λ/(2NA) (Wolf pp. 276–277). The method was introduced to lithography by Mack and by Fehrs in 1989 and built into Canon and Nikon steppers in 1992 (Wolf p. 275).

Which gratings a 248 nm, NA 0.6 lens can image, on axis and off axis

Given λ = 248 nm, NA = 0.6; a grating of pitch 300 nm. Find the smallest pitch imaged with on-axis illumination and in the off-axis limit; whether the 300 nm grating images on axis; and the tilt that puts its zero and first orders symmetrically about the pupil centre.

Plan. Work in units of sin θ, in which the pupil is a circle of radius NA and the orders are spaced λ/p.

On-axis limit. pmin = λ/NA = 248/0.6 = 413 nm; half-pitch 207 nm, k1 = 207 × 0.6/248 = 0.50.

Off-axis limit. pmin = λ/(2NA) = 207 nm; half-pitch 103 nm, k1 = 0.25.

The 300 nm grating on axis. sin θ1 = 248/300 = 0.827 > 0.6: both first orders miss the pupil and only the uniform zero order arrives. No image.

Tilt. Place the zero order at −0.827/2 = −0.413; the +1 order is then at −0.413 + 0.827 = +0.413. Both lie inside ±0.6. On the wafer side the tilt is sin⁻¹ 0.413 = 24.4°; at a 4× reticle the sines are four times smaller, sin⁻¹ (0.413/4) = 5.9°.

On axis the lens images pitches down to 413 nm (k1 = 0.5); off axis, down to 207 nm in the limit (k1 = 0.25). The 300 nm grating, invisible on axis, images with the tilted illumination. Check: the two orders at ±0.413 are the same distance from the pupil centre, which is the condition for the depth-of-focus gain described next.

The larger benefit is in depth of focus. Defocus delays each diffraction order by a phase that grows with the square of its distance from the pupil centre, and the image degrades because the orders arrive out of step. When the zero and first orders are equidistant from the centre, as in the example, they acquire the same phase error, their relative phase is unchanged, and the image survives a larger defocus (Wolf pp. 277–278). The gains come with conditions. A setting is tuned to one pitch; an isolated line produces a continuous spread of diffracted light rather than discrete orders, so it gains little, and a field with both dense and isolated lines prints them with different widths; one first order is discarded, so exposure takes longer; and a single tilted beam images asymmetrically, so real illuminators use symmetric apertures — two poles (dipole) for lines in one direction, four (quadrupole) for horizontal and vertical lines, or a ring (annular) for all orientations with smaller gains (Wolf pp. 278–279).

3.2 Optical proximity correction

When features approach the wavelength, they no longer image independently: light diffracted from one feature adds to or subtracts from the intensity at its neighbours, so the printed size and shape of a feature depend on what surrounds it, the optical proximity effect (Wolf pp. 279–281). Three consequences are common. An isolated line prints at a different width from the same line in a dense group — with positive resist, 0.35 µm isolated lines printed 0.09 µm wider than closely spaced ones (Wolf p. 281). The ends of lines pull back (line shortening). Corners round, so a square contact hole near the resolution limit prints as a circle (Wolf p. 282).

Optical proximity correction (OPC) pre-distorts the mask so the printed feature comes out as designed. Line widths are biased individually according to their surroundings; small squares called serifs are added at corners and line ends to put back the light that the lens rounds off; line jogs change a line's width along its run; and scattering bars, lines too narrow to print, are placed beside an isolated line at the pitch of the dense lines, so the lens images the isolated line as if it were in a dense group (Wolf pp. 280–283). The corrections are chosen by look-up rules (fast, minutes to hours per mask level) or by simulating the image of every feature in its surroundings (model-based, hours to days). OPC has costs: the extra features multiply the mask data and writing time; serifs are smaller than the mask's minimum feature, which strains mask inspection; and they cannot be added where features are already at minimum spacing. It corrects distortion and does not improve the resolution itself (Wolf pp. 283–284).

A 248 nm, NA 0.6 tool must print a memory array of 120 nm lines and spaces and, in the periphery, isolated 120 nm lines. Can on-axis illumination print the array? What illumination would you choose, and what must be added to the mask for the isolated lines, and why?

The array's pitch is 240 nm; on axis the first orders lie at ±λ/p = ±248/240 = ±1.03 in pupil units, outside the pupil radius 0.6, so the array does not image. Off-axis illumination can, because 240 nm exceeds the off-axis limit λ/(2NA) = 207 nm: with the zero order at −0.517 the first order lands at +0.517, inside 0.6. Because the lines run in one direction, a dipole oriented across them gives the largest gain. The isolated lines produce no discrete orders and gain little from the tilt, and they will print at a different width from the array lines (iso-dense bias); scattering bars at the 240 nm pitch beside them make them image like dense lines, and a per-line width bias removes the remaining difference (Wolf pp. 278, 281–283).

4 · Phase-shift masks

Every mask so far is binary: chromium blocks the light and clear glass passes it, so the field just below the mask has amplitude 0 or 1. The notes take up a different idea, credited to Levenson and co-workers in 1982 (p. 99; M. D. Levenson, N. S. Viswanathan and R. A. Simpson, IEEE Trans. Electron Devices ED-29, 1828 (1982)): control the phase of the light leaving each opening as well as whether it is transmitted. Lecture 19 attributed the work to Swedish scientists; Levenson's group was at IBM.

4.1 How a 180° shift sharpens the image

The lens transmits only the low spatial frequencies of the mask, so the field at the wafer below each opening is a broadened version of the opening, spreading into the chromium-covered regions beside it. For two openings close to the resolution limit, the spread fields overlap under the chromium between them. With a binary mask both fields have the same sign, they add there, and the resist, which responds to the intensity, the square of the field, receives a bump of light where it should be dark: the two lines are barely resolved (notes p. 99). If the light through one opening is shifted by 180°, its field has the opposite sign. The two fields then cancel midway between the openings, the field passes through zero, and so does the intensity, however much the fields overlap. The dark gap between the lines is restored by destructive interference (notes p. 101; Wolf p. 287).

For a periodic grating the effect can be stated through the period. A phase shifter on every other opening makes the field alternate +1, −1, +1, −1, so the field pattern repeats only every two periods of the grating; its fundamental spatial frequency halves, and a lens that could not collect the first order of the binary grating can collect this one. The intensity, the square of the field, still repeats with the grating's own period.

The notes' "half the period" is the inverse

The notes (p. 101), copying Plummer §5.2.4, and Lecture 20 say that "the period of this added pattern is half the period of the original grating." A shifter on every other opening repeats every two openings, so the shifter pattern and the field it produces have twice the grating's period; it is their spatial frequency that is halved, which is why the lens can collect it. Read the sentence as "half the spatial frequency".

4.2 The shifter thickness

The 180° shift is made by an extra transparent layer of refractive index n and thickness d over alternate openings. Light crossing the layer travels the optical path nd, against d through air beside it, so it is delayed by an optical path difference (n − 1)d. A 180° phase shift is a delay of half a wavelength, (n − 1)d = λ/2, which gives the notes' equation (p. 99; Plummer eq. 5.16; Wolf p. 285):

d = λ2(n − 1)

4.3 The notes' example: 0.3 µm to 0.18 µm

The notes, Lecture 20 and Wolf give the same case: a process that resolves 0.3 µm with 248 nm KrF light at NA 0.6 can resolve 0.18 µm with a phase-shift mask (notes p. 101; Wolf p. 285). Plummer, whose sentence the notes copy (notes p. 101), gives the gain two uses: a better aerial image, or a longer depth of focus at constant resolution, obtained by printing the same feature with a lower-NA system, which by eq. (11.2) gains depth of focus as 1/NA² (Plummer §5.2.4).

The PSM example as a change of k1

Given λ = 0.248 µm, NA = 0.6; resolution 0.3 µm with a binary mask and 0.18 µm with a phase-shift mask (notes p. 101). Find k1 before and after, and the fractional improvement.

Plan. Invert R = k1λ/NA: k1 = R NA/λ.

Binary. k1 = 0.3 × 0.6/0.248 = 0.726.

4.4 Kinds of phase-shift mask, and their limits

The mask of §4.1, with shifters on alternate openings, is the alternating PSM (ALT-PSM, or Levenson PSM). It gives the largest gain, but only where every opening can be given the opposite phase to all its neighbours (Wolf pp. 286–288). That fails for a pattern of offset rows, like bricks in a wall, where each opening has neighbours in two rows that cannot all differ from it; isolated features, which have no neighbour to interfere with, gain nothing; and where a shifted clear region meets an unshifted clear region the two phases cancel along the boundary and print an unwanted dark line, which needs an intermediate 90° step or a negative resist to suppress (Wolf pp. 287–290). These are the "arbitrary mask shapes" whose complexity the notes cite as the method's cost (p. 101).

Two weaker forms trade resolution for applicability (Wolf pp. 288–291). The attenuated (halftone) PSM replaces the opaque chromium with a film that transmits about 10 % of the light, shifted by 180°, too weak to expose the resist but enough to cancel the light spreading from the openings at the feature edges; for i-line, films of chromium oxynitride or molybdenum silicon oxynitride are used. It works for any layout, including isolated contacts, is made and repaired like a binary mask, and was the first PSM in commercial production. The rim-shift PSM shifts only a rim around each opening. Wolf groups them by what they deliver: strong PSMs (alternating) give up to 40 % in resolution and less in depth of focus; weak PSMs (attenuated, rim) give less resolution and more depth of focus. By 2000, attenuated PSMs were used on 0.18 µm contact levels and alternating PSMs on gates, interconnect and memory arrays; in 1997 only about 1 % of masks carried any phase shift. Wolf singles out attenuated PSMs combined with off-axis illumination as especially effective, because each alone removes the zero-order light only partly (pp. 291–292).

5 · Extending optical lithography

In the late 1970s optical lithography was expected to end near 1 µm (Wolf p. 296). Wolf lists why it outlived that forecast (p. 297): lenses became diffraction limited and reached NA 0.8 by 2001; the wavelength fell from 436 nm to 193 nm; resists lowered k1 from about 0.8 in 1980 to below 0.5; chemical-mechanical polishing (M13) flattened the wafer, so the depth of focus needed fell to about ±0.2 µm; and the RETs of §§3–4 were developed. The notes (pp. 101–102) take two items from Wolf's list of what remains: reaching 70 nm needs shorter wavelengths, 157 nm (F₂) or 126 nm (the argon excimer Ar₂*), with CaF₂ single crystals for the mask (§2); and CMP improves the depth of focus to ±0.2 µm. They conclude that the lower limit of optical lithography is around 70–50 nm (p. 102). Wolf adds two limits the notes do not copy: CMP cannot flatten the wafer further, and NA cannot exceed 1 (pp. 297–299).

5.1 Immersion: the limit that moved

Wolf's argument on aperture rested on an assumption: "it is unlikely that lithography will be carried out in a medium other than a vacuum or gas", which "limits the NA of a lens to < 1" (p. 299). The numerical aperture is in general NA = n sin θ (M11 §3.1), where on the wafer side n is the refractive index of the medium between the last lens element and the resist. Filling that gap with water, whose index at 193 nm is about 1.44 (J. H. Burnett and S. G. Kaplan, J. Microlith. Microfab. Microsyst. 3, 68 (2004)), raises the ceiling on NA from 1 to 1.44. Equivalently, the light travels through the water with wavelength λ/n = 193/1.44 = 134 nm. Lecture 19 raised immersion with ArF lasers, as a means of better resolution and as a project topic; it is not in the notes. In 2004 Lin argued for water immersion at 193 nm as the successor to dry 193 nm in place of the 157 nm generation for which §§1–2's CaF₂ optics were being developed (B. J. Lin, J. Microlith. Microfab. Microsyst. 3, 377 (2004)). ArF immersion scanners now use NA 1.35 and print 38–40 nm in production (ASML, TWINSCAN NXT:1980Di product data), well below the 70 nm the notes give as the limit.

6 · Beyond light: the non-optical candidates

Below the optical limit the notes list four approaches (p. 102): extreme-ultraviolet (EUV) reflective projection, electron-beam projection, X-ray proximity printing and electron-beam direct writing. Lecture 20 took them in a different order — X-ray, EUV, electron beam, ion beam — and added ion beams, which the notes do not mention. Each replaces the photon of a few electronvolts by radiation of far shorter wavelength: X-rays of order 1 nm, EUV of 10–15 nm, and electrons whose wavelength is a small fraction of an ångström (§9). For X-rays and electrons diffraction then hardly limits the resolution; EUV remains a diffraction-limited projection system, at a wavelength 14 times shorter than ArF's. In all of them the problems move to the source, the mask and the optics. The notes and Wolf name two common to the mask-based methods (notes p. 102; Wolf p. 301). Masks are expensive: the notes give $5000–100,000 per mask, and Wolf's printed figure, "$50,00–$100,000", is itself misprinted, so the lower bound is uncertain. And no pellicle, the thin transparent film stretched over an optical mask to hold particles out of focus, can be mounted, which leaves the masks far more vulnerable to particles; for EUV, Wolf gives the reason, that no material transmits the radiation (pp. 303–304). By 2001, Wolf records, EUV had become the leading candidate, X-ray proximity printing and direct-write electron beams had been abandoned as production methods, and electron-beam projection was in decline (p. 301).

7 · X-ray proximity lithography

Lecture 20 opened the non-optical methods with X-rays, from outside the notes. Lecture 20 gave wavelengths of 4–20 Å, photon energies of 0.6–3.1 keV; Plummer gives energies of 1–10 keV and wavelengths of order 1 nm (§5.6.2). No lens or ordinary mirror focuses them, because every material has a refractive index within a small fraction of 1 at these wavelengths, so the systems print by proximity: the mask is held a small gap above the wafer and its shadow is printed at 1:1, as in the shadow printing of M11 §2 (Lecture 20; Plummer). Because the wavelength is some 400 times shorter than the g-line's, the diffraction blur of eq. (11.1) is small even across a gap of micrometres.

The mask must stop X-rays where the pattern is dark and pass them elsewhere, and no material passes them easily. The clear areas are therefore a membrane a few micrometres thick of a light material — silicon or silicon nitride (Lecture 20), or Si, Si₃N₄, SiC or BN (Plummer) — and the absorber is a heavy metal, gold or platinum (Lecture 20), with tungsten preferred where gold contamination of the fab is a concern (Plummer). The membrane is fragile and its stresses distort the pattern, and because the mask is 1:1, its features are as small as the wafer's, written by an electron beam (Plummer). The resist named in Lecture 20 was PMMA (poly(methyl methacrylate)), which the lecture called a negative resist; At the doses used for patterning, PMMA under X-ray and electron exposure is a positive resist, its chains cut by the radiation so that the exposed areas dissolve (at much higher doses it cross-links and turns negative). Weak sources, such as medical X-ray tubes, need impractically long exposures; the intense source is a synchrotron, a storage ring in which electrons circulating at high energy emit X-rays at every bending magnet, so one ring can feed several aligners, at a very high cost (Plummer §5.6.2); Lecture 20 named the synchrotrons at Berkeley, Brookhaven and Argonne. That cost is the reason Lecture 20 gave for the move to EUV.

8 · Extreme-ultraviolet reflective projection lithography

EUV uses photons of 10–15 nm, the soft-X-ray end of the spectrum (notes p. 102), with energies near 100 eV (Wolf p. 301); Lecture 20 gave 13.5 nm as the wavelength used. Its advantage, the notes say, is that it "needs few changes in manufacturing from the current methods": it is still a projection system that reduces a larger mask onto the wafer, which makes the mask easier to make than a 1:1 X-ray mask (notes p. 102; Wolf p. 302). The difference is that nothing transmits EUV, so every element, the mask included, is a mirror, and the whole beam path is in vacuum (Wolf pp. 303–304).

8.1 The multilayer mirror

A single surface reflects EUV poorly, because below about 50 nm all materials have refractive indices close to 1 and an interface between two of them reflects very little (Wolf p. 304). The mirror is therefore built from many interfaces: about 40 pairs of alternating layers, each tens of ångströms thick, of a high-atomic-number (high-Z) and a low-Z material, molybdenum and silicon (notes p. 103; Wolf p. 304). The small index step at each Mo/Si interface reflects a little of the wave. If the period d of the stack satisfies the Bragg condition, the waves reflected from successive interfaces leave in phase and add; with enough pairs the sum reaches the notes' "as high as 70 %" (p. 103). Plummer describes the same structure as a scatterer (the heavy layer) and a spacer (the light one) (§5.6.2). Both layers must absorb weakly so the light reaches the deeper pairs, and each must be deposited to better than an ångström (Wolf p. 305). The Bragg condition, as Wolf writes it (p. 304):

8.3 The mask and the source

The EUV mask is a mirror carrying the pattern: the Mo/Si multilayer is deposited on a flat substrate and an absorber is deposited and patterned on top. The notes and Wolf name Cr, TiN and TaN as absorbers (notes p. 103; Wolf pp. 305–306); Lecture 20 added gold and platinum and said the mask stack uses fewer pairs than the mirrors. Mo and Si begin to intermix above 150 °C, which lowers the reflectivity, so every step that deposits or etches the absorber must stay below that temperature (notes p. 103; Wolf p. 306). Because nearly every material absorbs EUV, the resist must also be thin, below 200 nm (Wolf p. 306).

The notes' source is a laser-produced plasma: pulses from a Nd:YAG laser, wavelength 1.06 µm (Lecture 20), strike a supersonic jet of xenon, and the hot plasma emits broad-band radiation with a significant EUV component (notes pp. 102–103; Wolf p. 303). Xenon was the most common target because it emits relatively strong EUV and, being inert, does not directly generate debris that would contaminate the optics (Wolf p. 303). The 2001 prototype converted 1500 W of laser power into a 10 W EUV beam (notes pp. 103–104). EUV has since entered high-volume manufacturing at 13.5 nm, the wavelength Lecture 20 gave — TSMC reported volume shipments from its N7+ process in 2019 — with a different source from the notes': a CO₂ laser strikes droplets of tin. In 2017 such a source delivered 210 W of dose-controlled EUV, against the more than 200 W production needs (I. Fomenkov et al., Adv. Opt. Technol. 6, 173 (2017)). Lecture 20 mentioned tin as another group's approach.

9 · Electron beams

Lecture 20 distinguished two kinds of electron-beam lithography: projection through a mask (the notes' SCALPEL, §9.4) and the beam writer, which draws the pattern directly with a focused beam and is "the one mostly used for making masks". Plummer adds that the beam writer has long been the tool of advanced device research for its resolution (§5.6.1).

9.2 What limits the beam writer

If diffraction does not limit the electron beam, scattering does. Lecture 20 described the beam broadening as it scatters in the resist. Plummer gives the scale: electrons of 10–20 keV travel more than a micrometre in the resist and substrate before stopping, scattering off nuclei and losing energy to the electrons of the solid (the same stopping processes as in ion implantation), so regions beside the intended pattern are also exposed, the proximity effect (§5.6.1). Because the mask pattern is known, the dose can be adjusted point by point to compensate. The second limit is speed. A beam writer exposes the pattern one point at a time, where an optical stepper exposes a whole field at once, and each point needs a minimum number of electrons set by the resist's sensitivity; Plummer quotes about one wafer per hour against more than 50 for an optical stepper. That is why, as Lecture 20 put it, the beam writer is used to make masks and not wafers: a mask is written once and printed many thousands of times. Plummer adds research devices and small runs of special chips, for which skipping the mask saves time. Ionizing radiation in the resist also reaches the device below; electron-beam and X-ray exposure are among the process steps that charge the gate oxide (EEE 5416 M10 §1).

9.3 Raster and vector scanning

A beam writer can visit the pattern in two ways (Lecture 20). In raster scanning the beam sweeps every row of the field in turn, like the electron beam of a cathode-ray television tube, and is switched off (blanked) wherever there is no pattern. In vector scanning the beam is deflected directly from one pattern element to the next and writes only those, skipping the empty areas. The time a raster scan takes is set by the area of the field, whatever the pattern; the time a vector scan takes is set by the area of the pattern plus a settling time for each jump. Raster scanning therefore suits dense patterns, and vector scanning sparse ones. Lecture 20 gave the raster case as effective when about 80 % of the field is to be written and "a waste of time" when the pattern density is low, and vector scanning as more efficient when the pattern is sparse; one later sentence of the captions reverses this and is taken to be garbled. Vector writing's advantage at low density and raster writing's at high density are the standard description of beam writers.

Ignoring settling time and taking the same dwell per written point, how much longer does a raster scan take than a vector scan when the pattern covers 80 % of the field? When it covers 5 %? Which effect, left out here, narrows the second ratio in practice?

The raster visits every point of the field, the vector only the pattern's, so the ratio of times is 1/(pattern fraction): 1/0.8 = 1.25 at 80 %, so raster is nearly as fast and the simpler choice, and 1/0.05 = 20 at 5 %, where raster wastes 95 % of its time on blanked points. The vector beam must settle after every jump between elements, and a pattern of many small elements adds many such settling times, so the real advantage of vector scanning at low density is less than 20.

9.4 Electron-beam projection: SCALPEL

SCALPEL (Scattering with Angular Limitation in Projection Electron-beam Lithography), invented at Bell Laboratories in 1989 (Plummer), was the notes' answer to the beam writer's slowness: it projects a whole mask pattern at once, with 4:1 reduction (notes pp. 104–105; Wolf pp. 306–308). Its mask is a thin silicon nitride membrane, 100–150 nm, carrying the pattern in 25–50 nm of tungsten or gold. Both regions transmit the electrons — Plummer gives a 100 keV beam — but they scatter them differently. The light SiN scatters them weakly, through small angles; the heavy metal scatters them strongly, through large angles. A lens focuses the beam, and an aperture in its back focal plane passes the weakly scattered electrons and blocks most of the strongly scattered ones, so the wafer receives a high-contrast, 4:1-reduced image in which the metal regions are dark (notes pp. 104–105).

Scattering rather than absorbing is the point: an absorbing stencil mask would heat and distort under the beam, while the thin scattering mask absorbs little (Plummer). The notes list four advantages (p. 105): no synchrotron is needed; a tool can be added one stepper at a time; the scanned beam heats the thin SiN little, so the pattern distorts little; and the depth of focus is 50 times that of 0.25 µm optical lithography. And five limitations, the traditional ones of electron beams: low throughput, proximity effects, radiation damage, charging and the lack of a good resist. By 2001 the effort was losing support to EUV; Lucent's successor Agere had cancelled its programme, and the Nikon–IBM projection approach, PREVAIL, carried electron projection forward (Wolf pp. 301, 309).

9.5 Ion beams

Lecture 20 closed with ion-beam lithography, from outside the notes: a beam of gallium ions, focused, like electrons, by electric fields — "if you apply negative … then you can focus", in contrast with X-rays, which cannot be focused. The lecture's argument for it was that the ions are much heavier than electrons, which it said reduces the exposure time; it gave no mechanism. It described the method as talked about but not in use: "maybe one day". This page records the lecture's account; neither the notes nor the course texts treat ion-beam lithography.

A SCALPEL tool is to print 0.13 µm lines. What width are they on the mask, and of what? An engineer proposes replacing the 25–50 nm metal by a 1 µm absorber so that the dark regions stop the electrons outright. What does the design lose?

With 4:1 reduction, 4 × 0.13 = 0.52 µm on the mask, written in 25–50 nm of W or Au on a 100–150 nm SiN membrane (notes p. 104). A thick absorber would stop the electrons by absorbing their energy in the mask, which heats it and distorts the pattern — the problem of the stencil masks SCALPEL was designed to avoid (Plummer). The thin scatterer deflects the electrons instead of absorbing them, and the aperture, not the mask, removes them, so the mask stays cool: the third advantage in the notes (p. 105).

  • KrF: 200–2500 Hz, 2–20 W, 5–20 ns, ≈ 10 mJ/pulse → MW peaks. ≈ 100 pulses per exposure for 1 % dose. Gas refill ≈ 5 days; clean at 10⁹ pulses, replace cavity at 2.5 × 10⁹. Line narrowed to < 1 pm: 1 pm ≈ 0.15 µm of focus.
  • ArF: 20 W at 4 kHz; longer pulses lower peak power and lens damage. F₂: 157.63 and 157.52 nm, 87 % in the longer line.
  • Optics: glass absorbs below ≈ 350 nm; fused silica to 193 nm but compacts under ArF light; CaF₂ at 157 nm (hygroscopic, soft, chips, birefringent); N₂ purge at 157 nm; 157 nm scanner $12–20 M. CaF₂ reticle expands 38× more than fused silica.
  • R = k1λ/NA; RETs take k1 from ≈ 0.75 toward 0.35. Off-axis illumination: zero and one first order at opposite pupil edges, minimum pitch λ/NA → λ/(2NA); more DOF; dense patterns only. OPC: bias, serifs, jogs, scattering bars; corrects distortion, not resolution.
  • PSM (Levenson et al. 1982): 180° shift makes neighbouring fields cancel between features. d = λ/(2(n − 1)) (12.1): 243.8 nm at 248 nm, 172.6 nm at 193 nm in fused silica. 0.3 → 0.18 µm at 248 nm, NA 0.6 (k1 0.73 → 0.44). Alternating: strongest, dense lines only, phase-edge and brick-wall problems. Attenuated (≈ 10 %): any layout, contacts. MEF = M ΔCDprinted/ΔCDmask.
  • Notes: 157/126 nm, CMP to ±0.2 µm, optical floor 70–50 nm; Wolf adds NA < 1 and no further flattening. Immersion (lecture, not notes): NA = n sin θ, water n ≈ 1.44 at 193 nm, NA 1.35 → 71.5 nm by the course's form (38–40 nm in production with RETs).
  • Non-optical: masks $5000–100,000 (notes), no pellicle. X-ray: 4–20 Å, 1:1 proximity, membrane mask with Au/Pt/W, synchrotron; PMMA is positive.
  • EUV: 10–15 nm (13.5 nm), all-reflective, 4–5× reduction, vacuum. Mirrors: ≈ 40 Mo/Si pairs, Bragg d = λ/(2 sin θ) = 6.75 nm at normal incidence, ≈ 70 % each; nine reflections pass ≈ 4 %. Mask absorber Cr/TiN/TaN; Mo/Si intermix above 150 °C. Nd:YAG on Xe jet, 1500 W → 10 W (notes); production uses a CO₂ laser on tin (210 W in 2017).
  • E-beam: λ = √(150/V) Å (12.5): 0.122 Å at 10 kV, 0.055 Å at 50 kV; limited by scattering (proximity effect) and serial writing (≈ 1 wafer/h), so used for masks. Raster for dense patterns, vector for sparse. SCALPEL: SiN 100–150 nm with 25–50 nm W/Au, back-focal-plane aperture, 4:1; DOF 50× that of 0.25 µm optics; low throughput, proximity, damage, charging, resist. Ion beam (Ga⁺): lecture only.
