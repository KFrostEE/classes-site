Module 13

Chemical Mechanical Polishing: The Process

Why a chip with several levels of wiring must be made flat between levels; the four degrees of planarization and the planarization factor that measures them; the older planarization methods and why they stop working beyond three metal levels; the polishing tool; the metrics by which a CMP process is judged; Preston's law and the hydration-and-plowing mechanism by which oxide is removed; and why polishing removes the high regions of a surface faster than the low ones.

Prerequisites M04, M11 Read time ~45 min Sources Class notes pp. 106–120 (Chapter 8); Lectures 20–24; Wolf Vol. 4 Ch. 8

A finished CMOS chip carries its transistors in the top micrometre of the silicon and its wiring above them, in several levels of metal lines separated by layers of deposited oxide. Each level is deposited over the patterned level beneath it, so every line and every gate leaves a step in the surface above it, and the steps add up level by level. Lithography cannot print on a surface that is rougher than its depth of focus, the tolerance in height over which the image stays sharp (M11 §3). Chemical mechanical polishing (CMP) removes the steps. It is the process that M04 §6.4 used to give the bare wafer its mirror finish, applied now to the deposited oxide and metal layers of a half-built chip. This module, the first half of Chapter 8 of the notes, covers why planarization is needed (§1), how its degree is classified and measured (§2), what preceded CMP (§3), the tool (§4), the metrics of a CMP process (§5), how polishing removes oxide (§6) and why it removes the high regions faster than the low ones (§7). M14 takes up the slurries, metal polishing, the copper damascene process, endpoint detection and low-k dielectrics; M15 applies CMP to shallow trench isolation.

1 · Why multilevel interconnect needs planarization

Interconnect is the network of conductors that joins the transistors into a circuit. A modern logic chip needs more wiring than one level can carry, so the conductors are stacked: metal-1 is patterned, an insulator is deposited over it, metal-2 is patterned on that insulator, and so on, with vertical plugs (vias) joining the levels. The insulator between the polysilicon gate level and metal-1 is the pre-metal dielectric (PMD); the insulator between two metal levels is the intermetal dielectric (IMD); both are usually silicon dioxide deposited by chemical vapour deposition (CVD), sometimes doped with phosphorus or boron (§3.1). The notes put the requirement at the start of Chapter 8: multilevel interconnection schemes require planarization, and global planarization of ULSI circuits requires CMP. This section shows why.

1.1 Steps accumulate

A CVD film is close to conformal: it follows the surface it is deposited on, with roughly the same thickness on the tops of features and in the spaces between them. A conformal film therefore reproduces every step beneath it, and a feature patterned on top of the film adds its own thickness to the step. The notes illustrate this with a two-level-metal, single-polysilicon process (p. 108, from Wolf §8.1.2). The field oxide that separates the transistors stands 0.3 µm above the active regions; on it run a 0.4 µm polysilicon line, a 0.5 µm metal-1 line and a 1.0 µm metal-2 line, each above the last. After each layer the largest step on the wafer surface is the sum of all the thicknesses below it.

1.2 Three ways a rough surface fails

The notes list three conditions by which a non-planar surface prevents reliable connections between levels (p. 108).

  1. Poor step coverage. A metal film deposited over a high, steep step is thinner on the step than on the flat. Wolf defines the step coverage as the ratio of the minimum thickness on the step, ts, to the nominal thickness on horizontal regions, tn, eq. (13.1). Coverage worsens as the step height and the aspect ratio (the height-to-spacing ratio of two adjacent steps) increase, and improves as the step's contour is smoothed and its slope made less vertical. The thinned metal on the step carries the full line current through a smaller cross-section, so it has a higher resistance and a higher current density there.
  2. Metal stringers. A stringer is a strip of metal left along the foot of a step after the metal is patterned. Metal lines are etched anisotropically, that is vertically downward with almost no sideways etching. A film of thickness h2 deposited conformally over a step of height h1 is h1 + h2 thick when measured vertically against the step's sidewall (Figure 2). An etch timed to clear h2 on the flat leaves the excess as a stringer, which can short two lines that cross the same step. Clearing it needs an overetch, during which the resist is also eroded, thinnest over the upper corners of the steps (Wolf §8.1.2).
  3. Depth of focus. Submicron lithography needs the surface planar within ±0.5 µm. If the dielectric surface carries steps larger than 0.5 µm, metal-1 cannot be patterned to the full resolution of the stepper (notes p. 108). This condition is the one that makes planarization mandatory: the first two can be eased by smoother step profiles, but the focus limit tightens as the numerical aperture rises and the features shrink (M11 eq. 11.2, §3.3).

1.3 Design rules set by the dielectric

Before planarization was routine, the shape of the deposited dielectric constrained the layout. Design rules are the minimum dimensions a layout must respect. The notes (p. 109) list three that the intermediate dielectric forces in a process with polysilicon and two metal levels: (1) a minimum polysilicon-to-metal-1 spacing, where the metal does not overlap the polysilicon; (2) a minimum distance between coincident edges, where metal-1 overlaps polysilicon; and (3) a minimum metal-1-to-metal-1 spacing.

Each case follows from conformal deposition. If polysilicon and metal-1 come too close, the gap between them either closes over and leaves a void in the CVD oxide (a) or remains as a deep, narrow crevice that the next metal cannot cover (b). If their edges coincide, the two thicknesses stack into one step that may be too high for metal-2 to cover (c), and a crevice at such a step combines both problems (d). Adjacent metal-1 lines must be spaced widely enough that the dielectric deposited between them forms neither a void nor a crevice (notes p. 110). Spacing everything generously enough to avoid all four cases is impractical, because it enlarges the chip; the purpose of planarization processes is to remove these rules altogether (Wolf §8.1.3).

A process has a 0.25 µm field-oxide step, 0.35 µm polysilicon, 0.6 µm metal-1 and 0.8 µm metal-2, all conformal and unplanarized. Over what step is metal-2 patterned, and does it meet a ±0.5 µm planarity requirement? The 0.6 µm metal-1 film is deposited over the full step beneath it: what overetch clears its stringers?

The running sum gives 0.25, 0.6, 1.2 and 2.0 µm. Metal-2 is patterned on the IMD over metal-1, where the step is 1.2 µm, more than twice the 0.5 µm allowance, so it fails. The step under metal-1 is t² = 0.6 µm; the 0.6 µm metal-1 film over it is 0.6 + 0.6 = 1.2 µm thick vertically at the foot, so clearing it takes 0.6/0.6 = 1.0 times the nominal etch time beyond the nominal etch, a 100 % overetch. Both numbers grow with every unplanarized level, which is the case for planarizing each dielectric before the metal above it is patterned.

2 · Degrees of planarization and the planarization factor

"Planarized" is used loosely, so the notes (pp. 106–107, from Wolf §8.1.1) classify it, for the case of an IMD deposited over patterned metal-1. The classification separates two properties of a step that different processes improve to different extents: its slope and its height. A process can round a step without lowering it, or lower it everywhere except over the widest features.

  1. First degree, smoothing. The slopes of the steps at the IMD surface are lessened; the step height is not significantly reduced.
  2. Second degree, partial (semi-) planarization. The step height is reduced and the slopes are smoothed.
  3. Third degree, complete local planarization. The steps are eliminated where the features beneath are close together, which Wolf gives as less than about 10 µm apart; wide, isolated features still show a step.
  4. Fourth degree, complete global planarization. The surface is planar over arbitrary topography.

The quantitative measure is the planarization factor, the fractional reduction of the step height, eq. (13.2). With no change in step height, as in no planarization or smoothing only, tfstep = tistep and β = 0; with the step removed, tfstep = 0 and β = 1. Lecture 21 made the same two limits the point of the definition, and added that a process in the third stage reaches about 0.9 rather than 1. CMP achieves β = 0.90–0.95 (notes p. 112), a 90–95 % step-height reduction, and does so over distances of several millimetres; the techniques of §3 planarize only over distances below about 10 µm (Wolf §8.2).

β = 1 − tfsteptistep
(13.2)
β planarization factor, dimensionless, 0 to 1 · tfstep final step height · tistep initial step height (same length unit). β = 0: no planarization or smoothing only; β = 1: complete planarization. A dielectric step of 1.0 µm is polished with β = 0.92. What step remains? A second process rounds a 0.6 µm step into a smooth bump of the same 0.6 µm height: what is its β, and which degree of planarization is it?

By eq. (13.2), tf = (1 − 0.92) × 1.0 = 0.08 µm. The second process leaves tf = ti = 0.6 µm, so β = 1 − 0.6/0.6 = 0: it changes only the slope, which is the first degree, smoothing. The planarization factor measures height alone, so it cannot distinguish smoothing from no planarization at all; the degree classification exists because the slope also matters for step coverage (§1.2).

3 · Planarization before CMP

CMP spread through the industry in the early 1990s (Lecture 21; Wolf calls it the fastest-growing IC process technology of the 1990s). It was developed at IBM from 1983, exclusively there until 1988, and first described in public, for oxide films, in an IBM paper of 1989 (Wolf §8.4). Two- and three-level-metal processes before it used other methods, and the notes name three: thermal flow of oxide layers, sacrificial resist etchback and spin-on glass (p. 112). They are worth knowing because each reached only local planarization, and the reason why defines what CMP had to do instead.

3.1 Doped glass and reflow

Silicon dioxide doped with boron is borosilicate glass (BSG), with phosphorus phosphosilicate glass (PSG), and with both borophosphosilicate glass (BPSG); in the doped forms the B₂O₃ and P₂O₅ enter the SiO₂ network. Pure SiO₂ needs a far higher temperature: the lectures put it at about 1700 °C and called it a melting point (Lectures 22, 23; the notes and Wolf give no value). Doping lowers the temperature at which the glass softens and flows to the range the wafer can tolerate: Lecture 23 gave 800–900 °C and an anneal at 850–900 °C, and Wolf mentions a 900 °C glass-reflow step and reflow of doped glasses above 800 °C. A glass has no single melting point, so the lectures' "melting point" here means the temperature at which the doped oxide flows. Heating the wafer to that temperature lets surface tension round the sharp corners of the deposited film. This is reflow. Lecture 23 gave an example of its use: rounding the sharp top corners of a trench etched in doped oxide so that a barrier film later sputtered into it is continuous over the corners; undoped oxide cannot be reflowed at a temperature the wafer survives.

Reflow smooths a surface and partly planarizes it over short distances. Wolf lists it as the planarization method for the PMD (§8.11.1) and classes it with the methods that give only local planarization. It is also limited to the dielectric under the first metal: once metal is on the wafer, later steps stay far below reflow temperatures, about 450–500 °C in Wolf (tungsten plugs are never exposed above about 500 °C, and the final hydrogen anneal is at about 450 °C). The same doping that lowers the flow temperature makes the oxide softer, which matters again in §6.4: doped oxides polish faster.

3.2 Sacrificial-layer etchback

The etchback method uses a liquid to make the surface flat and a plasma etch to transfer that flatness into the oxide (notes pp. 110–111; Wolf §8.1.5). It was used for two-level-metal processes down to 1 µm technology, and it planarizes well between steps 2–10 µm apart. The sequence:

  1. Deposit the CVD dielectric over the metal lines.
  2. Coat it with a sacrificial layer, spin-on glass (SOG, a silicate dissolved in a solvent) or photoresist, applied as a liquid. The liquid fills the low regions more than the high ones, and on baking becomes a solid film whose surface is nearly flat. With most photoresists the degree of planarization exceeds 70 % for features smaller than about 10 µm (Wolf).
  3. Etch in an O₂ or O₂ + CF₄ plasma. Wolf describes a fast first etch of the sacrificial layer until the tops of the dielectric are exposed; the chemistry is then adjusted so that the sacrificial layer and the dielectric etch at about the same rate.
  4. Continue until the sacrificial layer is gone. Because the two materials etch equally fast, the flat profile of the sacrificial layer is transferred into the dielectric, which is left fairly flat. A further CVD oxide is usually deposited to restore the minimum thickness over the metal.

The method fails as the metal spacing shrinks. If the aspect ratio of the gap between adjacent metal-1 lines with vertical sidewalls exceeds 0.4, most CVD oxides close over the gap and leave a void (notes p. 111). Voids trap resist and moisture, which escape if the etchback opens them, and an opened void fills with metal during the metal-2 deposition, leaving a metal stringer after metal-2 is patterned. The notes conclude that the process is not feasible for metal-1 spacings below 1.25 µm unless the oxide can be deposited at low temperature without voids; Wolf names PECVD TEOS, O₃–TEOS and high-density-plasma CVD oxides as such processes.

3.3 Why only local planarization

Reflow, etchback and spin-on glass all rely on a liquid, or a glass softened by heat, levelling itself. Such a film levels a narrow gap but follows the topography across a wide one: Wolf's data for spun-on polymers show their degree of planarization falling as the feature size grows (Fig. 8-6), and he limits all three methods to smoothing and local planarization over distances below about 10 µm. They reach the third degree of planarization at best (§2). The notes record the result: with more than three metal layers these techniques are inadequate, since they cannot provide global planarization (p. 112). CMP removes material from the highest points of a surface first and planarizes over distances of up to several millimetres (§2, §7), so it planarizes globally.

A process uses 0.7 µm thick metal-1 with vertical sidewalls. What is the smallest metal-1 spacing at which a CVD oxide deposited for resist etchback stays free of voids? If the design needs 1.25 µm spacing, what happens, and what two remedies does the material of this section offer?

The void criterion is h/s ≤ 0.4, so smin = 0.7/0.4 = 1.75 µm. At 1.25 µm the aspect ratio is 0.7/1.25 = 0.56 > 0.4: the oxide closes over the gap and leaves a void, which the etchback may open and metal-2 then fill, leaving a stringer. The remedies are a void-free low-temperature deposition (PECVD TEOS, O₃–TEOS or HDP CVD oxide), which removes the void, or a different planarization altogether: the etchback still gives only local planarization, so beyond three metal levels the answer is CMP.

4 · The CMP process

CMP combines a chemical reaction that softens or converts the surface with mechanical abrasion that removes the product. The notes (p. 112) describe it as mechanical polishing assisted by chemical action, which produces selective material removal. The equipment is the polishing machine of the wafer shop (M04 §6.4), but the job is different: wafer polishing removes on the order of 10 ± 0.1 µm of silicon, while an oxide CMP step removes on the order of 0.5 ± 0.01 µm, about 5 % of what wafer polishing removes, with ten times tighter absolute control (Wolf §8.5.1).

The wafer is mounted face down on the wafer carrier, which presses it against a polishing pad with a controlled downforce and rotates it. The pad covers a rotating platen. An aqueous slurry containing abrasive particles is dripped onto the pad and spread across it, forming a thin colloidal layer that saturates the pad. A colloid is a suspension of particles small enough, here 10–100 nm (notes p. 124), to stay dispersed in the liquid. The mechanical action and the chemical reactions together remove material from the wafer surface (notes p. 112). The pad, its conditioning and the slurry chemistries for each material are the subject of M14.

The carrier and the platen both turn, about axes that are offset. The local removal rate depends on the speed at which the pad passes each point of the wafer, so the two rotations set how uniform the removal is. M04 §6.4 derives the result: when carrier and platen turn in the same sense at the same angular velocity ω, the pad moves past every point of the wafer with the same velocity, of magnitude ωd for axes a distance d apart, and the removal is uniform; turned in opposite senses, the relative speed varies across the wafer. Lecture 5 described opposite senses, and M04 flags the difference. Wolf §8.5.1.1 describes the same-sense, equal-frequency case differently, with zero relative speed at the outer edge of the wafer and the greatest at the inner edge. That instantaneous description is wrong; his conclusion, uniform removal on time average, agrees with the exact result (M04 §6.4). Exact synchronization usually excites the worst vibration, so the carrier is usually driven at a constant difference from the platen frequency, found by experiment (Wolf §8.5.1.1).

Lecture 21 dictated one operational fact: the slurry is not recycled. Slurry dripped onto the pad is collected and discarded, and every wafer is polished with fresh slurry, because used slurry carries particles picked up from the mechanical action. Re-use has been studied, a KOH-based silica slurry giving roughly equal removal rates when re-used up to six times after pH and solids were restored, but Wolf (2002) describes slurry re-use as still in its infancy.

In a CMOS process CMP is applied to the oxide of shallow trench isolation (M15), to the PMD and IMD oxides, to tungsten plugs and to copper lines, and to the titanium nitride and tantalum barrier layers beneath the metals (Lectures 20, 22, 23). Wolf adds three reasons it is worth its complexity. It allows logic with many metal levels, eight in his account, without metal thinning over steps; it gives memories a larger depth-of-focus budget even with two metal levels, so smaller features and smaller chips; and it can reduce the defect density rather than increase it, one reason being a final light buff that removes residual slurry particles and the small scratches of the main polish. By 2000 a stacked-capacitor DRAM used upwards of nine polishing steps and a microprocessor up to sixteen.

A wafer is polished with the carrier held stationary (not rotating) at a distance R from the platen axis, the platen turning at ω. How does the pad speed differ between the wafer edges nearest to and farthest from the platen axis, and what does that do to the removal? Why does a CMP step on a finished interconnect level need tighter control than the final polish of a bare wafer?

The pad moves at ωr, with r the distance from the platen axis, so across a wafer of radius a the speed runs from ω(R − a) at the near edge to ω(R + a) at the far edge. Removal follows the pad speed (§6), so the far edge is polished faster and the film is left wedge-shaped across the wafer, a within-wafer non-uniformity (§5.3); rotating the carrier in the same sense at the same rate makes the relative velocity uniform (M04 §6.4). The interconnect step removes about 0.5 µm and must stop within about ±0.01 µm, because the oxide left over the metal is part of the circuit (§5.4), whereas wafer polishing removes about 10 µm to ±0.1 µm; the absolute tolerance is ten times tighter.

5 · Measuring a CMP process

A CMP process is judged by how fast it removes material, how completely it removes steps, how uniform the result is at three scales (within a die, across a wafer, from wafer to wafer) and how much oxide it wastes between features. The notes define a metric for each (pp. 113–117, following Wolf §8.3). Wolf states the corresponding goals: a high degree of planarization; less than 150 nm between the highest and lowest points of a die; film thickness uniform within the wafer to ±2–5 % (1σ); removal rates of 100–140 nm/min; uniform removal from wafer to wafer; good selectivity or endpoint detection; few added defects; and low cost per polished wafer.

5.1 Removal rate

The removal rate (RR) is the thickness removed per unit time from a non-patterned wafer, eq. (13.3). It is measured by polishing a blanket film for a set time and measuring its thickness before and after. A blanket film is measured because on a patterned wafer the local rate depends on the pattern (§7), whereas the blanket rate characterizes the process itself. A high RR gives a high throughput; Wolf notes that it is most often expressed in Å/min.

5.2 Degree of planarization

The degree of planarization (DOP) is the percentage by which the height of a feature above its surroundings is reduced, eq. (13.4). Hdi is the height of the feature, for example the oxide bump over a metal line, before polishing and Hdf its height after; DOP = 100 % means the feature is completely planarized (Lecture 22 made the same point with Hdf = 0). When the feature height is the step height, the DOP is the planarization factor of eq. (13.2) expressed as a percentage: DOP = 100β.

5.3 Non-uniformity: within a die, within a wafer, wafer to wafer

The notes define three non-uniformities in the same form: the standard deviation of n thickness measurements divided by their mean, eq. (13.5). They differ in where the measurements are taken. Within-die non-uniformity (WIDNU) uses n points within one die; within-wafer non-uniformity (WIWNU) uses n points across one wafer; wafer-to-wafer non-uniformity (WTWNU) uses measurements on each of n wafers. Which thickness is measured depends on the non-uniformity being specified, for example the oxide thickness in the field between metal lines, or the distance from the polished oxide surface to the planarized substrate beneath (Wolf). The standard deviation is the sample form, with n − 1 in the denominator, because the mean is estimated from the same measurements.

Each scale has its own causes. Within a die, the polishing rate depends on the pattern: narrow, isolated features are planarized to a greater degree than wide, isolated features or narrow, densely spaced ones, a pattern-dependent planarization treated in §7. A second within-die effect is dishing. When a metal film is deposited into trenches in a planar dielectric and polished back, the ideal result is metal flush with the dielectric surface; the real result has the metal recessed below it, especially in wide trenches (notes p. 114). Wolf (§8.3.3) also calls dishing the case where the steps are planarized but film has been eroded between them until the oxide surface is not planar across the die, so the oxide thickness over the substrate varies within the die. The notes (p. 115) give that sentence with "metal film" where Wolf has "some film"; the surface that ends up non-planar is the oxide in both.

Across a wafer, Wolf names three causes: a wafer whose own thickness varies, polished with a completely rigid pad; the relation between the carrier and platen rotation rates (§4); and an edge bead, a rim of thicker film left because removal is slower near the wafer edge. From wafer to wafer, the causes he names are pad ageing and batch-to-batch variation of the slurry.

5.4 Efficiency of planarization

When oxide is deposited over metal lines and polished flat, enough oxide must remain over the lines to limit the electrical cross-talk and parasitic capacitance between metal levels (notes p. 117). The capacitance between lines, together with their resistance, sets the RC delay of a signal along them, which is why Lecture 22 led from this point to low-dielectric-constant insulators (M14). The designer specifies the oxide thickness to be left over the metal; the process engineer must deposit enough oxide that polishing leaves at least that much. The efficiency of planarization (%EOP) measures how much of the polishing goes into flattening rather than thinning, eq. (13.6). Tr is the oxide thickness removed from the top of a feature and Td the oxide thickness lost at the centre of a wide space between metal lines. An ideal process, %EOP ≈ 100 %, planarizes completely without eroding any oxide in the wide spaces.

%EOP = Tr − TdTr × 100
(13.6)

Wolf rewrites the efficiency for planning the deposition, eq. (13.7). If the final surface is planar, the oxide over a line is Tcov and the oxide in a wide space is Hmetal + Tcov, with Hmetal the line height. A conformal deposit Ttotal thick then loses Tr = Ttotal − Tcov over the line and Td = Ttotal − Hmetal − Tcov in the space, so Tr − Td = Hmetal and eq. (13.6) becomes EOP = Hmetal/(Ttotal − Tcov).

6 · How CMP removes oxide

The metrics of §5 describe what a process does; this section describes why the removal rate depends on pressure, speed and the material. The notes give two levels of description (pp. 117–120): an empirical law relating the rate to the pressure and the speed of the pad, and a mechanism that explains it. Both come from optical-glass polishing, because the silicon oxides of a chip are glasses (Wolf §8.5.1).

6.1 Preston's law

Preston's law, a model developed for polishing glass, states that the removal rate of a flat region is proportional to the applied pressure and to the linear velocity at which the polishing pad moves relative to the glass, eq. (13.8) (notes pp. 117–118; F. W. Preston, 1927). Preston's constant Kp is independent of pressure and velocity. Everything else that affects the rate is collected into it: the hardness and Young's modulus of the glass, the slurry, and the composition and hardness of the pad (Wolf). The same law describes the CMP of oxide. It was found empirically, not derived from a physical model, and it describes only the mechanical variables; §6.3 supplies the mechanism behind it.

RR = Kp P v
(13.8)
RR removal rate (m/s in SI) · P applied pressure (Pa = N/m²) · v linear velocity of the pad relative to the wafer (m/s) · Kp Preston's constant, independent of P and v; with these units it is in Pa⁻¹ (m²/N). Process data come in psi and ft/min: 1 psi = 6894.76 Pa, 1 ft/min = 5.08×10⁻³ m/s. Notes p. 118; Wolf eq. 8.13.

6.2 Beyond the linear law

Later work found that the dependence of the oxide removal rate on pressure and velocity is not simply linear (notes p. 118). The general fitted form, eq. (13.9), raises each to an exponent determined by experiment for the system being polished. Zhao and Shi (1999), in what Wolf calls the first model to include the hardness of the pad and the wafer, derived a sub-linear dependence on pressure, eq. (13.10), which experiments on oxide follow (Wolf Fig. 8-19). Shi and Zhao had derived the non-linear pressure dependence for polishing with soft pads (1998); Wolf gives no physical reason for the exponent 2/3. The same model adds a threshold. A particle that rolls against the surface removes little, while a particle held firmly by the pad slides against the wafer and removes material, and sliding needs a minimum local pressure. Below that threshold pressure Pth nothing is removed, eq. (13.11), and Wolf reports that the planarization efficiency improves as Pth rises. §7.1 shows why.

RR = Kp Pαvβ
(13.9)

RR = Kp v P2/3
(13.10)

The difference matters when the pressure changes. Doubling the pressure doubles the rate by Preston's law but multiplies it by 22/3 = 1.59 by eq. (13.10). Raising the downforce is therefore a less efficient way to speed up oxide removal than the linear law suggests, while within eq. (13.10) the rate stays proportional to the pad speed. That too is a property of the model rather than a general finding: Tseng and Wang (1997, Wolf ref. 13) derived a sub-linear dependence on speed as well, which is why the fitted form (13.9) leaves both exponents free.

6.3 The removal mechanism: hydration and plowing

The mechanism differs from the polishing of bare silicon in M04 §6.4. There the slurry oxidizes the silicon surface, and abrasive particles harder than the oxidized layer but softer than silicon remove the oxidized layer; fresh silicon is exposed and the cycle repeats. An oxide film is already oxidized, so that sequence cannot operate (Wolf §8.5.1). The slurry for oxide CMP is silica particles in an aqueous alkaline solution, KOH or NH₄OH in deionized water, and silica particles have about the same hardness as the oxide; rubbed against unreacted SiO₂ they neither remove nor damage it appreciably (Wolf §8.5.3). Water is what makes removal possible. The polishing rate is extremely low without water, and a silica slurry in a non-aqueous liquid such as oil polishes substantially more slowly than one in water (Wolf §8.5.3). Izumitani and Harada (1971) proposed that glass polishing proceeds through a hydrated layer: glass that reacts with water forms a surface layer with a much lower hardness and strength than the SiO₂ beneath, and that layer is what is removed.

The notes (p. 120) give the oxide sequence. Each step depends on the one before.

  1. Plastic deformation. An abrasive particle held by the pad is pressed onto the oxide and deforms its surface plastically. The degree of deformation increases with the pressure of the particles on the surface and with frictional heating.
  2. Hydration. Oxide deformed plastically by a diamond indenter is known to take up water readily, and Wolf infers the same for oxide deformed by the abrasive particles. The hydrated layer is softer than the oxide beneath it.
  3. Plowing. The particles, sliding across the surface with the pad, cut the soft hydrated layer away. This plowing action increases with the linear velocity of the pad.
  4. Repeat (implied, not stated in the notes). Fresh oxide is exposed and the cycle continues.

Lecture 24 returned to the word "plowing" with a general definition the lecturer had looked up: mechanical plowing is one of the primary removal modes in CMP, alongside chemical bonding; the high points of the pad surface, its asperities, are pressed into the wafer by the down-force and cut it, removing material as small chips or debris; and the lecture added that the pad, "not only the particle", takes part. A student offered a description heard elsewhere, plowing as rough sanding and the chemical action as smooth sanding, and the lecturer agreed. That definition is a general one, said in the lecture to dominate for hard, ductile materials. For oxide the notes and Wolf give the pad a different part: it grips the abrasive particles and transmits the pressure, and the particles plow the hydrated layer (notes p. 120; Wolf §8.5.2.1, §8.5.3). This page keeps that mechanism.

The mechanism accounts for Preston's law (Wolf). Pressure sets how much oxide each contact deforms and so makes ready for removal; velocity sets how often and how far the particles slide across it to plow it off. The rate therefore rises with both, as the product Pv in eq. (13.8) says. The pad has a part in both stages: Wolf judges that it probably has to be soft enough to grip the particles, so that they slide rather than roll (§6.2), and hard enough to transmit the pressure to the oxide. A chemical process is a co-factor: the notes name dissolution of the SiO₂, and the higher removal rate of higher-pH slurries is often cited as evidence of it (Wolf).

One contact, two actions. In Lecture 23 the class found it hard to see how the same particles can both cause the hydration and remove the hydrated layer. The sources give the two actions different drivers. The deformation that lets water in increases with the pressure of the particles on the surface and with frictional heating (notes p. 120); the plowing that removes the hydrated layer increases with the linear velocity of the pad. One way to picture it, this page's reading rather than the sources': the load pressing a particle into the surface prepares the oxide, its motion along the surface removes oxide that is already hydrated, and both happen at once wherever particles slide over the wafer, rather than one after the other in time.

Cook proposed a complementary model in which the surface charges of particle and oxide control how often a particle reaches the surface: silica on silica repel, while a particle of a different oxide whose surface charge attracts the SiO₂ contacts it more often and polishes faster. He suggested ceria (CeO₂) particles on that basis (Wolf §8.5.2.1). The slurries of M14 include ceria for this use.

6.4 Hardness and doping

Dielectrics range in hardness from silicon nitride, very hard and inelastic, to the softer doped oxides and spin-on dielectrics, and their removal rate rises as their hardness falls (notes p. 118). The trend appears within a single material family. In fluorinated silicate glass (FSG) the hardness falls as more fluorine is incorporated, and the removal rate rises with it (Wolf Fig. 8-22). In PSG and BPSG the removal rate rises with the phosphorus and boron content (Wolf Fig. 8-23). The doping correlates with hardness because phosphorus and boron oxides incorporated into the SiO₂ network soften the oxide. Higher doping therefore gives a higher removal rate, and PSG and BPSG pre-metal dielectrics polish faster than undoped oxide (Wolf §8.11.1). The softening of §3.1, which lowers the flow temperature of the glass, and the faster polishing are two consequences of the same change in the network (Lecture 23). The notes (p. 118) write the oxides as "P₂O₃ and B₂O₅", as does Wolf (p. 342). The oxides that enter phosphosilicate and borosilicate glass are P₂O₅ and B₂O₃.

The hardness contrast also has a use. A hard layer under a soft one polishes more slowly, so it can serve as a polish stop, as the silicon nitride layer does in shallow trench isolation (M15; Wolf §8.12.1). How sharply it stops the polish also depends on the slurry chemistry, the subject of M14.

An oxide CMP process runs at 6 psi and 50 ft/min. If the pressure is raised to 9 psi, by what factor does the removal rate change under Preston's law and under Zhao and Shi's law? Which would you raise to increase the rate without losing the selectivity of §7, and why? Why does a silica slurry in oil barely polish an oxide?

The pressure ratio is 9/6 = 1.5. Preston: RR ∝ P, factor 1.50. Zhao and Shi: RR ∝ P2/3, factor 1.52/3 = 1.31. Both laws are linear in v, so raising the pad speed increases the rate in proportion everywhere on the wafer, while raising the pressure raises the pressure on the down-areas too. Under Preston's law, or Zhao and Shi's, a uniform rise leaves the up/down ratio of §7.1 unchanged; under the threshold law it lowers the selectivity the threshold adds, because the threshold becomes a smaller fraction of the down-area pressure (8.81 falls to 4.15 if the §7.1 pressures are all raised by 1.5 times). In oil there is no water to hydrate the oxide: the silica particles, about as hard as the oxide, deform it but cannot cut away unreacted SiO₂, so almost nothing is removed. The water is the chemical half of chemical mechanical polishing for an oxide.

7 · How CMP planarizes

A removal rate that was the same everywhere would lower the surface without flattening it. To planarize a film with topography, the surfaces of elevated features, the up-areas, must be eroded faster than those at lower elevations, the down-areas (notes p. 119). The selectivity is observed to depend on the feature dimensions, the structure and surface condition of the pad, and the height of the step being removed (notes p. 119; Lecture 23 listed the same factors). This section explains the selectivity and why it depends on the pattern.

7.1 Up-areas and down-areas

The selectivity comes in large part from the compliance of the pad (Wolf §8.5.3.1). The pad is pressed onto the wafer, and the wafer's raised features compress it more than the recessed regions do. The pad presses on the surface through the slurry, so the local pressure is highest where it is most compressed: on the up-areas, and most of all at the upper corner of the leading edge of a feature, the edge that the motion drives into the pad. By Preston's law the local removal rate follows the local pressure. The up-areas are therefore removed faster, and the leading corners fastest. As the step erodes, the pad reaches further into the down-areas, their pressure and removal rate rise toward those of the up-areas, and the rate at which the step height falls declines toward zero. In the last stage of polishing the selectivity is so small that a small residual step remains, which in practice continued polishing cannot remove (Wolf). That is consistent with CMP reaching β = 0.90–0.95 rather than 1 (§2), though Wolf states the two facts separately.

The sub-linear and threshold laws of §6.2 change how much a given pressure difference buys. The example below uses an assumed split of the pressure between up- and down-areas, chosen only to compare the three laws.

This is why Wolf reports that a higher threshold pressure improves the planarization efficiency.

7.2 Pattern dependence

The degree of planarization depends on the size of the features and on their density within a region of the die, and this dependence is one cause of within-die non-uniformity (notes p. 120). Narrow, isolated features are planarized to a greater degree than wide, isolated features or narrow, densely spaced ones (notes p. 115). Wolf (§8.5.3.2) gives the reasons.

  • Narrow against wide. The leading edge of a feature is removed fastest, and because the carrier rotates the wafer, every top edge of a feature becomes the leading edge in turn. A narrow feature is all edge and is planarized quickly. A wide feature loses its edges, but its centre is polished more slowly than its edges, so wide features planarize more slowly.
  • Isolated against dense. The time to planarize an up-area depends on the width of the up-area, the width of the down-areas beside it, and the ability of the pad to span the distance between up-areas rigidly (Burke). A stiffer pad spans the gaps, removes less from the down-areas and planarizes better; a softer pad sags into the gaps, removes more from the down-areas and planarizes worse. When features are farther apart than the levelling distance of the pad, the pad follows the surface and there is no selectivity at all.

Planarization is therefore slowest over the largest or densest interconnect structures next to wide open field areas, scribe lines and the wafer edge, and fastest over small arrays and narrow, isolated features (Wolf). The pad's stiffness cannot simply be raised to fix this: a pad with a large levelling distance also responds to the non-flatness of the wafer itself (thickness variation and bow), which creates within-wafer non-uniformity. The standard pad for oxide CMP is a compromise between the two, a hard cast-polyurethane top pad (IC-1000) for planarization over a soft felt underpad (Suba-IV) for uniformity, at some cost in planarization (Wolf §8.11.1). An acceptable degree of planarization also has to be specified, because late in the polish material is removed nearly as fast from the down-areas as from the up-areas and the step no longer falls appreciably (Wolf §8.5.3.2).

7.3 Two kinds of polishing job

The notes divide CMP processes into two categories (p. 119), and they end differently.

  1. Planarizing a homogeneous film, such as an ILD oxide, and stopping while a layer of the film remains everywhere. The polished surface is all oxide, and everything in §§6–7.2 applies: the selectivity between up- and down-areas does the work. (The notes' wording, "stopping while the removal of the surface film is complete", reverses Wolf's "incomplete"; the film is not removed.)
  2. Removing a deposited film from the high regions entirely, so that it remains only in recesses below the surface, as in copper damascene, where copper over a tantalum barrier is polished until it is left only in trenches. The film starts roughly planar, so the removal is essentially non-selective until the field is cleared. If polishing continues, material in the trenches keeps being removed, more in wide trenches than in narrow ones: this is dishing (notes p. 120).

The second kind, with the slurries, endpoint detection and the dishing of copper lines, is the subject of M14.

A die has an isolated 1 µm wide metal line, an isolated 200 µm wide metal pad and a dense array of 1 µm lines at 1 µm spacing, all under the same oxide. Which is planarized fastest, and why? What do the sources say about the order of the other two? What does a much softer pad do?

Fastest: the isolated narrow line, which is all leading edge and carries a high local pressure (notes p. 115). The notes and Wolf put both the wide isolated pad and the narrow dense array below it, but do not order those two against each other. The 200 µm pad loses its edges first while its centre polishes slowly, so a plateau remains; the array's planarization time depends on the widths of its up- and down-areas and on the pad's ability to span them (Burke). Wolf's summary puts the slowest planarization over the largest or densest structures next to wide open areas. A softer pad has a shorter levelling distance: it sags into the down-areas, removes more from the low regions and planarizes less selectively, while the within-wafer uniformity may improve.

Summary

  • Conformal films stack the steps: 0.3, 0.7, 1.2, 2.2 µm for field oxide 0.3, poly 0.4, M1 0.5, M2 1.0 µm. Three failures: step coverage (ts/tn), stringers (vertical thickness h1 + h2 at the foot), depth of focus (planar within ±0.5 µm). Dielectric design rules: poly–M1 spacing, coincident edges, M1–M1 spacing.
  • Degrees: smoothing, partial, complete local (gaps < ≈ 10 µm), complete global. β = 1 − tf/ti: 0 for none or smoothing, 1 for complete; CMP 0.90–0.95, over several mm.
  • Before CMP: doped-glass reflow (Wolf: above 800 °C, a 900 °C step; lecture 850–900 °C; pure SiO₂ ≈ 1700 °C per the lectures); sacrificial etchback (SOG or resist, O₂/CF₄, equal rates; two-level metal to 1 µm; steps 2–10 µm apart; voids above aspect ratio 0.4, so not below 1.25 µm M1 spacing). All local only; inadequate beyond three metal levels.
  • Tool: wafer face down on a rotating carrier, downforce, pad on a rotating platen, colloidal slurry (10–100 nm abrasive). Same-sense equal rotation gives uniform relative velocity (M04). Slurry is not recycled. Oxide CMP removes ≈ 0.5 ± 0.01 µm.
  • Metrics: RR = ΔT/t on a blanket wafer (goal 100–140 nm/min); DOP = (Hi − Hf)/Hi × 100 % = 100β; WIDNU, WIWNU, WTWNU = σ/x̄ with n − 1 (goal ±2–5 % within wafer); dishing; %EOP = (Tr − Td)/Tr × 100, deposit Ttotal = Hmetal/EOP + Tcov.
  • Preston (1927): RR = KpPv, empirical; general KpPαvβ; Zhao and Shi KpvP2/3, with a threshold pressure. Typical 7 psi, 30–40 ft/min.
  • Oxide removal: particle pressure deforms the oxide plastically (and frictional heat helps) → water enters (hydration) → the soft layer is plowed off by sliding particles → repeat. Pressure feeds the first step, speed the third. SiO₂ dissolution a co-factor (higher pH faster). Silica slurry in KOH/NH₄OH. Softer dielectrics polish faster: Si₃N₄ slowest; doping with P₂O₅, B₂O₃ or F softens SiO₂.
  • Planarization: pad compliance puts more pressure on up-areas and leading corners; selectivity falls as the step falls, leaving a small residue. Narrow isolated features planarize fastest; wide isolated and narrow dense ones slower; beyond the pad's levelling distance, none. Stiff pad: better planarization, worse WIWNU; IC-1000 on Suba-IV. Two jobs: planarize a homogeneous film (ILD), or clear the field and leave the film in recesses (damascene; dishing) — M14.
