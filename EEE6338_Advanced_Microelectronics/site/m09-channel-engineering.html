Module 09

Channel Engineering

How the doping beneath the gate is shaped in depth and along the channel. What sets how steep an implanted profile can be, how channelling and transient enhanced diffusion blur it, the super-steep retrograde profile and the electrostatics that make it worth building, the limit that ends it below 0.1 µm, and the tilted pocket implant that puts dopant only where punch-through starts.

Prerequisites M02, M03, M08 Read time ~55 min Sources Class notes pp. 75–76, 85–86 and 89–90 (Chapter 5); Lectures 17–18; Wolf Vol. 4 ch. 5; Plummer, Deal & Griffin ch. 7–8; Taur & Ning 3/E §6.3; SRIM-2013

The channel is the strip of silicon under the gate oxide, between the source and drain, where the gate inverts the surface and the current flows (M01). Its doping does three jobs at once. It sets the threshold voltage through the depletion-charge term (M03 §4), it fixes how far the source and drain depletion regions reach toward each other and so whether they merge (punch-through, M02 §5.2), and it sets the mobility of the carriers that form the channel, through the transverse field its depletion charge adds and through scattering by the dopant ions themselves. M02 §5 surveyed the structures that answer this: the threshold implant, super-steep retrograde profiles and halo and pocket implants. M08 built the well underneath them. This module takes the part of Chapter 5 that sits between the well and the drain (notes pp. 75–76 and 89–90) and goes into the mechanism: what makes an implanted profile steep or broad, what destroys its steepness afterwards, why a profile that is light at the surface and heavy below lowers the threshold voltage while still holding the depletion layer short, and how a tilted implant places dopant at the two ends of the channel only. Two effects the notes mention only in passing are developed here because the profiles depend on them: channelling, which Lecture 18 explained, and transient enhanced diffusion, which the notes treat under shallow source–drain junctions (pp. 85–86). M10 then applies both to the source and drain.

1 · What the channel doping has to do

Shrinking the gate length brings the source and drain closer, and the body doping has to rise to keep their depletion regions apart. The same doping sits where the inversion layer forms, where it raises the threshold voltage and lowers the carrier mobility. This section states the two requirements as the notes give them and shows, with M02's punch-through rule, why they cannot both be met by a uniform profile below about 0.13 µm.

1.1 Two requirements on one profile

The notes (p. 75) give both numbers: for a 0.13 µm gate the doping between source and drain must exceed 5×10¹⁷ cm⁻³ to prevent punch-through, and to avoid a loss of carrier mobility the doping is kept below 5×10¹⁷ cm⁻³. Read alone, the two sentences contradict each other; the notes' next sentence resolves them by keeping the surface concentration low and the mean concentration above the value. Wolf, whom the notes follow sentence by sentence here, states the limits that way: it is the mean channel doping that must exceed 5×10¹⁷ cm⁻³ for punch-through control, and the doping at the surface that must stay below 5×10¹⁷ cm⁻³ for the carriers to follow the universal mobility curve (Wolf p. 191). The two requirements apply at different depths. The inversion layer is a few nanometres thick and sits against the oxide. Doping lowers its mobility in two ways: ionised dopants in that layer scatter the carriers, which takes the mobility below the universal curve at low fields, and the depletion charge adds to the transverse field that presses the carriers against the interface, along which the universal curve falls (M02 §5.2; Taur & Ning 3/E pp. 192–194, eq. (5.51)). A light surface layer reduces the first directly and, as §5.1 shows, the depletion charge as well. Punch-through is decided by the depletion regions of the source and drain junctions, which reach tens of nanometres into the body, so it is decided by the doping averaged over that depth. A profile that is light at the surface and heavy below satisfies both. The notes call it a vertical retrograde profile, and, because the change from light to heavy has to happen over a short distance, a super-steep retrograde (SSR) profile (p. 75).

The super-steep retrograde layer is not the halo

In Lecture 17 the professor drew the heavily doped layer beneath the channel and said that this region "is called halo", formed by a deeper implantation, before turning to the source and drain. In the notes (pp. 89–90, the "pocket implant"), Wolf (p. 219), Plummer (p. 458) and Taur & Ning (§6.3.3.4) the halo and the pocket are lateral structures; they differ among themselves only on whether "halo" also covers the tilted implant (§6.1). The super-steep retrograde layer (§5) varies in depth: it is a blanket layer under the whole channel, implanted before the gate exists. The halo and pocket implants (§6) vary along the channel: they are implanted after the gate is patterned, self-aligned to it, and put dopant only at the two ends of the channel beside the source and drain. Lecture 17 also called the buried layer the "punch-through implantation" and the "retrograde implantation", close to the notes' "punch through control" region and "retrograde" profile (pp. 75–76). Use "SSR" or "punch-through-control layer" for the vertical profile and "halo" or "pocket" for the lateral one.

A process moves from a 0.18 µm to a 0.13 µm gate at the same drain bias and the same uniform body doping. In which direction do punch-through margin, threshold voltage and inversion-layer mobility move, and what does the designer have to change to restore the punch-through margin without giving up the other two?

Punch-through margin falls: W is set by the body doping and the bias (eq. (2.4)), neither of which changed, while L fell by 28 %, so 2W is a larger fraction of L and may exceed it. The threshold voltage and the mobility do not change to first order, because they depend on the doping at the surface, which did not change. Restoring L > 2W needs a higher doping (eq. (9.1)), which shortens W as NA−1/2. Put uniformly, that doping also reaches the surface, where it raises the depletion-charge term of the threshold voltage and lowers the mobility. The answer is to raise the doping only below the inversion layer: a retrograde profile, steep enough that the surface layer stays light — the super-steep retrograde profile — or doping added only at the channel ends, the pocket implant.

2 · What sets how steep an implanted profile can be

A super-steep retrograde profile asks for a change of more than a decade in concentration over a few tens of nanometres. Whether an implant can deliver that depends on the spread of stopping depths of the ions, and that spread depends mostly on the mass of the ion relative to silicon. This section quantifies the flank of an implanted profile, compares the light dopants with the heavy ones the notes substitute for them (p. 75), and explains the mass dependence.

Assumes: the implanted profile, Rp and ΔRp (M03 §9.2)

An implanted ion slows down by collisions with the electrons and nuclei of the target and stops after a path whose depth varies from ion to ion. The mean depth is the projected range Rp, and the standard deviation of the depths is the straggle ΔRp. To first order the as-implanted profile is a Gaussian, C(x) = Cp exp(−(x − Rp)²/(2ΔRp²)), with peak Cp = Q/(√(2π) ΔRp) for a dose Q in cm⁻² (M03 eq. (3.9)). Both Rp and ΔRp grow with energy; the depth of the peak is chosen by choosing the energy.

2.2 Why a heavy ion stops in a narrower band

An implanted ion loses energy in two ways: to the target's electrons, which slows it smoothly without deflecting it, and in elastic collisions with target nuclei, which slow it in steps and change its direction. The straggle comes mostly from the second, because each nuclear collision both removes a random amount of energy and turns the path by a random angle. Two things differ between boron and indium.

  • Which loss dominates. SRIM gives, for indium at 150 keV, nuclear stopping of 191 eV/Å against electronic stopping of 23 eV/Å, so at that energy 89 % of the stopping is nuclear; for boron at 20 keV the share is 33 %. Integrated over the whole slowing-down from those energies, the same tables give 92 % of indium's energy and 48 % of boron's lost in nuclear collisions.
  • How far one collision can turn the ion. In an elastic collision a projectile heavier than the atom it strikes cannot be deflected by more than arcsin(M2/M1), where M1 is the ion's mass and M2 the target atom's. For indium on silicon that is arcsin(28.09/114.82) = 14.2°, and for antimony 13.3°. Boron, lighter than silicon, can be turned through any angle in one collision, including straight back.

An indium ion therefore loses its energy mostly in collisions none of which can turn it by more than 14°, and none can send it back toward the surface; it still wanders sideways (SRIM's lateral straggle at 150 keV is 15.3 nm), but its stopping depths cluster more tightly about the mean. A boron ion is thrown sideways and backwards by individual collisions, and its stopping depths spread widely. At equal energy the heavy ion also stops at a shallower depth, because it loses more energy per unit path; the professor's Lecture 3 statement that indium "penetrates deeper" has the direction reversed, as M02 §5.3 records. The depth of an indium peak is chosen by giving it a higher energy, which is why the channel implants of the notes run at 100–200 keV (p. 76; Wolf p. 193 names indium and arsenic).

3 · Channelling

Table 1 describes a target in which the atoms are randomly placed. A silicon wafer is a single crystal, and along certain directions its atoms line up in rows with open space between them. An ion that enters one of those open directions can travel much further than the table predicts. Lecture 18 explained what channelling is and how it is prevented; it matters here because it puts a deep tail on exactly the profiles that are meant to be steep.

Seen along a low-index direction, the rows of silicon atoms form open tubes, the channels: axial channels along ⟨110⟩ and ⟨100⟩, and planar channels between {111} planes (Plummer p. 461, Fig. 8-10). An ion that enters a channel at a small enough angle to its axis is steered by small-angle scattering from the atoms that line the walls, so it travels a long way along the channel and loses energy mainly to the electrons. It stops when the electronic drag has slowed it, or when a hard collision knocks it out of the channel (Plummer p. 461). The ions that channel therefore come to rest far deeper than the random-target range, and the profile acquires a tail that continues much further than expected.

Plummer's simulated boron implants into ⟨100⟩ silicon at zero tilt (35 keV, Fig. 8-12) show the result: a main peak and a separate channelled tail, so that two distributions are needed to fit one profile. The tail depends on dose, because the first ions damage the lattice near the surface and block the channels for the ions that follow; low-dose implants channel most (Plummer pp. 462–463). A threshold or super-steep retrograde implant is a low-dose implant, of order 10¹² cm⁻² (§2 example; the notes' threshold implant, p. 74, is 5.5×10¹² cm⁻²), so it is the kind most exposed to channelling.

Channelling is reduced in three ways, each with a cost.

  • Tilt and rotate the wafer so that the beam meets no low-index direction. Lecture 18 gave a tilt of 7–10°; Plummer's end-of-chapter Problem 8.1 tilts the wafer 7° "to make it appear amorphous". A tilt cannot remove channelling completely, because a scattered ion can still enter a channel below the surface (Plummer p. 461). The cost is shadowing: a tilted beam is blocked on one side of every gate and lands asymmetrically (Plummer p. 458, Fig. 8-6). Many processes therefore implant at zero tilt (Plummer p. 475).
  • Implant through a thin amorphous screen oxide, which scatters the beam into random directions before it reaches the crystal (Plummer p. 463). This is the textbooks' reason for the screen oxide that M03 §9.2 describes beside the lecture's own reason. The cost: part of the dose stops in the oxide and is lost when it is stripped, a large fraction for sub-keV implants (notes p. 88).
  • Use a low energy. Wolf states that channelling effects are minimal for low-energy, high-current implants, and that a 0° implant gives the most abrupt lateral junction, since any tilt creates shadowed areas near the gate (Wolf p. 218). Channelling, like the straggle, scales with the projected range, and for sub-keV boron it becomes relatively unimportant compared with the broadening TED causes (Wolf p. 206). This is the argument for zero tilt in the shallow source–drain extensions of M10. The cost is throughput: sub-keV beams carry 1–2 mA against 10–25 mA above 10 keV (notes p. 85). (A high dose also blocks the channels with its own damage, as above, but the dose is set by the doping wanted.)

Explain in a few sentences what channelling is, why a low-dose threshold implant is more affected by it than a high-dose source–drain implant, and name two ways of reducing it together with what each one costs.

Channelling is the deep penetration of implanted ions that enter the crystal along an open lattice direction: between the aligned rows of silicon atoms the ion is steered by gentle collisions with the channel walls, loses energy mainly to electrons, and travels far beyond the projected range before it stops, which adds a deep tail to the profile. A high-dose implant damages the surface region with its first ions and blocks the channels for the rest; a low-dose implant leaves the crystal nearly intact, so a larger fraction of its ions channel. Tilting (7–10° in the lecture) and rotating the wafer makes the crystal look random to the beam, at the cost of shadowing beside each gate; implanting through a thin amorphous screen oxide randomises the beam's direction before it enters the silicon, at the cost of some range in the oxide and of dopant left in it.

4 · Transient enhanced diffusion

Every implant is followed by an anneal, which repairs the lattice and moves the dopant onto lattice sites where it is electrically active. During the first part of that anneal the dopant diffuses much faster than its normal diffusivity predicts, and then slows to the normal rate. This burst is transient enhanced diffusion (TED). It is one of the reasons an implanted profile does not stay as implanted, and it bears on the second half of the notes' case for the heavier species, that they "provide decreased spread during subsequent thermal process" (p. 76).

Assumes: dopants diffuse by pairing with point defects (M02 §5.3)

A substitutional dopant atom moves through silicon by pairing with a point defect: a self-interstitial, an extra silicon atom between lattice sites, or a vacancy, an empty lattice site. Boron and phosphorus move mainly with interstitials; antimony mainly with vacancies; arsenic by both. The diffusivity is proportional to the concentration of the defect that carries the dopant, so anything that raises the interstitial concentration above its equilibrium value speeds up boron and phosphorus by the same factor (M02 §5.3, and the corrections recorded there).

4.1 From implant damage to excess interstitials

An implanted ion displaces silicon atoms from their sites, creating vacancy–interstitial pairs. Most of them recombine early in the anneal, but not all: the implanted atoms now occupy lattice sites, and the silicon atoms they displaced remain as interstitials, about one per implanted atom (Wolf p. 209). This is the "+1" approximation: the net effect of an implant is one extra interstitial for every dopant atom introduced (Plummer p. 489). Plummer adds that it is better called "+n", with n of order one, because sputtering and the screen oxide change the balance. The evidence for it is direct: implants placed at the same depth with very different energies, and so very different primary damage, give identical TED (Plummer p. 488).

The excess interstitials do not stay single. They coalesce rapidly into extended defects: rod-like clusters lying on {311} planes, visible in the electron microscope after 1 s at 600 °C, and the more stable dislocation loops (Plummer p. 488; Wolf p. 209). The clusters are metastable. As the anneal continues they dissolve, and as they dissolve they emit interstitials back into the lattice.

4.2 The supersaturation, and why it ends

While the {311} clusters exist, they hold the free interstitial concentration above its equilibrium value — an interstitial supersaturation. In Plummer's simulation of a 40 keV, 10¹⁴ cm⁻² boron implant annealed at 750 °C the supersaturation is more than 10,000-fold (Fig. 8-34). Because boron diffuses with interstitials, its diffusivity is raised by the same factor, the time-averaged enhancement equalling the time-averaged supersaturation (Wolf p. 209). When the clusters are gone, the excess interstitials diffuse to the surface, where they are annihilated; the supersaturation and the enhancement end together. The enhancement is therefore transient (Wolf p. 210).

4.3 How much extra depth

Wolf gives the increase in junction depth that TED causes in terms of the two quantities that set the size and duration of the transient.

ΔXj2 ∝ N Rp exp (+1.4 eVkT)
(9.3)

Wolf eq. 5.9, written there as exp[−(−1.4 eV)/kT]. A proportionality: use it for ratios at fixed everything else. Low and medium doses and energies.

Each factor has a physical reading (Wolf p. 210).

  • N: a larger dose leaves more interstitials. Wolf reads this as a larger supersaturation and a larger enhancement (p. 210); in Plummer's cluster model the plateau's level is set by the temperature and the number of trapped interstitials sets how long the plateau lasts (pp. 490–495), which is the picture Figure 4 draws. Either way more dose gives more TED, as eq. (9.3) states.
  • Rp: the surface is where the interstitials are annihilated, and the nearer they start to it, the sooner they reach it and the shorter the transient. This linear dependence has been demonstrated experimentally, and it is the reason a low-energy implant shows less TED: the notes' "with low energy implants one can minimize transient enhanced diffusion" (p. 86). Below 1 keV TED falls further, because each ion does less damage.
  • The negative activation energy. The supersaturation sustained by the extended defects is larger at a lower temperature, so the extra depth grows as the temperature falls. Defects annealed out slowly at a low temperature give a deeper junction than the same defects annealed out quickly at a high one. That is one of the main reasons the source–drain anneal is done in a rapid thermal annealer, which ramps through the low temperatures in seconds, rather than in a furnace that ramps at a few degrees per minute (Wolf p. 210; M10 develops the anneal).

The notes' list of the advantages of low-energy implantation reads "Transient Enhanced Diffusion (TED) occuring during implantation is reduced" (p. 85). The notes' conclusion — a lower-energy implant gives less TED — is right, for the reason eq. (9.3) gives.

4.4 TED in the channel: the reverse short-channel effect

The source and drain implants are heavy, and their damage sits on both sides of the channel. During their anneal the interstitials they release flow toward the nearest sink, which for the channel region is the silicon–oxide interface under the gate. That flow sets up a gradient of interstitials pointing toward the surface, and boron, which moves with interstitials, is carried "uphill" toward the interface and piles up under the gate (Plummer p. 436). The pile-up is a larger fraction of the channel in a short device, whose source and drain damage lies closer together, so the threshold voltage rises as the gate length falls. This is the reverse short-channel effect: a slight increase of threshold voltage with decreasing channel length, before punch-through and charge sharing take over at the shortest lengths (Plummer p. 499; Wolf p. 221). It works directly against a retrograde profile, because it moves channel dopant back toward the surface.

Why indium helps here: the sources disagree on the mechanism

Both books say indium channels suffer less reverse short-channel effect; they give different reasons. Wolf attributes it to "the lower susceptibility of these species to TED" (p. 221), and shows SIMS profiles of indium that change little for anneals up to 1000 °C (p. 193, Fig. 5-11a). Plummer states the opposite about TED: indium's diffusion is "very similar to boron, including identical susceptibility to TED", and the benefit comes from indium's strong segregation to the oxide interface — some indium is lost there, but no further indium piles up during TED, so the profile stays retrograde (p. 499). Griffin et al. measured indium's enhancement from implant damage as comparable to boron's (M02 §5.3). The course record is therefore: the notes' reasons for indium are its smaller straggle and its lower diffusivity (p. 76); the straggle advantage holds for the as-implanted profile (§2); the diffusivity advantage holds for an undamaged lattice, and whether it survives TED is disputed in the sources.

Two anneals are proposed for a shallow boron implant: a furnace that ramps to 1000 °C at a few degrees per minute, and a rapid thermal anneal that ramps at 100 °C/s to the same temperature. Which gives the shallower junction, and which term of eq. (9.3) decides it? Would lowering the implant energy help the furnace process, and why?

The rapid anneal gives the shallower junction. The extended defects begin to dissolve during the ramp, at temperatures well below 1000 °C, and eq. (9.3)'s negative activation energy says that defects dissolving at a lower temperature produce more extra depth (by 3.28 times in ΔXj between 800 and 1000 °C). The furnace spends minutes in that range; the rapid anneal passes through it in seconds, so more of the defects dissolve near the peak temperature. Lowering the energy helps either process through the Rp factor: the interstitials start nearer the surface, reach it sooner, and the transient is shorter (ΔXj ∝ Rp1/2 at fixed dose and temperature).

5 · Super-steep retrograde profiles

§1 showed that the punch-through doping cannot sit at the surface, and §2–§4 what limits how sharply an implant can be confined below it. This section puts the two together. It first shows, with the electrostatics of a step profile, why a light surface layer over a heavy one lowers the threshold voltage while barely lengthening the depletion layer; then how the profile was made by implantation from the 0.18 µm to the 0.1 µm node (notes pp. 75–76); and finally why implantation probably cannot make the profile the 50 nm node needs.

5.1 What a light surface layer does to the threshold voltage

The two equations say opposite things about the two quantities a designer cares about. The depletion width grows only as the square root of a sum, so a surface layer thinner than the uniform depletion width adds little to it. The depletion-charge term of VT loses the charge that would have been in the surface layer, qNAxs/Cox, less the extra charge in the slightly deeper depletion layer; for xs much smaller than the uniform depletion width the second part is negligible and the whole of qNAxs/Cox is removed (Taur & Ning p. 250). Taur and Ning summarise it: the threshold voltage is reduced and the depletion width increased, the opposite of a high–low profile (p. 249). Figure 5 shows the mechanism through the field. The band bending at threshold is 2φF in both cases, which is the area under the field–depth line; in the undoped layer the field is constant, so the same area is reached with a lower surface field, and the surface field is what the oxide must support, εSiEs = Cox × (the depletion term).

In the limit of a very heavy layer, xs ≫ (4εSiφF/qNA)1/2, the depletion charge becomes a thin sheet at depth xs, Wdm ≈ xs, and the depletion term tends to εSi(2φF/xs)/Cox: half the value of a uniformly doped channel with the same depletion width. Taur and Ning call this the extreme retrograde profile, and the device a ground-plane MOSFET; its threshold voltage, subthreshold slope and short-channel behaviour are then set by the depth of the undoped layer rather than by the doping (§6.3.3.3). They add that the heavy layer should not extend under the source and drain, where it would raise the junction capacitance, so the ideal profile is low–high–low: a narrow heavy layer used only to confine the gate depletion. That remark is where the pocket implants of §6 begin.

5.2 Making it by implantation, 0.18 to 0.1 µm

From the 0.18 µm node to about 0.1 µm the SSR profile was made by ion implantation, in place of the conventional threshold-adjust implant (Wolf p. 193) — the 30 keV, 5.5×10¹² cm⁻² boron step at the end of M08's chained recipe (notes p. 74; M08 §6.3). The notes (p. 75) give the substitution: indium replaces boron for the n-channel device, and arsenic or antimony replaces phosphorus for the p-channel device. Their reasons (p. 76) are the two of §2 and §4: a steeper as-implanted profile from the smaller straggle, and less spreading in later anneals from the lower diffusivity.

With indium and arsenic implanted at 100–200 keV the profile reaches about 5×10¹⁶ cm⁻³ at the gate-oxide interface and a peak of about 10¹⁸ cm⁻³ 80 nm below it (notes p. 76; Wolf p. 193). Table 1 places an indium peak at 80 nm at about 160 keV, inside that range, and the §2 example is consistent with the low surface value: a Gaussian indium profile with that peak contributes only 3×10¹⁴ cm⁻³ at the surface, far below 5×10¹⁶ cm⁻³, so the implant's own Gaussian tail does not set it. The sources do not say what does; the well doping and diffusion during later anneals are candidates (a channelling tail lies on the deep side of the peak, §3, so it is not). Two limits come with indium. Its solid solubility caps the peak at about 10¹⁸ cm⁻³ (Wolf p. 193), and it is a deep acceptor, only partly ionised at room temperature (M02 §5.3).

Wolf is careful about the result. SSR profiles reduce short-channel effects and drain-induced barrier lowering, but "it is not equally certain whether this translates into overall improvement in device performance", and the subthreshold slope was reported improved in some indium studies and degraded in another (p. 193). One data point: a 100 nm process reported in 1999, with indium and arsenic SSR channels, low-energy boron and arsenic extensions and high-angle pocket implants, gave on-currents of 1.04 mA/µm (n-channel) and 0.46 mA/µm (p-channel) at 1.5 V with an off-current of 3 nA/µm (Wolf pp. 193–194).

5.3 The indium ion source

The notes record that the indium beam is produced in the ion source's vaporiser from solid InCl₃ (p. 76). Wolf explains why this is a problem (pp. 194–195). InCl₃ is hygroscopic: it absorbs water from the air and may need long outgassing if it is handled outside the vaporiser. It also has a low melting point, so heat from a second vaporiser in the same source can evaporate it unintentionally. Both can lead to premature loss of the source charge and to indium coating the source region. In operation, indium drifts down the beamline and coats its parts, from which ions of other species later knock it loose: a risk of cross-contamination of wafers implanted afterwards. Active cooling of the source removes the unintended heating, and an electrostatic filter near the end station removes most of the cross-contamination, which lets one implanter run all species.

5.4 Why implantation stops working below 0.1 µm

The notes close the topic with a number (p. 76, from Wolf p. 195). For the 50 nm node, a surface layer at 10¹⁷ cm⁻³ over a punch-through-control layer at 10¹⁹ cm⁻³ requires the heavy layer to begin 7 nm below the silicon surface. The inversion layer is typically thinner than 7 nm, so the channel electrons still sit in the light layer. But a layer this thin with so abrupt a step cannot be made by ion implantation and its anneals, and other methods are needed, such as low-temperature selective epitaxy, perhaps of Si–Ge layers. Lecture 17 gave the same two doping levels. The notes state the limit outright ("it is not possible"); Wolf writes that it "probably will no longer be possible". 6 · Pocket implants

The SSR layer varies with depth and is the same everywhere along the channel. Punch-through, though, starts at the two ends of the channel, where the source and drain depletion regions reach in. The notes' second structure puts the extra doping only there: "lateral tailoring of the channel doping profile" (p. 89). M02 §5.4 introduced the halo and the pocket and their order in the flow; this section quantifies the tilt, explains how a pocket counteracts the fall of threshold voltage in short devices, and works through what it does to the junction capacitance.

6.1 Doping only where punch-through starts

The notes (p. 89) name the structure a large-angle tilt implanted punch-through stopper, LATIPS, also known as a pocket implant. It "puts additional dopants in the channel close to the S/D junctions for punchthrough control and leaves much of the channel region with lighter doping as needed for low VT". Its peak doping must be somewhat higher than a punch-through-control layer uniform along the channel would need (p. 89). If the same structure is made with the beam normal to the wafer it is called a halo (Wolf p. 219, footnote; M02 §5.4). The usage is not uniform: Plummer calls a 30° tilted implant a halo (p. 458), and Taur and Ning use "halo or pocket" for one structure (p. 253). All the sources agree that both names mean doping placed along the channel, at its ends.

Placing the doping at the channel ends has a second effect, which Taur and Ning describe (§6.3.3.4). Lithography prints gates with a spread of lengths about the design value. In a short gate the two pockets lie closer together and occupy a larger fraction of the channel, so the average doping, and with it the threshold voltage, is higher than in a long one (Figure 7). Short-channel effects act the other way: as the channel shortens, the source and drain take over part of the depletion charge, and the threshold voltage falls (threshold roll-off, M07 §5.1). The pocket's rise therefore offsets the roll-off over the spread of gate lengths; with an optimised two-dimensional profile, the "superhalo", Taur and Ning state that it is possible in principle to hold the threshold essentially constant for channel lengths within 20–30 nm.

6.2 The tilt, the order of steps and the quad implant

The pocket is implanted after the source–drain extension implant and before the spacer is formed, so that the gate alone masks it and it is self-aligned to the gate (notes pp. 89–90). Its dopant is the same type as the well: boron or indium for an n-channel device, an n-type dopant for a p-channel one (the notes name antimony). The tilt exists to give the pocket "greater lateral penetration… than the adjacent SDE implant without thermal diffusion"; typical tilts are 20–45° from the wafer normal (notes p. 90). Wolf states the aim: the pocket's dopant should lie outside the final extension profile after implant and anneal (p. 221). Because gates on a chip run in two perpendicular directions, the dose is split into four equal segments with the wafer rotated 90° between them (notes p. 90), so that every gate edge receives a beam tilted toward it (Figure 8) — the quad implant M08 met for its wells (M08 §6.1).

6.3 What the pocket buys

The notes (p. 90) give two results: junction capacitance reduced by 65 %, and a steeper subthreshold slope. Wolf writes "up to 65 %" and explains both: they follow from the controlled doping under the gate edge and "the near elimination of all other undesired doping" (p. 221). A punch-through-control layer uniform along the channel also lies under the source and drain, so their junctions face heavy doping; with pockets, the junction bottoms face the light well, and the channel centre is lightly doped. Taur and Ning's low–high–low remark (§5.1) is the same point. Wolf adds a third effect: the pocket's opposite-type dopant compensates part of the extension, which keeps the extension junction shallower (p. 220).

The capacitance follows from the junction electrostatics of M08 §5.2: a one-sided junction has Cj = εSi/W, and W ∝ NA−1/2 (eq. (2.4)), so Cj grows roughly as NA1/2. The subthreshold slope follows from M07 eq. (7.7), St = 2.3(kT/q)(1 + Cd/Cox): a lighter channel centre has a wider depletion layer, a smaller depletion capacitance Cd and a swing closer to the ideal 2.3 kT/q, about 60 mV/decade at 300 K.

The principle that the table encodes is to put each dopant only where it does its job. The threshold voltage and the mobility are set in the top few nanometres, punch-through tens of nanometres down and at the channel ends, and the junction capacitance under the source and drain. A uniform profile spends dopant everywhere to satisfy the most demanding of these places. The steeper the profile and the more precisely placed, the less it costs; the limits are the straggle (§2), channelling (§3), TED (§4) and, below 0.1 µm, the number of atoms (§5.4).

Write the half-page answer: "uniform channel doping — its problem at a 0.13 µm gate — the profile that fixes it — that profile's problem at 50 nm — the fix". Name the species and give one number at each stage.

At 0.13 µm, punch-through control needs a mean channel doping above 5×10¹⁷ cm⁻³ (eq. (9.1) gives 6.9×10¹⁷ at 1.2 V), but the surface must stay below 5×10¹⁷ cm⁻³ for mobility; a uniform profile cannot do both. The fix is a super-steep retrograde profile, light at the surface and heavy below, implanted with indium in place of boron (n-channel) and arsenic or antimony in place of phosphorus (p-channel), indium and arsenic at 100–200 keV giving about 5×10¹⁶ cm⁻³ at the oxide and 10¹⁸ cm⁻³ at 80 nm. Heavy ions are used because their straggle is about half boron's at the same depth (18.9 against 37.9 nm), so the profile is steeper, and their diffusivity is lower. At 50 nm the light layer must be only 7 nm thick over 10¹⁹ cm⁻³; boron's flank needs 22 nm even at 2 keV and indium cannot exceed about 10¹⁸ cm⁻³, so implantation, by Wolf's hedged judgement, probably cannot make it, and the light layer holds only about two atoms under the gate. The fix the sources point to is a method other than implantation, likely low-temperature selective epitaxy, perhaps of Si–Ge, with the surface layer left effectively undoped — and pockets at the channel ends for the lateral part of punch-through control.

Summary

  • Punch-through needs the mean channel doping above 5×10¹⁷ cm⁻³ below about 0.13 µm; mobility needs the doping at the surface below 5×10¹⁷ cm⁻³ (notes p. 75, Wolf p. 191). Only a retrograde profile meets both. Minimum uniform doping from L > 2W: eq. (9.1).
  • An implant's flank falls a decade in 2.15 ΔRp (eq. (9.2)). Heavy ions straggle less: at ≈ 76 nm depth, indium 18.9 nm against boron 37.9 nm; antimony against phosphorus likewise.
  • Channelling: ions steered down open lattice directions leave a deep tail, worst for low doses. Reduced by a 7–10° tilt and rotation (at the cost of shadowing), a screen oxide, low energy and high dose.
  • TED: the "+1" excess interstitials cluster into {311} defects whose dissolution keeps boron's diffusivity enhanced until they are gone. ΔXj² ∝ N Rp exp(+1.4 eV/kT): less TED at low energy and with fast, hot anneals. It drives the reverse short-channel effect.
  • A light surface layer of thickness xs removes up to qNAxs/Cox from VT (all of it when xs ≪ Wm) while Wdm grows only as (Wm² + xs²)1/2 (eqs. (9.4)–(9.5)); in the extreme limit the depletion term is half that of a uniform channel of the same depletion width (ground plane).
  • SSR by implantation, 0.18–0.1 µm: In for B, As or Sb for P; In and As at 100–200 keV, ≈ 5×10¹⁶ cm⁻³ at the oxide and 10¹⁸ cm⁻³ at 80 nm; indium capped near 10¹⁸ cm⁻³, sourced from solid InCl₃. At 50 nm the 7 nm step cannot be implanted; low-temperature selective epitaxy is the likely route (Wolf).
  • Pockets (LATIPS): after the extension, before the spacer, well-type dopant, 20–45° tilt in four 90° segments. They place punch-through doping at the channel ends, offset threshold roll-off, cut junction capacitance by up to 65 % and steepen the subthreshold slope.
