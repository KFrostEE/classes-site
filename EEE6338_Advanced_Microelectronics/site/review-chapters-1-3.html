1 · The argument of Chapters 1–3

Chapters 1 to 3 of the notes make one argument. The gate length of the MOSFET fell from 10 µm in 1970 to 0.25–0.13 µm by 2001–2002, in the notes' dating, because drain current rises as the channel shortens and a shorter device occupies less silicon. A gate cannot simply be shortened: the drain then competes with it for control of the channel, so the rest of the transistor has to change with it. Chapter 1 traces those changes along the four axes the notes name — the gate stack, the gate dielectric, the channel doping profile and the drain structure — and writes down the threshold-voltage equation that ties the gate material, the oxide and the channel doping to one number. Chapter 2 is the substrate: a dislocation-free single crystal grown, sliced and polished to a specification that tightens with each generation, and enlarged to 300 mm because most processing is paid for per wafer. Chapter 3 follows the gate-dielectric axis to its end. The oxide thins in step with the gate length, and as it approaches 2 nm it begins to fail as an insulator, in the five ways the notes label on one gate stack; the chapter's growth methods and chemistries kept silicon dioxide in service a little longer, and Chapter 4 replaces it.

Sections 2 to 5 take the chapters in turn, Chapter 3 in two halves, and each has the same three parts. The facts and lists come first: tables of what the notes enumerate or quantify, in the notes' wording and numbering and with the notes' values, each row linked to the module section that explains it; anything a row adds from a lecture or a module is attributed in that row. Where a module flags a notes value against the literature, the row keeps the notes' value and carries the flag. The explanations follow as prompts, each opening onto a reference answer of one paragraph, with a sketch where the idea is spatial, and a link to the full treatment. The problems close each section: the numerical types worked in the course, with new numbers, solved step by step in the modules' notation and with their constants.

2 · Chapter 1: MOSFET structures

Chapter 1 of the notes (pp. 1–12) is a map of the deep-submicron transistor rather than a primer on the device. It opens with the gate-length history, follows the structure along four axes — gate stack, gate dielectric, channel doping profile, drain structure — adds the substrate, the doping of the gate and the isolation between devices, and closes on the limits of the conventional structure below 0.1 µm. The device it assumes is the subject of M01, background built from the lecture; M02 is the chapter itself; and M03 takes apart the threshold-voltage equation the notes write beside the change of gate material, which is where most of the chapter's arithmetic lives. Nearly every entry points forward: the gate-dielectric axis is the rest of this page and Chapter 4, and the channel and drain axes return in later chapters.

2.2 Explain in brief

What is a self-aligned gate, and why could the aluminium gate not be self-aligned?

In the aluminium-gate process the source and drain are diffused first, through windows in a masking oxide, and the gate is patterned afterwards by a separate lithography step. Diffusion spreads the dopant sideways under the window edges, and the gate has to overlap both diffused regions by more than the alignment tolerance between the two masks so that no ungated gap is left; wherever it lies over n⁺ silicon, the gate oxide forms an overlap capacitance that is charged and discharged on every transition. In the self-aligned process the gate oxide and a polysilicon gate are patterned first, and the source and drain are then implanted with the gate itself as the mask. Ions reach the silicon only where the gate is absent, so the junction edges fall at the gate edges by construction, the residual overlap is only the lateral spread of the implant and its anneal, and the same implant dopes the gate. Aluminium cannot take that sequence: it melts at 660 °C, below the anneal that activates the implant, so an aluminium gate must go down after the source and drain are finished.

Full treatment: M02 §3.1; the aluminium-gate flow is M01 §7.3.

Why did polysilicon replace aluminium as the gate, and why was a silicide then added to it?

The course's reason is the first term of the threshold equation, which the notes write beside the change. Aluminium has one work function, 4.1 eV in this course, so an aluminium gate fixes φMS for a given body doping. Polysilicon is silicon, and its work function is set by its doping, from 4.05 eV with the Fermi level at the conduction band edge (n⁺) to 5.17 eV at the valence band edge (p⁺); doping the gate turns φMS into a process variable. Polysilicon also survives the source–drain anneal, which is what makes the self-aligned gate possible. The notes date the change to the mid-1970s, by when polysilicon was the standard gate; the first commercial silicon-gate process, in 1968, was built for both reasons. The cost is resistance: even heavily doped with phosphorus, polysilicon lines below 1.25 µm gate length were too resistive for local interconnect. Tungsten silicide on top, WSix, made the polycide and cut the resistance to one fourth in the notes' figure (the literature puts the gain at five to ten times). In deep-submicron CMOS, TiSi₂ or CoSi₂ replaced it: lower resistivity, and formed on the source and drain in the same step as on the gate — the self-aligned silicide, salicide, which also lowers the contact resistance. The polysilicon stays against the oxide, because the work function is set by what touches it.

Full treatment: M02 §3 and M03 §8.

What is punch-through, and what does raising the body doping do to it?

Each n⁺ source and drain forms a junction with the p-type body, and its depletion region lies almost entirely in the lightly doped body, because the two sides hold equal and opposite charge (NAWp = NDWn) and the n⁺ side is doped far more heavily. The drain junction carries the drain voltage as reverse bias and is the wider. Punch-through is the merging of the two depletion regions beneath the gate: no neutral body remains between them, the drain potential reaches across to lower the barrier that holds the source's electrons out of the channel, and a current flows that the gate cannot turn off. The professor's criterion is L > 2W. Because W = [2εSi(Vbi + VR)/(qNA)]1/2, the width falls as NA−1/2: more body doping narrows both regions and permits a shorter channel — ten times the doping, a channel 3.2 times shorter. The price is mobility, because the larger depletion charge raises the field normal to the surface, and the peak field at the drain junction rises as well. That is why the later profiles put the extra dopant only where the depletion regions meet.

Full treatment: M02 §5.2.

What is a super-steep retrograde channel profile, and why are indium and arsenic or antimony used for it?

A retrograde profile is one whose doping rises with depth. The extra doping that holds off punch-through is needed where the source and drain depletion regions approach each other, below the surface; at the surface, where the inversion layer carries the current, it lowers the mobility. A super-steep retrograde (SSR) profile separates the two places vertically: light doping at the surface, then a steep rise to a heavy peak beneath it. The notes state what it achieves — the correct VT without degrading the channel mobility, by keeping the doping in the inversion layer below 5×10¹⁷ cm⁻³. A profile that steep must survive the anneals that follow, so the notes specify heavier species: indium replaces boron for n-channel devices, and arsenic or antimony replaces phosphorus for p-channel devices, because they have smaller diffusivities (the notes' reason, which M02 qualifies for a lattice damaged by implantation). A heavier ion also stops with a narrower straggle, so the profile is steeper as implanted; the depth of its peak is chosen by the implant energy, not by the ion's mass.

Full treatment: M02 §5.3.

What is a halo implant, where does it sit, and what does the tilted pocket implant add?

A blanket anti-punch-through implant raises the doping along the whole channel, and in deep-submicron devices that degrades performance: the threshold rises and the mobility falls everywhere, although punch-through begins only at the ends of the channel. The halo puts the extra dopant only at the edge of the drain-extension region, around the extension's tip, much as a halo surrounds a head in a medieval painting, and leaves the rest of the channel with the lighter doping a low threshold needs. It is self-aligned, because the gate blocks the ions from the channel it covers. The species is the one already in the well — boron for an n-channel device — implanted at an energy somewhat higher than the lightly doped drain implant, so its greater range and lateral straggle carry it beyond the extension it surrounds. The later form directs the beam at a large angle to the wafer: the ions enter the silicon beside the gate and come to rest beneath its edge, so the peak sits at a chosen depth and lateral distance from the gate edge. The notes call it the large-angle tilted implanted punch-through structure, LATIPS, or pocket implant; Wolf gives 20–45° from normal, in four segments with the wafer turned 90° between them so that every gate edge receives a beam.

Full treatment: M02 §5.4.

What is implanted to adjust VT, and why that one species for both n-channel and p-channel devices?

Boron, in both. The threshold-adjust implant changes the doping only in the first fraction of a micrometre, inside the depletion region, so it acts through the depletion term −Qd/Cox. In an n-channel device the body is p-type and that term is positive; boron is an acceptor, so it raises NA, increases |Qd| and enlarges the positive term, moving VT upward to the positive value a logic device needs. In a p-channel device the body is n-type and the same term is negative; boron compensates part of the donors, lowers the net donor concentration and |Qd|, and shrinks the negative term, so VT again moves upward, to a smaller negative value. Both devices need that direction, because with an aluminium or n⁺ polysilicon gate the work-function and interface-charge terms are negative for both. Phosphorus would raise the p-channel donor concentration and drive its threshold further negative. One species and one implant step therefore serve both devices. The net doping after compensation is a subtraction of concentrations: 10¹⁶ − 10¹⁵ = 9×10¹⁵ cm⁻³.

Full treatment: M03 §9.1.

Why does the threshold implant go through an oxide — what is the two-junction problem?

An implanted profile does not start at the surface. Each ion comes to rest at a random depth, and the resting depths gather into a roughly Gaussian profile centred at the projected range Rp below the surface, falling away on both sides with the straggle ΔRp. A junction forms wherever an implant of the type opposite to the background crosses the background concentration NB. Implanted into bare silicon, a profile whose peak sits deep enough falls below NB on its shallow side as well as its deep side, so it crosses twice: two junctions, with a buried layer of the implanted type between a thin surface layer and the substrate. That happens when Rp > ΔRp√(2 ln(Cp/NB)). The lecture's remedy is to implant through an oxide about Rp thick, which puts the peak at the Si/SiO₂ interface: the shallow half of the profile lies in the oxide, where there is no junction to form, and in the silicon the concentration only falls, crossing NB once. A threshold implant of boron into p-type silicon forms no junction at all; the problem belongs to an opposite-type implant, such as boron into the n-type body of a p-channel device. The textbooks give the screen oxide a second job — suppressing channelling and protecting the surface — and in both accounts the profile in the silicon ends up shallower by about the oxide thickness.

Full treatment: M03 §9.2.

What is the lightly doped drain, why was it needed, and what are the steps that build it?

As channels reached 2 µm, the field in the drain depletion region grew large enough that electrons crossing it gained energy from the field faster than they lost it to the lattice. These hot electrons surmounted the Si/SiO₂ barrier, broke bonds at the interface and were trapped in the oxide near the drain: hot electron injection, which the notes record as long-term reliability degradation. A peak field falls if the same voltage is dropped over a longer distance, so a lightly doped region, which supports a wider depletion layer, is placed between the channel and the heavily doped drain: the LDD, about 10¹⁷ cm⁻³ in the notes, beside a 10²⁰ cm⁻³ contact region. The sidewall spacer puts the two in place without lithography. Lecture 4's five steps, then the anneal: (1) pattern the polysilicon gate; (2) implant phosphorus for the LDD with a medium-current implanter, the gate aligning it; (3) deposit a conformal SiO₂ film by LPCVD; (4) etch the film by anisotropic RIE, which clears the flat surfaces and leaves spacers on the gate sidewalls; (5) implant the n⁺ contact with a high-current implanter, the spacer shielding the LDD. A single anneal then activates both implants and drives the LDD edge the proper distance under the gate. The notes count the same flow as four steps, starting from the patterned gate and joining deposition and etch. Three details of the lecture's account are not supported by the written sources, and M02 §6.1 flags each: the lecture patterned the spacer etch with a mask, where the spacer is formed by a maskless etch-back whose film thickness sets its width; it credited the spacer's rounded face to an 800–900 °C reflow anneal, where the rounding is the shape of the conformal film; and no textbook confirms its deposition temperature for silane with nitrous oxide.

Full treatment: M02 §6.1.

Why did the source/drain extension replace the lightly doped drain, and how do the two differ?

The LDD cured hot electron injection at a price: a lightly doped region is resistive, and it sits in series with the channel. The notes record the supply reduced from 5 V to 2 V at 0.18 µm (the nominal supply at that generation was 1.8 V, reached in steps). The drain field falls with the voltage, so hot electron injection no longer had to be controlled by an LDD, while the LDD's share of the on-resistance had become unacceptably large. The two-region drain was kept and its near region redefined: the implant dose rose from 1–5×10¹³ cm⁻² to 5–10×10¹⁴ cm⁻², ten to a hundred times as much (about 25 times between the middles of the two ranges), so its peak doping is as high as the contact region's and its resistivity far lower. Since it is no longer lightly doped, the notes rename it the source/drain extension, SDE. What separates it from the contact region is depth: the SDE junction is shallower, and that shallowness is now its purpose, because it limits how far the drain field reaches along the channel. The scaling-limits table returns to the same trade at 30 nm junction depth, where the extension's resistance, RSDE, sets the limit.

Full treatment: M02 §6.2.

Why does an n⁺ polysilicon gate make the p-channel device a buried-channel device, and how does dual-doped polysilicon fix it?

Degenerately doped n⁺ and p⁺ polysilicon have work functions near the conduction and valence band edges, a band gap apart, so on an n-type well an n⁺ gate gives a φMS about 1.1 V more negative than a p⁺ gate would, and the p-channel threshold is about 1.1 V more negative with it — too negative. The correction is the boron threshold implant, and in the n-well boron counter-dopes: a dose of order 10¹² cm⁻² confined to the top 0.1 µm is of order 10¹⁷ cm⁻³, more than the well's donors, so a thin layer just beneath the oxide turns p-type. That layer is the channel. The gate depletes its upper part and the holes flow in its lower part, below the interface: a buried channel, which the gate reaches only through a depletion layer and which turns off only when the gate's depletion meets the depletion of the p-layer/n-well junction. The drain competes for it more effectively than for a surface channel, and the notes record off-state leakage that is unacceptably large at 0.25 µm gate lengths. A p⁺ gate on the p-channel device raises its threshold by about 1.1 V, so no counter-doping is needed and the holes gather at the surface: n⁺ poly over the n-channel device, p⁺ over the p-channel device — dual-doped polysilicon. Its costs, in the notes: extra process steps, boron penetration through the gate oxide, and boron counter-doping the poly along shared lines.

Full treatment: M02 §8; the φMS values for each gate are in M03 §8.

What are the four terms of the threshold voltage, what is each physically, and what sign does each take for each device type?

VT = φMS − Qi/Cox − Qd/Cox + 2φF, every quantity per unit area and in centimetre units. φMS is the gate-to-body work function difference: two materials in contact equalise their Fermi levels, a built-in potential appears if their work functions differ, and this much gate voltage is consumed cancelling it; it is negative for aluminium and n⁺ polysilicon gates on either body. −Qi/Cox is the voltage needed to cancel the charge at the oxide–silicon boundary, Qi = qNi with Ni between 5×10¹⁰ and 10¹³ cm⁻²; the charge is positive, so the term is negative for both devices. −Qd/Cox is the voltage that supports the ionised dopants exposed in the depletion region at its maximum width Wm: acceptors are negative, so the term is positive for an n-channel device; donors are positive, so it is negative for a p-channel device. +2φF is the band bending at strong inversion, where the surface is as strongly inverted as the bulk is doped; positive for a p-type body, negative for an n-type body. An n-channel device therefore has two terms of each sign that partly cancel (− − + +), a p-channel device four negative terms that add (− − − −), which is why the p-channel threshold was historically the larger and set the supply. Because Cox divides both charge terms, thinning the oxide shrinks both.

Full treatment: M03 §1–6; the signs are tabulated in M03 §6.

Why was LOCOS replaced by shallow trench isolation?

Neighbouring transistors are isolated by a thick oxide over all the silicon where no transistor is built, so that a metal line crossing it is too far from the silicon to invert it. LOCOS grows that oxide locally: a thin pad oxide (10–50 nm), a silicon nitride mask patterned over the future active areas, then thermal oxidation, which proceeds wherever the nitride is absent because nitride is nearly impermeable to oxygen. The notes record that LOCOS served until the 0.35 µm generation and name its two failures. The first is the bird's beak: oxidant reaching the nitride edge diffuses sideways through the pad oxide and oxidises the silicon under the nitride, and the volume expansion of oxidation, about 2.2 times, lifts the nitride edge into a taper. The field oxide thus encroaches into the active area by an amount the oxidation sets, not the mask. The second is topography: a grown, semi-recessed field oxide stands partly above the original surface and leaves a non-planar wafer for lithography and metal to cross. STI etches a trench, fills it with deposited oxide and polishes the fill flat: no silicon is consumed sideways, so there is no bird's beak, and the surface is planar.

Full treatment: M02 §9.

3.2 Explain in brief

How is sand turned into electronic-grade polysilicon, and which step does the purifying?

Sand is silicon dioxide. Carbothermic reduction in an electric arc furnace, SiO₂ + 2C → Si + 2CO with charcoal as the carbon, leaves molten silicon that freezes polycrystalline: metallurgical-grade silicon, about 98% pure. That is useless for a device, since 1% of impurity is about 5×10²⁰ cm⁻³ against doping levels of 10¹⁵ cm⁻³. A solid is hard to purify, so the silicon is turned into a liquid: crushed and reacted with hydrogen chloride, Si + 3HCl ⇌ SiHCl₃ + H₂, it gives trichlorosilane, which boils at 31.8 °C and comes out about 99% pure, because most metallic impurities form no volatile chloride or one with a very different boiling point. Fractional distillation, repeatable as often as needed, does nearly all the purifying, to better than 99.9999% — the class's six nines; electronic grade is specified at nine to eleven. The Siemens reactor runs the reaction backwards on a heated high-purity silicon rod and deposits the purified silicon onto it without purifying it further. The rod, broken into chunks, is still polycrystalline: purity and crystallinity are separate problems, and the crystal is grown next.

Full treatment: M04 §1 and §2.

How does Czochralski growth make a dislocation-free single crystal, and what limits how fast it can be pulled?

Chunks of electronic-grade polysilicon are melted in a quartz (fused silica) crucible, about 30 inches across for 300 mm growth, which softens at the melt temperature and sits in a graphite susceptor. The melt is held a few degrees above 1414 °C under a flowing inert gas. A seed crystal is dipped into the melt and withdrawn; silicon freezing onto it adopts its lattice, so the boule is one crystal with the seed's orientation. The seed is rotated and the crucible counter-rotated, which averages the temperature the freezing front sees around its circumference and keeps the diameter uniform. Contact with the melt puts dislocations into the seed, so the crystal is first pulled fast into a thin neck at least ten times as long as it is wide; the dislocations glide out to its surface on inclined {111} planes, and everything grown afterwards is dislocation-free. The latent heat released at the freezing front must leave through the crystal, and since that heat and its conduction both scale with the cross-section, the pull rate is limited by the axial temperature gradient, which the crystal's radiation from its cylindrical surface sets. A larger diameter therefore needs a slower pull — v ∝ 1/D in the notes, D−1/2 by the heat balance — and a 300 mm crystal takes up to 100 hours from a 150 kg charge. The silica crucible dissolves into the melt, so every Czochralski crystal contains oxygen.

Full treatment: M04 §3.

Why did wafers grow to 300 mm, and what did the change cost?

A wafer is processed as a unit: one furnace run, one implant or one wet clean treats every die on it, so the cost of those steps depends on the number of wafers and not on their size. A larger wafer spreads that per-wafer cost over more dies, and the gain goes as the area, (300/200)² = 2.25 — slightly more in usable dies, because the excluded edge is a smaller fraction of a larger wafer. The substrate pulls the other way. It costs more per unit area on the larger wafer — in the notes' table $2/in² at 200 mm against $3/in² at 300 mm, half as much again (the wafer-cost column implies nearer 1.9×) — so the silicon under each die costs more, however many dies the wafer carries. The cost per die falls because processing, not the bare substrate, is the larger part of the cost of a finished wafer (Wolf projects the 300 mm substrate at as much as 30% of it): the condition is that the manufacturing cost per wafer rise by less than the area, 125%. The steps that gain least are those whose cost already scales with area, such as lithographic exposures repeated field by field. The change had its own costs, in the notes: most 200 mm tools — crystal pullers, grinders, polishers, cleaners, inspection stations — were unsuitable; the quartz crucible grows to about 30 inches and must survive four days at the melt temperature; the pull slows; and the wafer is 50 µm, or 7%, thicker, with its back surface polished.

Full treatment: M04 §4.

How do flats identify a wafer's orientation and doping type, and why did a notch replace them?

Once a boule is sliced, nothing distinguishes one face of a wafer or one rotational position from another, yet processing needs both the crystal orientation and the doping type. Flats are planes ground along the length of the boule before slicing, at positions found by X-ray diffraction, which identifies the crystal planes directly. The primary flat, the longer, is a reference direction: equipment registers wafers against it, so a whole boat is loaded in one orientation. The secondary flat, shorter, encodes orientation and type by its angle from the primary: 90° for (100) p-type, 180° for (100) n-type, none for (111) p-type, 45° for (111) n-type. Flats are used up to about 150 mm, where the notes give a 55–60 mm primary flat. At 200 mm and above a small notch in the edge replaces them, because a flat removes a chord of the disc that would otherwise hold dies — about six per flat on a 200 mm wafer, in the professor's estimate — while a notch identifies the crystal orientation just as well at almost no cost in area. The notes' wafers are (100) at every diameter.

Full treatment: M04 §6.1.

How does chemical mechanical polishing leave a silicon surface free of scratches and damage?

The wafer is mounted on a carrier and pressed face down against a polishing pad on a powered platen, both turning, while a slurry is dripped onto the pad: colloidal silica, particles about 10 nm across, in sodium hydroxide. Neither component works alone. The frictional heat of the sliding wafer lets the NaOH oxidise the silicon surface to a soft hydrated oxide, which reacts on to sodium silicates (the class's two reactions give Na₂SiO₃ and Na₄SiO₄). The silica particles then abrade that layer and expose fresh silicon, which reacts in turn, and the cycle repeats for the length of the polish. The particles must be harder than the oxidised layer and softer than silicon: amorphous colloidal silica, Knoop about 600 against silicon's 1150, is both, so material is removed without scratching. The roughness falls from about 2.5 µm after the etch to under 100 Å, a mirror surface free of damage. Double-side polishing gives 300 mm wafers better flatness, and plasma-assisted chemical etching (PACE) then corrects the local flatness by chemical removal, without the subsurface damage of reactive ion etching.

Full treatment: M04 §6.4.

4.2 Explain in brief

The prompts follow the order of the notes. Each answer is a reference explanation in a paragraph or two, with the sketch that belongs to it and a link to the module section that treats the idea in full.

Why must the gate oxide keep thinning as the gate shortens, and what does a thinner oxide cost?

As the gate shortens, the drain's field reaches along the channel and lowers the source barrier that the gate is meant to control — drain-induced barrier lowering — so the off-state subthreshold current rises. The notes' remedies are a shallower source/drain extension junction and a shallower channel depletion region, made by heavier channel doping: a heavier boron threshold-adjust implant in the n-channel device. That doping raises the depletion-charge term QD/Cox and with it VT; a thinner oxide raises Cox and brings VT back, and the industry held LE/tox ≈ 45. The thinner oxide also raises the drain current, through Cox.

The cost is the notes' four negative factors. A thinner film cannot tolerate the field, so the supply voltage must fall; tunnelling leakage rises; boron from a p⁺ gate penetrates more easily; and defects and thickness non-uniformity matter more, because a defect of fixed size, or a 1 Å variation, is a larger fraction of a thinner film. The notes' drawing adds a fifth concern, charge injection: the damage that carriers crossing the film leave in it over time.

Full treatment: M05 §2 and §3.

What is the SiO₄ tetrahedron, and how does a bridging oxygen join two of them?

Silicon forms four bonds and oxygen two. In silicon dioxide each silicon sits at the centre of a tetrahedron with an oxygen at each of its four corners, bonded through its four sp³ hybrids, so every O–Si–O angle is 109.5° and every Si–O bond is 1.62 Å long (notes p. 22). An oxygen uses one bond on its own silicon and the second on the silicon of the neighbouring tetrahedron. In the notes' words each oxygen "belongs to 2 tetrahedra and is thus bonded to 2 Si atoms": a bridging oxygen. Four oxygens per silicon, each shared by two silicons, gives 4 × ½ = 2 oxygens per silicon, the formula SiO₂.

The bridge is bent. The Si–O–Si angle ranges from 110° to 180°, most probably 144°, and the two silicons sit 3.12 Å apart (notes; 1.62 Å at 144° gives 3.08 Å). That separation is a distance between atoms that are not bonded to each other. The notes' flat drawing of an oxide silicon — one bond down, three oxygens fanned above — is this tetrahedron seen from the side: of the three upper oxygens, one lies in the page, one points toward the reader and one away.

Full treatment: M05a §3.

What is a non-bridging oxygen, and why is a dry-grown oxide more cohesive than a wet one?

Thermal oxide is a glass. Every tetrahedron is intact, but the bridge angles and the rotations about the bridges vary from site to site, so only short-range order survives. In this amorphous network "some of the vertices of the tetrahedra have non-bridging oxygen atoms" (notes p. 22): an oxygen bonded to one silicon only. Its second bond ends in a hydrogen (Si–OH), in an extra electron (Si–O⁻) with a positive ion such as Na⁺ held ionically beside it, or in an unpaired electron. Each bridging oxygen holds two tetrahedra together and each non-bridging oxygen is a place where the network stops, hence the notes' rule: "the greater the ratio of bridging to non bridging oxygens, the greater the cohesiveness of the SiO₂ structure."

Water breaks bridges, ≡Si–O–Si≡ + H₂O → 2 ≡Si–OH, so an oxide grown in steam contains OH and H₂O and fewer bridges; that is why dry oxide is the more cohesive (Lecture 7). Foreign atoms divide the same way: a network former such as phosphorus or boron takes the place of a silicon and bonds through bridging oxygens; a network modifier such as Na⁺ or K⁺ sits in the open spaces, held ionically.

Full treatment: M05a §3.

Why does growing an oxide consume a thickness of silicon equal to 0.46 of the oxide thickness?

A thermal oxide is made from the wafer. Isotope experiments show that new oxide forms at the Si/SiO₂ interface: the oxidant — O₂ in dry oxidation, H₂O in wet — diffuses inward through the oxide already grown and reacts there, so every silicon atom in the film was once in the crystal. Counting atoms under 1 cm² gives the split. Consuming a thickness xSi of silicon releases nSixSi atoms, and an oxide of thickness xox holds nSiO₂xox of them, so xSi = (nSiO₂/nSi) xox = (2.28×10²²/5.00×10²²) xox = 0.456 xox, the 0.46 of Lectures 8 and 9.

Each silicon atom occupies 20.0 Å³ in the crystal and 43.9 Å³ in the oxide, 2.2 times as much, and a film bonded to the substrate cannot spread sideways, so the whole expansion goes upward: the interface moves down by 0.46 xox and the top surface rises 0.54 xox above the original surface. Texts quote 0.44 to 0.46 because they assume different oxide densities; Wolf's 2.20 g/cm³ gives 0.44.

Full treatment: M05a §4; formula sheet (5a.3).

Name the four charges in and on a gate oxide, place each one, and say which two still matter.

The notes (p. 23) list four, each as a charge per area Q (C/cm²) or a number per area N (cm⁻²), Q = qN. Mobile ionic charge Qm is Na⁺ and K⁺ ions anywhere in the oxide, drifting under bias and temperature. Oxide trapped charge Qot is holes or electrons held at defects in the bulk of the oxide, so it has either sign. Fixed oxide charge Qf is positive and lies in the transition region, the partly oxidised layer, one or two atomic distances thick, between the crystal and the oxide; experimentally it appears as a sheet of charge within about 2 nm of the interface, perhaps closer (Plummer). Interface trapped charge Qit sits on the interface between the gate oxide and the channel silicon, in states inside the silicon gap whose charge follows the surface Fermi level, so it is also counted per unit energy, Dit (cm⁻² eV⁻¹).

The notes: "Reliability involves Qit and Qot and are ongoing. Qm and Qf are not well controlled. Their impact does not get any more severe as oxides are scaled." Wolf, which the notes condense, reads "not only well controlled". On either reading Qit and Qot are the two that still matter: hot carriers and current through the oxide go on creating them in service, while the amounts of Qm and Qf are fixed during manufacture.

Full treatment: M05b §1.

What is a dangling bond, and why does it put a state in the silicon band gap?

In crystalline silicon each atom forms four sp³ hybrid orbitals, and each pairs with a hybrid of a neighbour. Two overlapping hybrids give a bonding level, lower in energy, and an antibonding level, higher; across the crystal the bonding levels broaden into the valence band and the antibonding levels into the conduction band, 1.12 eV above. A surface silicon with no partner for its fourth hybrid — a trivalent silicon atom, in the notes' term (p. 24) — keeps one electron in an orbital that overlaps nothing. That orbital is the dangling bond. It is neither bonding nor antibonding, so its level lies between the two, inside the gap.

It can give up its electron (+), keep it (0) or accept a second (−), so its charge follows the Fermi level at the surface, which the gate moves: that is interface trapped charge. Electron spin resonance, which detects the unpaired electron of the neutral bond, identifies the interfacial dangling bond as the Pb centre. The notes place its level "near the Si midgap" (p. 26). On dry-oxidised (111) silicon, spin resonance places the two charge changes at Ev + 0.31 eV (+ to 0) and Ev + 0.80 eV (0 to −), and capacitance–voltage measurements find the interface-trap density peaking close to them, at Ev + 0.26 and + 0.84 eV; both pairs straddle midgap, Ev + 0.56 eV (Poindexter 1984).

Full treatment: M05b §3.1 and §3.2.

What are the Si–Si and Si–O stretched bonds at the interface, and how do they differ from a dangling bond?

At an ideal interface each surface silicon bonds up through a bridging oxygen to a silicon of the oxide. The notes (p. 25) draw three defects, each a change at one such site. In a dangling bond nothing is there: the surface silicon's bond ends on no atom. In a Si–Si stretched bond the bridging oxygen is missing — an oxygen vacancy — and the two silicons bond to each other directly. "Stretched" compares that bond with the 2.35 Å Si–Si bond of crystalline silicon: the two atoms, 3.12 Å apart while the oxygen bridged them, move toward each other, to about 2.5 Å in calculations for the oxide, and the network around them resists the contraction, leaving the bond strained and weak. In a Si–O stretched bond the oxygen is present and bonded but displaced from its equilibrium position, on a longer, weaker bond; nothing is missing and nothing is broken.

At the interface both stretched bonds give "a continuum of deep trap levels", and in the oxide bulk shallow levels (notes p. 26; theory and the measured U-shaped Dit place the stretched-bond levels away from midgap). The vacancy is also a hole trap.

Full treatment: M05b §3.4 and §3.5.

How does a hydrogen anneal passivate a dangling bond, and where in the process is it done?

Hydrogen gives the dangling bond a partner. In an anneal in "100% H₂ or 4% H₂ in N₂ forming gas" (notes p. 26), molecular hydrogen diffuses through the oxide to the interface, dissociates, and a hydrogen atom bonds to the trivalent silicon: H₂ → 2H, ≡Si• + H → ≡Si–H. The former dangling orbital and the hydrogen orbital combine into a filled bonding level below the valence-band edge and an empty antibonding level above the conduction-band edge, so no level is left in the gap and the site stops trapping carriers. The anneal takes the interface from the 6.8×10¹¹ cm⁻² of the notes' example toward 10¹⁰ cm⁻².

It runs at 400–450 °C (Lecture 7) as the last thermal step, after metallization: done earlier, the Si–H bonds would break and the hydrogen leave at the higher temperatures of later steps, and the 450 °C ceiling is set by the aluminium already on the wafer. Lecture 7 gave the mixture as 3% H₂ in argon; forming gas is hydrogen in nitrogen, and the carrier takes no part in the reaction. Fixed charge is unchanged.

Full treatment: M05b §4.1; formula sheet (5b.5).

Trace the hot-carrier degradation chain from a broken bond to a failed device.

A hot carrier is an electron or hole that a strong electric field has accelerated to a kinetic energy far above the thermal average; in a MOSFET this happens near the drain, where the lateral field peaks. The notes (pp. 26–28) and Lecture 7 give the chain. Injected hot electrons break the Si–H bonds that passivate the interface — at 3.30 eV the weakest bond of the terminators — so the hydrogen is released and the dangling bond returns. More dangling bonds raise Qit. By ΔVT = −Qox/Cox the threshold shifts, upward in an n-channel device, whose interface traps at inversion take a second electron and are negative. The drain current, which depends on VG − VT, falls, and if the degradation is severe enough the device fails to operate.

Hence the notes' corollary: hydrogen introduced in the post-anneal can increase hot-electron degradation, and the ideal gate dielectric contains as little hydrogen as possible beyond what the interface needs. Fluorine and interfacial nitrogen are the stronger terminators.

Full treatment: M05b §4.3 and §4.4.

What is an oxygen vacancy, and how does it become the E′ centre?

An oxygen vacancy is a missing bridging oxygen, the two silicons it joined bonded to each other directly, O₃≡Si–Si≡O₃: in the oxide bulk, the Si–Si stretched bond. Across an intact bridge the two silicons sit 3.12 Å apart (notes p. 22). With the oxygen gone they move together, to about 2.5 Å in calculations — a bond longer than the 2.35 Å of crystalline silicon and strained by the network around it. The notes (p. 26) make it a hole trap.

Capturing a hole removes one electron from the Si–Si bond. In the model worked out for crystalline α-quartz the bond breaks: one silicon keeps the remaining electron, unpaired, and the other, now positive, relaxes into the plane of its three oxygens, or through it in the puckered form; calculations for amorphous SiO₂ find several forms, some keeping a weakened Si–Si bond. The result is the E′ centre: positively charged, and paramagnetic because of the unpaired electron, which electron spin resonance detects. It is the dominant deep hole trap of thermal oxide. Because the atoms rearrange, the charge stays after the stress is removed and is lost only slowly, by electrons tunnelling in from the silicon or when the oxide is heated.

Full treatment: M05b §5.2.

Where does oxide trapped charge come from in a process, and how is it annealed?

Oxide trapped charge is holes or electrons held at defects in the oxide bulk — strained or broken Si–O bonds, and oxygen vacancies that become E′ centres — so it can be positive or negative (notes p. 28). The notes trace the defects to ionizing radiation, hot-carrier injection, high currents through the oxide (Fowler–Nordheim tunnelling) or other reactions that bend or break Si–O bonds, and name the processes that introduce Qot: ion implantation, sputter deposition and plasma etching (p. 29). Lecture 8 added plasma-enhanced CVD, named the plasma etch as reactive ion etching, and listed the metals deposited by sputtering: titanium, molybdenum, cobalt, nickel and tungsten. Each step delivers energy to the oxide network — a plasma through energetic ions, electrons and ultraviolet photons, an implant through the ions themselves.

Qot "can be annealed out by low temperature treatments (above 550 °C)" (notes p. 29), in N₂ or Ar (Lecture 8, which gave "around 500 °C"; the notes' figure stands); Wolf adds that some neutral traps may remain. "Low" is relative to oxidation at 1000–1100 °C. The anneal must precede the aluminium, so traps made later by metal sputtering, etching and dielectric deposition cannot receive it, which is one reason not all Qot is removed by the end of fabrication.

Full treatment: M05b §5.3 and §5.4.

What makes mobile ions mobile, and why are they no longer the problem they once were?

Mobile ionic charge is alkali ions — Na⁺ and K⁺ in the notes (p. 23) — that entered the oxide during processing. Each is a network modifier: it sits in an open space of the network beside a non-bridging oxygen that carries the extra electron, Si–O⁻, held by non-directional ionic attraction, not by covalent bonds. Thermal oxide is open enough for this, with only 43% of its volume occupied, so the ion hops from site to site, drifting toward the silicon under positive gate bias and toward the gate under negative bias, faster when hot. A charge shifts VT more the closer it lies to the silicon, so the threshold drifts with operating history.

The ions come from the process itself: sodium, potassium and lithium in water, chemicals and solvents, and from people (Lecture 6). Cleanliness in fabrication — low-sodium reagents, deionised water, masks — had largely removed the problem by the 1970s. A film impervious to the ions, such as silicon nitride, protects the oxide, and Al₂O₃ and phosphosilicate glass also serve as sodium barriers (Sze & Ng p. 224). The number of ions does not grow in service, and the shift they cause, Qm/Cox, shrinks with tox as oxides thin.

Full treatment: M05b §6.

5 · Chapter 3, second half: conduction, breakdown and growth

The second half of Chapter 3, notes pp. 29–48, follows the gate oxide once it is thin enough to carry current. It starts with conduction — Fowler–Nordheim and direct tunnelling, and the 1 A/cm² criterion that turns gate leakage into a floor on thickness — and then what that current does to the film: trapped electrons and holes, trap-assisted tunnelling, stress-induced leakage and, at the end of the damage, breakdown in two stages. Polysilicon depletion thins the oxide electrically with no current at all, and the six factors that set the minimum usable thickness gather these effects into Hu's numbers. The chapter closes with the response: models for the anomalous thin-oxide regime, single-wafer rapid thermal oxidation and in-situ steam generation, the nitrogen and fluorine chemistries that bought the last few ångströms, two oxide thicknesses on one wafer, process damage, and how so thin a film is measured. All of it is taught in M06, which takes conduction before breakdown, the reverse of the notes' order; the material that replaces silicon dioxide is M07.

5.2 Explain in brief

Each prompt opens onto a reference explanation of the length a short written answer takes, with the sketch that goes with it where the idea is spatial, and a link to the section of M06 that treats it in full.

What separates Fowler–Nordheim tunnelling from direct tunnelling, and which of the two limits how thin a gate oxide can be?

Both carry inversion-layer electrons through the 3.1 eV barrier between the silicon and the oxide conduction band, and in both the probability falls exponentially with the width of the forbidden region crossed. Under positive gate bias the oxide conduction band slopes down toward the gate, with slope Eox. In Fowler–Nordheim tunnelling the voltage across the film exceeds the barrier, Vox > φox ≈ 3.1 V, so the band edge falls below the electron's energy inside the film: the electron tunnels through the triangular tip only, a distance φox/Eox, and is then accelerated to the gate in the oxide conduction band. Its current depends on the field alone. It is the thick-oxide, high-field mechanism, about 5×10⁻⁷ A/cm² at 8 MV/cm — far below any leakage limit. In direct tunnelling, below about 4 nm in the notes, Vox < φox and the barrier stays trapezoidal: the electron crosses the whole film in one step, the tunnelling distance is the thickness itself, and the current becomes very large below 3 nm. Direct tunnelling is what limits thinning.

Full treatment: M06 §1.2 and §1.3.

Why does gate tunnelling current put a floor under the gate-oxide thickness, and where does the 1 A/cm² figure come from?

A MOSFET that is off already leaks: subthreshold conduction carries a source-to-drain current, specified per unit width, Ioff/W, which the notes take as 10 nA/µm. Gate current becomes intolerable when it is no longer small beside that leakage, and the notes place the limit where the gate-to-channel tunnelling current equals the off-state subthreshold leakage current. Direct tunnelling grows exponentially as the film thins, so that equality fixes a thickness below which the oxide cannot go. For a 1 µm × 1 µm device, Ioff = 10 nA = 10⁻⁸ A over 1 µm², that is 10⁻⁸ A/µm², and since 1 cm² = 10⁸ µm², 1 A/cm². Gate leakage scales with area and subthreshold leakage with width, so in general Jmax = (Ioff/W)/L: the 1 A/cm² carries the 1 µm gate length, and a shorter gate tolerates a proportionally larger density. The notes put excessive tunnelling below 1.4 nm; Wolf shows one low-leakage RTO oxide crossing the 1 A/cm² line at about that thickness. The notes conclude that alternate gate dielectrics are required for 50 nm MOSFETs and beyond.

Full treatment: M06 §2.

What do electrons and holes trapped in the oxide do to the tunnelling barrier, and why is one feedback negative and the other positive?

The cathode is the electrode that supplies electrons — the inverted silicon under positive gate bias — and the anode the one that collects them. The bias fixes the voltage across the film, so charge trapped inside it redistributes the field between the two faces. Trapped electrons, which enter from the silicon or the gate by tunnelling or hot-electron injection, lower the field near the cathode and raise it near the anode. A shallower band edge at the cathode lengthens the tunnelling distance φox/Eox — the barrier becomes broader — and the Fowler–Nordheim current falls through its exponential field dependence: negative feedback, since the current fills the traps that suppress it. Trapped holes, from anode hole injection or impact ionisation, do the reverse: cathode field up, the barrier narrower, and a small field increase gives a large current increase — positive feedback, more current injecting more holes. Hole transport also creates new bulk traps. Both run at once in a real stress, and electron trapping usually masks the rise until close to breakdown.

Full treatment: M06 §3.

What is SILC? Explain what causes it, how it behaves in thick and thin oxides, and why it sets a floor on the tunnel oxide of a non-volatile memory.

SILC, stress-induced leakage current, is the extra leakage a thin oxide conducts at low field after it has been exposed to high-field stress. The notes put it compactly — thin oxides are exposed to high electric-field stress, the Fowler–Nordheim gate current increases, and this is termed SILC — and the increase that carries the name is the low-field leakage measured afterwards, not the stress current itself.

The cause is trap generation. The high-field stress current generates neutral oxide traps in the bulk of the film: sites that are uncharged while empty, so their creation alone does not shift the threshold voltage. The traps help electrons tunnel. An electron tunnels from the cathode into a trap and from the trap on to the anode. Each step crosses a barrier about half as wide as the film, and the electron waits in the trap between them, so the slower step sets the current; because the tunnelling probability falls exponentially with distance, a half-width step is roughly the square root of the probability of crossing the whole width at once, and the two short steps carry far more current than the one long one. This trap-assisted current flows at fields where Fowler–Nordheim current is negligible.

In a thicker oxide SILC is transient: the traps fill, cannot easily empty across the thick remainder of the film, and the leakage decays. In a thinner oxide it is a continuous current, because tunnelling out of a trap is as easy as tunnelling in, and filling and emptying balance. Lecture 10 used "continuous" in a second sense that is also true: trap generation goes on for as long as the film is held at high field, so the leakage grows with the stress the film accumulates.

A floating-gate non-volatile memory moves its stored charge through a thin tunnel oxide at high field — by Fowler–Nordheim tunnelling in both directions in EEPROM and NAND flash, and for erasure in NOR flash, which programs by channel hot-electron injection — so every program and erase cycle stresses that oxide, and SILC grows with cycling. The leakage lets the stored charge escape and reduces data retention: with the usual ten-year retention requirement, it makes tunnel-oxide scaling much below 8 nm (80 Å) difficult.

Describe dielectric breakdown as the notes' two-stage process. What sets the field limits quoted for it, and what does burn-in do?

Breakdown is the permanent loss of insulation, and the notes require that it be avoided. In the first stage the oxide is slowly degraded over time, by the high field across it or by the current passing through it: electron and hole trapping, trap generation and bond breakage accumulate until the defects form a localised conductive path through the film. This stage occupies essentially the whole life of the device, and because the failure arrives only after prolonged stress the phenomenon is called time-dependent dielectric breakdown, TDDB. It runs fastest where a defect or a locally thin spot raises the field E = V/t. The second stage is the very short runaway: current concentrates in the path, Joule heating forms a filament, the oxide is shorted, and a very large current flows between the gate and the substrate — the notes' "poly short". Oxide life is therefore a time to breakdown, and it falls steeply with field: the notes give twenty years if the field stays below 8 MV/cm, and CMOS logic and memory are held to the 5 MV/cm range because a field somewhat above the design point shortens the lifetime by far more than proportionally — 5 MV/cm leaves room for 20–30-year lifetimes (M06). The notes also ask for a breakdown field above 8 MV/cm, so the same 8 MV/cm cannot be a safe operating field as well (M05 §4.2). Burn-in runs finished parts at elevated temperature and voltage, then checks them electrically, so that oxides that would fail in service fail in the factory instead.

Lecture 8's "two types" of breakdown are the two drivers of the first stage, plus the runaway of the second. Full treatment: M06 §5.

What is polysilicon depletion? Draw the band diagram, and say why it acts like a thicker oxide and what limits the remedies.

Take an n⁺ polysilicon gate on p-type silicon, biased into depletion or inversion. The negative charge induced in the silicon must be balanced by an equal positive charge on the gate side. A metal supplies it within an atomic distance; n⁺ polysilicon supplies it by letting mobile electrons leave the region next to the oxide — out through the gate electrode, not across the oxide — which exposes fixed donor ions. Because the donor density is finite, a layer of finite width Wpd = Qg/(qNp) must be emptied: the polysilicon depletion effect. The gate's bands bend across that layer, so a voltage Vpoly = qNpWpd2/(2εsi) is dropped inside the gate and Vg − VFB = Vpoly + Vox + φs: less reaches the oxide and the silicon — nearly all of it at the oxide's expense, since the surface potential is almost pinned in strong inversion — so the inversion charge, the drain current and the speed all fall. As a circuit the depleted layer is a capacitor, Cpolydep = εsi/Wpd, in series with Cox, which raises the equivalent oxide thickness. The notes: Cox is much smaller than Cpolydep until the oxide is thinner than about 10 nm; below that the poly term begins to impact the total. Heavier gate doping narrows the layer, but active doping stops near 10²⁰ cm⁻³ in n-type and in the mid-to-upper 10¹⁹ cm⁻³ in p-type polysilicon; a metal gate has no depletion layer, at the cost of a φMS fixed by the metal's work function.

Lecture 11 put the effect at 2–4 nm oxides, where it is severe, and called the poly-depletion capacitance the smaller one; on its own board numbers the oxide remains the smaller capacitor. Full treatment: M06 §6.1 and §6.2.

What is boron penetration, how do nitrided oxides counter it, and at which face of the film must the nitrogen sit?

In dual-doped CMOS the p-channel gate is p⁺ polysilicon doped with boron. During later high-temperature steps boron diffuses from the gate down through a thin gate oxide into the channel, where it adds acceptors and shifts the threshold voltage — one of the negative factors of a very thin oxide. Fluorine in the oxide makes it worse. Nitrided oxides, oxynitrides SiOxNy rather than silicon nitride, show improved boron-penetration resistance, the first of the notes' four advantages. Because the boron arrives from the gate above, the nitrogen that stops it is needed at the top face of the film, the poly/SiO₂ interface, at above about 5 atomic percent (Wolf). The 1–2 at.% at the Si/SiO₂ interface is a separate requirement, associated with a higher breakdown field and lower fixed charge, and nitrogen in the bulk degrades the film. The notes judge the thermal routes by how much nitrogen they incorporate: N₂O as a second-step nitridant brings too little for boron suppression, and NO a higher concentration at a lower thermal budget (p. 41). Those routes place their nitrogen peak at the Si/SiO₂ interface, though (M06 §9.3); a nitrogen-rich layer at the top face is a separate requirement. Growing the oxide hotter also helps: a 2 nm film grown by RTP at 1000 °C resisted boron better than one grown at 800 °C.

Full treatment: M06 §9.1, with the gases in §9.3.

Why are ultra-thin gate oxides grown by rapid thermal oxidation rather than in a batch furnace?

A 2 nm film needs very little oxidation. A batch furnace oxidises many wafers standing in a quartz boat, and its minimum oxidation time is at least a few minutes, so to keep the film thin it must run cool; the notes give the two consequences — furnace oxidation is difficult to scale below 2 nm, and the low temperature degrades oxide quality. Rapid thermal oxidation, RTO, is a single-wafer technology: tungsten-halogen lamps heat one wafer at 50–100 °C/s to the process temperature (800–1000 °C in the notes), hold it for a few seconds and cool it rapidly. It grows the film at high temperature yet keeps it thin, and the notes list what the higher growth temperature buys: smaller gate leakage as the growth temperature rises, up to 1050 °C; higher resistance to boron penetration (a 2 nm film by RTP at 1000 °C against one grown at 800 °C); and higher effective mobility for electrons and holes, from a smoother Si/SiO₂ interface. RTO is also conducive to cluster tooling, in which an in-situ pre-clean, oxidation, nitridation and polysilicon deposition follow one another without exposing the wafer to air.

Full treatment: M06 §8.2.

Give the two-step N₂O oxynitridation sequence for an 85 Å film, and say what each step does.

The two-step process separates growth from nitridation: the oxide is grown first in O₂ and then given an oxynitridation step in N₂O or NO. The notes' 85 Å recipe is a furnace process: 50 Å of SiO₂ grown in O₂ at 850 °C for 30 min, then N₂O annealing at 950 °C, which grows a further 35 Å; beside it they draw a profile of 10% O₂ at 900 °C, then 100% N₂O and 100% N₂ at 950 °C. Lecture 10 taught the two as one sequence: (1) oxidise in O₂ at 900 °C to about 50 Å of SiO₂; (2) anneal in N₂O at 950 °C, which incorporates nitrogen at the Si/SiO₂ interface and, because the O₂ released from the N₂O keeps oxidising the silicon, grows about 35 Å more, 85 Å in all; (3) anneal in N₂ at 950 °C, an inert anneal that does not react with the film; (4) cool. The N₂O step both nitrides and oxidises, so the final thickness is budgeted across the two steps. The lecture ran each step by RTA in about 30 s, and that cannot grow those thicknesses: by Wolf's Reisman–Nicollian constants, 30 s of dry oxidation grows under 2 nm even at 1000 °C, about 0.65 nm at 900 °C, where 5 nm needs about 9 minutes, and Wolf gives the N₂O step 5–40 minutes. The thicknesses are the furnace recipe's. N₂O nitrides only through the NO it dissociates into, which needs more than 850 °C; supplied directly, NO incorporates more nitrogen at a lower thermal budget, with better thickness control.

Full treatment: M06 §9.3.

Compare nitridation in ammonia, in nitrous oxide and in nitric oxide: what each does, and what limits each.

All three put nitrogen into a thin oxide to make an oxynitride; molecular N₂ is too inert to nitride at these temperatures. Ammonia, NH₃, came first, as ROXNOX, reoxidized nitrided oxide: grow the oxide, nitride it in NH₃ at high temperature, then re-oxidise to drive out the hydrogen the ammonia leaves. The films resist hot carriers better than SiO₂, but the process is hard to optimise: excess hydrogen leaves electron traps, the dominant degradation in p-channel devices; excessive re-oxidation loses the resistance to interface-state generation that n-channel devices need; Qf is higher than in the best SiO₂; and the steps are many and hot. Nitrous oxide, N₂O, carries no hydrogen. Reacted directly with silicon by RTP it grows a film with little hydrogen and a nitrogen peak at the Si/SiO₂ interface, as (time)½ because N₂O must diffuse through the oxide; n-channel devices show low electron trapping and small ΔDit, but the initial Dit and Qf are higher, growth is slow (practical only below 100 Å) and peak mobility is 5% lower. Used as the second step of a two-step process it incorporates too little nitrogen for boron suppression, because only the NO it dissociates into above 850 °C nitrides. Nitric oxide, NO, supplied directly, incorporates more nitrogen at a lower thermal budget, with better thickness control; mixed with O₂, NO supplies the nitrogen while O₂ continues the oxidation.

Full treatment: M06 §9.2 and §9.3.

Why does fluorine in the gate oxide raise its resistance to hot electrons, how does it arrive, and what limits how much is wanted?

A hot carrier damages the interface by breaking the Si–H bonds that passivate dangling bonds, and each break leaves an interface trap (M05b §4.3). Fluorine forms Si–F bonds, stronger than Si–H, and replaces Si–H at the Si/SiO₂ interface; a hot carrier energetic enough to break Si–H (3.30 eV) but not Si–F (5.86 eV) then leaves the site intact, so fewer interface traps form and the hot-electron resistance rises. The notes name two inadvertent sources. The first is a BF₂⁺ source/drain implant: the implanter's feed gas is BF₃, the analysing magnet passes BF₂⁺, and each ion delivers one boron and two fluorine atoms into the gate polysilicon (and the source and drain), from which the fluorine diffuses into the oxide during the activation anneals. The second is the LPCVD of tungsten or tungsten silicide from WF₆ and SiH₄, the subject of the next prompt. The cost falls on the p-channel device: fluorine in the oxide may increase boron penetration from the p⁺ polysilicon gate. The notes keep the fluorine dose during implantation below 1×10¹⁴ cm⁻², below which no noticeable enhancement of boron penetration is observed — a limit on the fluorine put into the gate polysilicon. The study behind the figure, which Wolf cites, implanted fluorine into the p⁺ gate on purpose: fluorine can also be added deliberately, implanted into the polysilicon and diffused into the oxide, with the same gain in hot-electron resistance.

Full treatment: M06 §9.4.

How is fluorine introduced into the gate oxide when tungsten is deposited?

Tungsten and tungsten silicide, WSix, are deposited by low-pressure chemical vapour deposition, LPCVD, in which reactant gases flow through a heated chamber at low pressure and react on the wafer surface to leave a solid film. The tungsten source is tungsten hexafluoride, WF₆, which carries six fluorine atoms for every tungsten atom; the notes give the reactants as WF₆ and SiH₄. Silane (or hydrogen) reduces the WF₆ at 250–500 °C — 2WF₆ + 3SiH₄ → 2W + 3SiF₄ + 6H₂, or WF₆ + 3H₂ → W + 6HF — and most of the fluorine leaves as SiF₄ or HF and is pumped away. Not all of it: CVD tungsten retains fluorine from its WF₆ source, often 1 atomic percent or more, and tungsten silicide retains it too.

In a polycide gate (M02 §3.2) the WSix lies directly on the gate polysilicon. In the anneals that follow, the retained fluorine diffuses down through the polysilicon into the gate oxide, and at the Si/SiO₂ interface it replaces Si–H bonds with the stronger Si–F bond. Lecture 10 drew the same path with tungsten deposited over the gate and over the source and drain and then annealed into a silicide: the fluorine enters the polysilicon from the film above it and reaches the oxide. Fluorine at the interface can raise the hot-electron resistance (the notes), and in excess it increases boron penetration from a p⁺ gate; in polycide gates the same fluorine has also been seen to thicken the gate oxide during the anneal and, the thicker the silicide, to shorten the oxide's time to breakdown (M06, beyond the notes). The fluorine is a by-product of the deposition, as it is of a BF₂⁺ implant, not an intended addition.

Lecture 10 wrote the deposition as WF₆ → W + 3F₂ at 700–800 °C. In CVD, WF₆ is not decomposed by heat alone: silane or hydrogen reduces it, the fluorine leaving as SiF₄ or HF, and CVD tungsten is deposited at 250–500 °C.

Full treatment: M06 §9.4.

How are two gate-oxide thicknesses made on one wafer by nitrogen implantation, and why does it work?

Analogue and input/output circuits run at higher voltage and need a thicker oxide, while logic uses the thinner one, so both must be built on one wafer. The notes' flow, under the heading "2 step oxidation": pattern photoresist to expose the regions meant for the thin oxide; implant nitrogen into them at low energy; strip the resist; strip the screen oxide the implant went through, and clean; perform the thermal oxidation. Nitrogen implanted into silicon before oxidation reduces its oxidation rate — Wolf suggests that Si–N bonds at the interface of the growing oxide constrain growth — so the single oxidation grows a thin oxide over the implanted regions and a thicker one everywhere else. Wolf's typical implant is 10–30 keV at 2×10¹⁴ to 1×10¹⁵ cm⁻². The older route oxidised, removed the oxide from the thin regions through a mask and oxidised again. Lecture 10 described a version that keeps the first thin oxide, which the implant nitrides, and then oxidises a second time — two oxidations, with the same outcome.

Full treatment: M06 §9.5.

How is the thickness of a gate oxide only a few nanometres thick measured, and why is it difficult?

The notes name two techniques. Ellipsometry directs a polarised beam of light — a laser in single-wavelength instruments — at the surface at an angle and measures the change in polarisation of the reflected beam; thickness and refractive index follow from a model of the film. It is optical, non-contact and fast, which makes it the production measurement, and it reaches below 50 Å — but accuracy degrades there, and multiple-wavelength or multiple-angle instruments are needed to recover it. The reasons lie in the model's assumptions. The Si/SiO₂ transition region can reach about 10 Å, 20–50% of an ultra-thin film, with a refractive index, dielectric constant, band gap and effective mass unlike those of the bulk oxide; surface roughness and an ad-layer of airborne contamination are counted as film; parameters correlate in stacked films; and a single angle of incidence can fit several thicknesses. High-resolution transmission electron microscopy, HRTEM, images a thinned cross-section of the stack at atomic resolution; oxide as thin as 2.4 nm has been measured with it. Preparing the sample means slicing the wafer, mounting the slice in epoxy, polishing it and ion-milling it thin. It is destructive and slow, so it serves as the calibration rather than the production measurement, and it calibrates well because, unlike ellipsometry, it needs no assumed optical constants.

Full treatment: M06 §10.

(a) Field. Eox = V/tox = 5.4 V ÷ 6.0×10⁻⁷ cm = 9.0×10⁶ V/cm = 9.0 MV/cm.

(b) Tunnelling distance. φox/Eox = 3.1 V ÷ 9.0×10⁶ V/cm = 3.44×10⁻⁷ cm = 3.44 nm. That is shorter than the 6.0 nm film, so the band edge falls below the electron's energy inside the film: Fowler–Nordheim. Equivalently, Vox = 5.4 V exceeds φox = 3.1 V.

(c) Convert to SI. The Fowler–Nordheim equation is SI-native: Eox = 9.0×10⁸ V/m, m* = 0.5 × 9.109×10⁻³¹ = 4.55×10⁻³¹ kg, q = 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ C, ℏ = 1.055×10⁻³⁴ J·s.

Rearrange. With Wpd = Qg/(qNp), the drop Vpoly = qNpWpd2/(2εsi) becomes Qg2/(2εsiqNp), so Np = Qg2/(2εsiqVpoly).

(a) Doping. Np ≥ (2.07×10⁻⁶)² ÷ (2 × 1.044×10⁻¹² × 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ × 0.080) = 4.29×10⁻¹² ÷ 2.67×10⁻³² = 1.6×10²⁰ cm⁻³. The depleted layer is then Wpd = 2.07×10⁻⁶ ÷ (1.6×10⁻¹⁹ × 1.6×10²⁰) = 8.1 Å thick.

(b) At the doping limits. At 10²⁰ cm⁻³: Vpoly = 4.29×10⁻¹² ÷ (2 × 1.044×10⁻¹² × 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ × 10²⁰) = 4.29×10⁻¹² ÷ 3.34×10⁻¹¹ = 0.128 V. At 5×10¹⁹ cm⁻³, half the doping, twice the drop: 0.26 V.

Result. Np ≥ 1.6×10²⁰ cm⁻³, above the ≈10²⁰ cm⁻³ that can be made electrically active in n-type polysilicon, so 80 mV at 6 MV/cm cannot be reached by doping: the limits give 0.13 V for n⁺ and 0.26 V for p⁺ gates. That is the quantitative case for a metal gate, which has no depletion layer (M07 §9).

Check. Vpoly scales as 1/Np at a fixed field: 0.128 V × (10²⁰/1.6×10²⁰) = 0.080 V.

Method: M06 §6.2, which gives the voltage across the depleted layer and its inverse for the doping; formula sheet (6.4) for the width and (6.10) for the voltage.

A 2.0 V logic process uses a defect-free gate oxide of 35 Å. (a) What field does the oxide carry at the supply, and at the supply +10% at which reliability is extrapolated? (b) Apply the notes' 30% thickness increase for defects: what production thickness results, and what field does it carry at 2.0 V? (c) A defect thins the production oxide locally by 25%: what is the field there? (d) A 1.2 V version is held to the field of (a): what defect-free and production thicknesses does it need?

(a) Field. Eox = V/tox = 2.0 V ÷ 35×10⁻⁸ cm = 5.71×10⁶ V/cm = 5.71 MV/cm. At 2.2 V: 6.29 MV/cm.

(b) Defect margin. 1.30 × 35 Å = 45.5 Å. At 2.0 V it carries 2.0 ÷ 45.5×10⁻⁸ = 4.40 MV/cm (4.84 MV/cm at 2.2 V).

(c) Thin spot. 0.75 × 45.5 Å = 34.1 Å, so the local field is 4.40 ÷ 0.75 = 5.86 MV/cm — back near the defect-free design field, which is what the margin is for.

(d) Same field at 1.2 V. tox = V/Eox = 1.2 V ÷ 5.71×10⁶ V/cm = 2.10×10⁻⁷ cm = 21.0 Å; with the margin, 1.30 × 21.0 Å = 27.3 Å.

Result. 5.71 MV/cm at 2.0 V and 6.29 MV/cm at 2.2 V; 45.5 Å in production, at 4.40 MV/cm; 5.86 MV/cm at the thin spot; 21.0 Å defect-free and 27.3 Å in production at 1.2 V.

Check. The method is the one worked in Lecture 10 for 5 V on 80 Å, 6.25 MV/cm. This design is more conservative than Hu's: its defect-free field, 5.71 MV/cm, lies below the 6.25–7.58 MV/cm of Hu's defect-free oxides at their supplies, and its production field, 4.40 MV/cm, below their 4.55–5.56 MV/cm.

Method: M06 §7.1, and §5.1 for the thin spot; formula sheet (5.2) and §15.6.

The notes give the gate leakage at 1 V as 1×10⁻¹² A/cm² at 35 Å and 1×10⁻¹⁰ A/cm² at 15 Å; the literature puts the 15 Å value near 10 A/cm². Treating the logarithm of the current as linear in thickness between the two points, (a) by how many decades per nanometre does the leakage rise on each pair of figures, (b) what does each give at 25 Å, and (c) at what thickness does each reach the 1 A/cm² ceiling?

(a) Rise. Notes: 10⁻¹⁰/10⁻¹² = 100, two decades over 20 Å, one decade per nanometre. Literature: 10¹/10⁻¹² = 10¹³, thirteen decades over 20 Å, 6.5 decades per nanometre (0.65 per ångström).

(b) At 25 Å, half-way between the points: notes, 10⁻¹² × 10¹ = 1×10⁻¹¹ A/cm²; literature, 10⁻¹² × 106.5 = 3.2×10⁻⁶ A/cm².

(c) The ceiling. 1 A/cm² is twelve decades above the 35 Å value. Notes: at one decade per nanometre that takes 120 Å of thinning, more than the whole film, so the notes' pair never reaches the ceiling. Literature: 12 ÷ 0.65 per Å = 18.5 Å of thinning, so the line crosses at 35 − 18.5 = 16.5 Å.

Result. One against 6.5 decades per nanometre; 1×10⁻¹¹ against 3×10⁻⁶ A/cm² at 25 Å; the notes' line never reaches 1 A/cm², the literature's crosses at about 16.5 Å.

Check. 16.5 Å lies close to the 1.4 nm below which the notes call tunnelling excessive. A pair of points whose line cannot reach the ceiling at any thickness is one more sign of the exponent lost in transcription that M06 flags. The interpolation is an approximation; measured leakage is not exactly exponential in thickness.

Method: M06 §7.2 and §2; formula sheet §15.6.

A wafer carries a 1.2 nm oxide before a dry oxidation at 800 °C. With Wolf's Reisman–Nicollian constants, a = 0.302 nm and b = 0.704 with times in minutes, (a) what is τ, (b) what thickness is reached after 20 min of growth, and (c) how long does growth to 3.5 nm take? (d) Repeat (b) for 1 min at 1000 °C, where a = 3.02 nm and b = 0.701.

(a) Time offset. τ = (toxi/a)1/b = (1.2/0.302)1/0.704 = 3.9741.420 = exp(1.420 × 1.380) = exp(1.960) = 7.10 min.

(b) After 20 min. tox = a(tg + τ)b = 0.302 × (20 + 7.10)0.704 = 0.302 × exp(0.704 × ln 27.10) = 0.302 × exp(0.704 × 3.300) = 0.302 × exp(2.323) = 0.302 × 10.20 = 3.08 nm, of which 1.88 nm is new growth.

(c) Time to 3.5 nm. Invert the Reisman–Nicollian equation for the total time: (3.5/0.302)1/0.704 = 11.591.420 = exp(1.420 × 2.450) = exp(3.480) = 32.5 min, of which τ = 7.1 min was already spent growing the starting layer: tg = 25.4 min.

(d) At 1000 °C. τ = (1.2/3.02)1/0.701 = 0.39741.427 = exp(1.427 × (−0.923)) = exp(−1.317) = 0.268 min; tox = 3.02 × (1 + 0.268)0.701 = 3.02 × exp(0.701 × 0.237) = 3.02 × 1.181 = 3.57 nm.

Result. τ = 7.10 min; 3.08 nm after 20 min; 25.4 min to 3.5 nm; 3.57 nm after one minute at 1000 °C.

Check. M06's example grows 4.35 nm in an hour on a 1.85 nm start at 800 °C; 1.88 nm in 20 min on a thinner start fits the sub-linear growth (b < 1). One minute at 1000 °C does what 25 minutes do at 800 °C, which is why a high-temperature oxidation of an ultra-thin film must last seconds.

Method: M06 §8.1; formula sheet (6.6) and (6.7).

An RTO chamber ramps the wafer at the notes' 50–100 °C/s. (a) How long does the ramp from 25 °C to 1050 °C, the growth temperature of lowest leakage in the notes, take at each end of that range? (b) A recipe must bring the wafer from 25 °C to 950 °C within 12 s: what ramp rate does it need, and is that inside the notes' range?

(a) Ramp time. t = ΔT/rate = (1050 − 25) °C ÷ 50 °C/s = 20.5 s, and ÷ 100 °C/s = 10.3 s.

(b) Ramp rate. (950 − 25) °C ÷ 12 s = 77 °C/s, inside 50–100 °C/s.

Result. 10.3 to 20.5 s; 77 °C/s.

Check. M06 gives 9.75 to 19.5 s from 25 °C to 1000 °C; the extra 50 °C adds 0.5 to 1 s. A batch furnace, by contrast, oxidises for at least a few minutes.

Method: M06 §8.2; formula sheet §15.6.

A two-step oxynitridation is to give a 70 Å film: 45 Å of oxide is grown in O₂ and the N₂O step supplies the rest. (a) How much must the N₂O step grow, and what fraction of the final film is that? Compare the 85 Å sequence as taught. (b) Treating the film as SiO₂ with the course's consumption ratio of 0.46, how much silicon does the whole film consume, and how much of the film lies above the original silicon surface?

(a) N₂O step. 70 − 45 = 25 Å, which is 25/70 = 36% of the film. In the sequence as taught, 35 of 85 Å, 41%. The N₂O step both nitrides and grows oxide — the O₂ released from the N₂O keeps oxidising the silicon — so the final thickness is budgeted across the two steps.

(b) Silicon consumed. 0.46 × 70 Å = 32 Å of silicon — 20.7 Å in the O₂ step and 11.5 Å in the N₂O step — and 0.54 × 70 Å = 38 Å of the film lies above the original surface.

Result. 25 Å, 36% of the film; 32 Å of silicon consumed; 38 Å above the original surface.

Check. 32 + 38 = 70 Å; the split 0.46/0.54 holds for any thickness, because each unit of oxide consumes the same amount of silicon wherever it grows.

Method: M06 §9.3 and M05a §4; formula sheet §15.7 and (5a.3).

Summary

  • Chapters 1–3 make one argument: the gate length shrinks, the transistor is rebuilt along four axes — gate stack, gate dielectric, channel doping, drain structure — and the gate-dielectric axis runs through Chapter 3, where SiO₂ thins until it fails, to high-k in Chapter 4. Chapter 2's 300 mm wafer runs alongside, driven by area economics.
  • Chapter 1 as sequences: Al → n⁺ poly ("mid-1970s" in the notes) → polycide, WSix below 1.25 µm → salicide, TiSi₂ or CoSi₂; LE/tox ≈ 45 (200 Å at 1 µm, 20 Å below 0.1 µm); flat doping → boron VT implant for both types → blanket anti-punch-through → SSR (In; As or Sb) → halo → LATIPS pocket; single drain → LDD at 2 µm (hot carriers) → SDE at 0.18 µm (resistance); LOCOS → STI after 0.35 µm; n⁺ poly on the PMOS → buried channel → dual-doped poly.
  • VT = φMS − Qi/Cox − Qd/Cox + 2φF, with signs − − + + for an n-channel and − − − − for a p-channel device, in the course's constants (kT/q = 0.0259 V, ni = 1.5×10¹⁰ cm⁻³, εs = 11.8ε0, centimetres throughout). Punch-through is avoided while L > 2W, and W ∝ NA−1/2: more body doping, less punch-through.
  • Chapter 2 as a sequence: sand → MG silicon (98%) → SiHCl₃ → fractional distillation, the step that purifies → Siemens rod → Czochralski crystal (quartz crucible, Dash neck, pull rate ∝ 1/D in the notes) → grind, flats or notch, slice, round the edge, lap, etch, polish by CMP (NaOH oxidises, colloidal silica abrades). A 300 mm wafer has 2.25 times the area of a 200 mm one. Its substrate costs more per unit area, so the silicon under each die costs more; the cost per die falls because per-wafer processing, now shared among 2.25 times as many dies, is the larger part of the cost of a finished wafer.
  • SiO₂ serves ten roles, as thermal oxide from 1.5 nm (gate) to 400 nm (LOCOS field oxide). The gate oxide thins with the gate, LE/tox ≈ 45, at the cost of the notes' four negative factors — the field, tunnelling, boron penetration, defects and non-uniformity — and must meet ten requirements, among them a breakdown strength above 8 MV/cm.
  • The oxide is SiO₄ tetrahedra joined at bridging oxygens: Si–O 1.62 Å, Si–O–Si 110°–180° (most probable 144°), Si···Si 3.12 Å. Thermal oxide is a glass; non-bridging oxygens weaken it, and water turns bridges into Si–OH pairs, so dry oxide is the more cohesive. Oxidation consumes silicon equal to 0.46 of the oxide thickness; 0.54 of the film lies above the original surface.
  • Four charges: Qm (Na⁺, K⁺, mobile), Qf (positive, in the transition region), Qit (on the interface, from dangling bonds), Qot (oxide bulk, either sign). Qit and Qot are the ongoing reliability problem.
  • One dangling bond per 1000 (100) surface atoms shifts VT by 0.63 V through 20 nm. Hydrogen, 100% or 4% in N₂, ties them up as Si–H at 400–450 °C as the last thermal step, and hot electrons break Si–H again. The oxygen vacancy, the Si–Si stretched bond, becomes the E′ centre on capturing a hole; Qot anneals out above 550 °C (notes).
  • Fowler–Nordheim tunnelling crosses only the triangular tip of the 3.1 eV barrier, φox/Eox: the thick-oxide, high-field mechanism, about 5×10⁻⁷ A/cm² at 8 MV/cm. Direct tunnelling, below 4 nm and very large below 3 nm, sets the floor: gate current equal to off-state leakage, 10 nA/µm over 1 µm², is 1 A/cm², and tunnelling becomes excessive below 1.4 nm.
  • Trapped electrons broaden the barrier and cut the current (negative feedback); trapped holes narrow it (positive feedback). Stress-generated neutral traps carry SILC by trap-assisted tunnelling — transient in thicker oxides, continuous in thinner ones — which keeps memory tunnel oxides near 8 nm. Breakdown is slow degradation by field or current into a conductive path (TDDB), then a short runaway; twenty years below 8 MV/cm, logic and memory near 5 MV/cm.
  • Polysilicon depletion, in depletion and inversion, drops Vpoly = qNpWpd2/(2εsi) inside the gate and adds a series capacitance, an electrically thicker oxide; it matters below about 10 nm, and heavier poly doping (up to about 10²⁰ cm⁻³) or a metal gate reduces it. Six factors set the minimum thickness; Hu's 80, 45 and 33 Å become 110, 65 and 45 Å with the defect margin.
  • Deal–Grove holds above 350 Å and fails below 200 Å for dry oxidation, where five models compete and Reisman–Nicollian's tox = a(tg + τ)b fits the data. RTO is single-wafer, 50–100 °C/s to 800–1000 °C (the notes' window) for seconds, with lower leakage, better boron resistance and higher mobility; ISSG reaches 2.0 nm below 20 Torr.
  • Nitrogen at the interfaces resists boron and hot carriers: NH₃ (ROXNOX) leaves hydrogen, N₂O avoids it, NO gives more nitrogen at a lower thermal budget, and the notes' two-step recipe grows 50 Å in O₂ at 850 °C for 30 min in a furnace and 35 Å more in N₂O at 950 °C (Lecture 10 taught it as one 900 °C / 950 °C sequence ending in an N₂ anneal; its seconds-long steps are too short to grow those thicknesses). Fluorine from BF₂⁺ implants and from WF₆-based tungsten deposition replaces Si–H with Si–F, its dose kept below 1×10¹⁴ cm⁻². A nitrogen implant gives two oxide thicknesses from one oxidation; plasma damage, ellipsometry and HRTEM close the chapter.
