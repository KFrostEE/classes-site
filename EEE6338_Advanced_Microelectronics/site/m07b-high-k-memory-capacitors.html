Module 07b

High-k Capacitor Dielectrics for DRAM and FeRAM

Why a memory cell must keep its capacitance near 30 fF as it shrinks, the three ways that was achieved, and the high-k dielectrics that took over when those ran out — tantalum pentoxide, then barium strontium titanate — together with the electrodes, deposition processes and integration problems each one brought. The same perovskite family, in its ferroelectric form, makes a non-volatile memory.

Prerequisites M07 Read time ~65 min Sources Class notes pp. 52–64 (Chapter 4); Wolf Vol. 4 §4.2–4.5; Taur & Ning ch. 12

Chapter 4 of the notes opens by listing three uses for a high-k dielectric: the MOSFET gate, the capacitor of a dynamic random-access memory (DRAM), and the capacitor of a non-volatile ferroelectric memory (FeRAM). M07 treated the first. This module treats the other two, which the notes cover on pp. 52–64. The driving problem is the same arithmetic — capacitance per unit area is ε0k/t, so when the area shrinks either the film thins or k rises — but the constraints are different. A memory capacitor must keep a roughly fixed capacitance and hold its charge between refreshes rather than induce a channel, it may be ten or more nanometres thick, it sits between two electrodes of its own rather than on the transistor channel, and the dielectrics it can use reach k values in the hundreds.

The module follows the order in which the problem unfolds. It starts with the memory cell itself and the reasons its capacitance cannot fall with each generation (§1–§2), then the three ways the industry held that capacitance while the cell shrank, and why they ran out (§3). Any new dielectric needs electrodes it does not react with, which is the subject of §4. Tantalum pentoxide, the first high-k capacitor dielectric, follows in §5. The titanate dielectrics that were expected to replace it belong to a crystal family, the perovskites, whose properties the notes assume rather than teach, so §6 supplies that background before §7–§9 treat barium strontium titanate — its films, its deposition and its integration. The same crystal family, used in its ferroelectric state, gives the non-volatile memory of §10.

1 · The one-transistor, one-capacitor cell

A random-access memory stores bits in an array of cells, any one of which can be read or written in the same time regardless of its position. In a static RAM each cell is a latch of cross-coupled transistors that holds its state for as long as power is applied. In a dynamic RAM each cell stores its bit as the presence or absence of charge on a capacitor. That charge leaks away over time, so every cell must be read and rewritten periodically — the operation called refresh — and that need for continual refresh is what "dynamic" refers to. The DRAM cell is far smaller than a static cell of six transistors, which is why DRAM is the dense, cheap memory of a computer's main store (Taur & Ning §12.2).

The notes (p. 56) draw the cell as an equivalent circuit, Figure 1. It has one transistor and one capacitor, the 1T1C cell. The transistor is an n-channel MOSFET used as a switch; the notes call it the pass transistor, and it is also called the access or transfer device. Its gate is connected to the word line, a conductor that runs along a row of the array and is shared by every cell in that row. One of its source/drain terminals is connected to the bit line, a conductor that runs along a column and is shared by every cell in that column. The other terminal is connected to one electrode of the capacitor. In the notes' words, the MOS capacitor is connected to the bit line through the pass transistor (MOSFET switch) whose gate is controlled by the word line.

The two capacitor electrodes have names that the rest of this module uses. The electrode connected to the transistor is the storage node; in a stacked cell it is the bottom electrode. The other is the plate electrode, or cell plate, which is common to every cell in the array; in a stacked cell it is the top electrode (notes p. 55).

A cell is written by raising its word line, which turns the pass transistor on, and driving the bit line to the supply voltage Vdd for a "1" or to 0 V for a "0". The word line is then lowered, which turns the transistor off and isolates whatever charge is on the storage node. The word line is boosted above Vdd + Vt during a write, because an n-channel switch passing Vdd turns itself off once its source rises to within a threshold voltage of its gate, which would leave the node short of Vdd (Taur & Ning §12.2.1).

A cell is read by charge sharing. The bit line is first precharged to Vdd/2. The word line is raised, the cell capacitance is connected to the far larger capacitance of the bit line — the wire itself plus the drain junctions of every other cell on it — and the charge redistributes between them. The bit line moves slightly above or below Vdd/2, according to whether the cell held a "1" or a "0", and a sense amplifier at the end of the bit line compares it with a reference line that was precharged identically. The sense amplifier is a cross-coupled latch: once activated it drives the two lines apart to Vdd and 0, and because the cell is still connected, that also writes the original value back into the cell. The read itself destroys the stored charge; the write-back restores it.

The numbers show why the cell capacitance matters. A bit line typically carries 256 to 1024 cells, and each unselected cell adds drain-junction and gate-fringe capacitance, so Cbitline is of order 100–300 fF (1 fF = 10⁻¹⁵ F). With Ccell = 30 fF the transfer ratio is therefore 30/(30 + 300) ≈ 0.09 on a long bit line to 30/(30 + 100) ≈ 0.23 on a short one. Taur & Ning (§12.2.1) take about 0.2, the short-bit-line end, so a cell charged to 1 V on a 1 V supply produces at most (1 − 0.5) × 0.2 ≈ 0.1 V at the sense amplifier, and about half that on a long bit line. The signal is a small fraction of the stored voltage, and it scales directly with Ccell.

Between reads the stored charge leaks — through the subthreshold current of the pass transistor, through the gate of the pass transistor, through the reverse-biased junction of the storage node, and, once it is thin enough, through the capacitor dielectric itself. The retention time is the longest interval over which the cell still reads correctly, and the refresh interval is set shorter than it; a typical worst-case specification is about 100 ms. For a 30 fF capacitor charged to 1 V, which holds 30 fC, keeping the loss below 10% over 100 ms allows a total leakage of about 30 fA from all paths together (Taur & Ning §12.2.2).

Taur & Ning go on to say where that budget falls. In the cells they describe, the capacitor insulator is thick enough for its tunnelling current to be negligible, and the diffusion current of the storage junction is negligible too, so the budget constrains mainly the pass transistor: its threshold voltage must be at least about 0.7 V at 25 °C, and its gate oxide thicker than about 2 nm, which is why the DRAM access device cannot be scaled like a logic transistor (§12.2.2). The capacitor dielectric becomes a leakage path of the first order when it is thinned toward the tunnelling limit — about 5 nm for ONO (Wolf p. 151) — and with the high-k films of this module: for Ta₂O₅, Wolf (p. 155) names a relatively high leakage current as the biggest drawback for DRAM. That is why leakage returns in every section below.

2 · Why the capacitance per cell stays near 30 fF

Every DRAM generation packs more cells onto a chip, so the silicon area available to each cell, and to its capacitor, shrinks. The notes (p. 52) set out the resulting squeeze. The operating voltage has fallen from 5 V to 3 V and may drop to 1 V. The charge storage capacity of each cell, however, has stayed at about 30 fF per cell, which the notes state corresponds to about 1–2×10⁶ electrons per cell, and "this number of stored charges has not been decreased". What the 30 fF figure fixes is the capacitance; the charge is that capacitance multiplied by the stored voltage.

Q = CcellV,  N = CcellVq
(7b.2)
Q stored charge (C) · Ccell cell capacitance (F) · V voltage across the capacitor (V) · N number of stored electrons (dimensionless) · q elementary charge, 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ C. Useful scale: 1 fF × 1 V = 1 fC = 6.24×10³ electrons.

The notes give three reasons why this charge-storage capacity cannot be allowed to fall with the cell area, and each is a separate physical constraint.

2.1 Soft errors

The first reason is soft errors (notes pp. 52–53). A soft error is a change in a stored bit caused by a transient disturbance rather than by damage to the device: the cell is rewritten correctly on the next write and works normally afterwards. The notes name two causes. The first is α-particles — helium nuclei, two protons and two neutrons, emitted by the radioactive decay of naturally occurring trace impurities, uranium and thorium at parts-per-million levels, in the packaging material; May and Woods reported this mechanism in dynamic memories in 1979. The second is cosmic rays, whose ground-level form is described in the box below.

An energetic charged particle crossing silicon loses energy by ionisation, creating electron–hole pairs along its track at an average cost of 3.6 eV per pair. An α-particle of 5 MeV that stops in silicon therefore creates about 5×10⁶ eV ÷ 3.6 eV ≈ 1.4×10⁶ pairs. The notes put the consequence in one sentence: such energetic particles generate minority carriers in silicon, whose charge can alter memory states. In a cell built on p-type silicon the minority carriers are electrons; those generated near the storage node's n⁺ junction are swept into it by the junction field or reach it by diffusion, and they discharge a node that held a "1". Whether the bit flips depends on how the collected charge compares with the charge that distinguishes a "1" from a "0" at the sense amplifier. By maintaining about 30 fF per cell, the notes conclude, the soft-error rate (SER) can be kept low. A smaller capacitor holding less charge would be upset by strikes that a larger one tolerates.

What "cosmic rays" means at ground level — beyond the notes

Primary cosmic rays — mostly protons and heavier nuclei from outside the solar system — do not reach the ground. They collide with nitrogen and oxygen nuclei high in the atmosphere and start a cascade of secondary particles, and the component of that cascade which upsets memories at sea level is the neutron. A neutron carries no charge and ionises nothing directly; it upsets a cell through a nuclear reaction with a silicon (or other) nucleus, whose charged recoil and fragments then ionise the silicon exactly as an α-particle does. The ground-level neutron environment, and the charge-collection and critical-charge physics of the upset itself, are treated in EEE 5416: M03 §8 for the atmospheric cascade and M11 §4 for critical charge and the dynamic-memory upset.

2.2 Sense-amplifier signal and retention

The second reason is detection. The detection capability of DRAM sense amplifiers has remained about the same across generations, so each cell still needs about the same charge storage capacity for its state to be read (notes p. 53). Equation (7b.1) shows the mechanism: the read signal is the stored voltage scaled by the transfer ratio Ccell/(Ccell + Cbitline), and the bit-line capacitance does not shrink in proportion to the cell because it is dominated by the number of cells on the line and by the wire. A smaller Ccell means a smaller signal at an amplifier that needs the same signal to decide reliably.

The third reason is retention. The data retention time has remained constant, requiring about the same storage capacity per cell (notes p. 53). The leakage paths of §1 remove charge at a rate set mainly by the transistor and the junction, which do not shrink with the capacitor, so a smaller stored charge reaches the unreadable level sooner and the cell would have to be refreshed more often, at a cost in power and in time the array is unavailable.

2.3 How many electrons 30 fF holds

Stored charge at 30 fF across the supply-voltage history

At 5 V. Q = 30×10⁻¹⁵ F × 5 V = 1.5×10⁻¹³ C = 150 fC. N = 1.5×10⁻¹³ C ÷ 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ C = 9.4×10⁵ electrons.

At 3 V. Q = 90 fC, N = 9.0×10⁻¹⁴ ÷ 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ = 5.6×10⁵ electrons.

At 1 V. Q = 30 fC, N = 3.0×10⁻¹⁴ ÷ 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ = 1.9×10⁵ electrons.

The voltage the notes' count requires. Inverting (7b.2), V = Nq/Ccell. For 1×10⁶ electrons, V = 1×10⁶ × 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ C ÷ 30×10⁻¹⁵ F = 5.3 V; for 2×10⁶ electrons, 10.7 V.

A 30 fF cell holds 1.9×10⁵ electrons at 1 V and 9.4×10⁵ at 5 V. Holding 1–2×10⁶ electrons would take 5.3–10.7 V across the capacitor.

The answer also shows what "constant" means here. At fixed capacitance the stored charge falls in proportion to the voltage, a factor of five from 5 V to 1 V. Keeping Ccell near 30 fF was the most the designers could do; had the capacitance also fallen with the cell area, the charge would have fallen with both.

The notes' electron count does not follow from 30 fF

The notes (p. 52) pair 30 fF per cell with "about 1–2×10⁶ electrons/cell". The same pairing is printed in Wolf Vol. 4 p. 150, which the notes condense, so it is inherited rather than introduced in transcription. The two figures are not consistent under Q = CV at any voltage the same passage mentions: 30 fF stores 1.9×10⁵ electrons at 1 V, 5.6×10⁵ at 3 V and 9.4×10⁵ at 5 V, and 1–2×10⁶ electrons would need 5.3–10.7 V. The notes' values are the ones stated above; the arithmetic is the physics. The 30 fF figure itself is the well-attested one — Taur & Ning (§12.2.1) give 30 fF as the typical storage capacitance, and a DRAM process review of the 100 nm era describes the required value as nearly constant at 30 fF per cell through every generation (Saino, 2004).

A cell is redesigned for 25 fF and a 1.2 V supply. How many electrons does it store, and by what factor has the stored charge fallen from a 30 fF cell at 3.3 V?

Q = 25×10⁻¹⁵ F × 1.2 V = 3.0×10⁻¹⁴ C = 30 fC, so N = 3.0×10⁻¹⁴ ÷ 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ = 1.9×10⁵ electrons. The 30 fF cell at 3.3 V held 99 fC, or 6.2×10⁵ electrons. The charge has fallen by 99/30 = 3.3 times, almost all of it from the voltage: the capacitance fell by only 30/25 = 1.2 times, and 3.3/1.2 = 2.75 is the voltage ratio.

3 · Keeping the capacitance as the cell shrinks

A parallel-plate capacitor offers three quantities to work with, and the history of the DRAM capacitor is the history of using them up in turn. The capacitance is

Ccell = ε0kAt = 3.9ε0AEOT
(7b.3)
Ccell capacitance (F) · ε0 permittivity of free space, 8.854×10⁻¹⁴ F/cm = 8.854×10⁻¹² F/m — use the form that matches the length unit · k relative dielectric constant of the film (dimensionless) · A electrode area actually covered by the dielectric (cm² or m²), which in a three-dimensional capacitor is the whole folded surface, not the footprint · t physical dielectric thickness (cm or m) · EOT equivalent oxide thickness, t(3.9/k), the SiO₂ thickness giving the same capacitance, defined in M07 §3 and extended to layered films in M07 §4. The area A can be increased without enlarging the cell's footprint by folding the capacitor into the third dimension or roughening its surface; the thickness t can be reduced; or k can be raised. The notes (pp. 53–54) list the three approaches that were adopted to keep the charge storage per cell constant, and all of them work on the first two quantities. When they were exhausted, k was the only lever left.

Taur & Ning (§12.2.1) use this figure to show that a planar capacitor is excessively large once design rules fall below 1 µm, and date the change: since the 4 Mb generation, built with 1.2 µm features, DRAM capacitors have been either trenches or stacks.

3.1 Three-dimensional cells: trench and stack

The first approach is a three-dimensional structure that increases the cell's surface area: trench or stacked capacitors (notes p. 53). A trench capacitor is etched down into the silicon beside the pass transistor, so that its area comes from the depth of the trench while its footprint is only the trench opening. The notes (p. 57) draw one, Figure 3: the trench wall is lined with the capacitor dielectric, the silicon outside the dielectric holds the stored charge, and a polysilicon capacitor plate fills the trench and spreads over the surface on a layer of SiO₂.

A stacked capacitor is built above the silicon surface, over the transistor, after the transistor is complete. A conducting plug rises from the pass transistor's source/drain through the interlevel dielectric; the storage-node electrode sits on top of it, the dielectric covers its top and sides, and the common cell plate covers everything. Its area comes from the height of the storage node. The notes draw the stacked cell on p. 61 in the context of barium strontium titanate, Figure 4, and the drawing follows Wolf's Fig. 4-11.

The two geometries have different thermal histories, which decided where new dielectrics could go. A trench capacitor is formed before the transistor's source and drain are activated, so its dielectric must survive about 1000 °C, and oxide–nitride stacks remained widely used in trench cells, though Al₂O₃ later entered them too; a stacked capacitor is built afterwards, with a wider margin in process temperature, and it is the stacked cell in which most of the high-k dielectrics of this module were developed (Saino, 2004, beyond the notes).

3.2 Thinner dielectric

The second approach is to use thinner layers of dielectric in the capacitor (notes p. 53). The dielectric of the SIS capacitor of §4 was SiO₂ (k = 3.9), silicon nitride (k ≈ 7), or a combination (Wolf p. 150). The combination the notes name is the oxide–nitride–oxide (ONO) multilayer, a sandwich of SiO₂, Si₃N₄ and SiO₂, whose nitride raises the capacitance above that of oxide alone. The notes give its thickness as 50 Å, and Wolf (p. 151) identifies that as the minimum: below about 5 nm, tunnelling currents through the film produce excessive leakage — the same direct tunnelling that limits the gate oxide in M06, but judged here against the far stricter leakage budget of §1, tens of femtoamperes per cell.

3.3 Roughened electrodes: hemispherical grain polysilicon

The third approach increases the capacitor's surface area by a factor of about 2 by roughening the surface of the polysilicon bottom electrode (notes pp. 53–54). The notes, following Wolf (p. 151), say this is achieved by growing the bottom-electrode polysilicon with larger grains, so that its surface is covered in rounded grains instead of being flat. The resulting films are called hemispherical silicon grain (HSG) or rough surface polysilicon (RSP). The dielectric deposited over them follows the bumps, and the capacitance scales with the true surface area, not the projected one.

The grains grow at the surface, by a mechanism the notes do not describe. The electrode is deposited as amorphous silicon and annealed near the temperature at which amorphous silicon crystallises, often after its surface has been seeded with disilane (Si₂H₆) in vacuum. Crystal nuclei form on the surface, silicon atoms migrate over the surface to them, and each nucleus grows into a hemispherical grain (Watanabe et al., 1995).

These approaches were combined rather than chosen between. ONO multilayers of 50 Å together with HSG were used in 0.18 µm and 0.15 µm DRAMs (notes p. 54). Beyond that the combination fails: because of unacceptable leakage currents, the notes write, this approach is not good for memories of more than 256 MB. The three-dimensional structures had also reached the point where they could not be scaled further to add area (Wolf p. 151), and Wolf adds that the capacitors suffered premature catastrophic breakdown even at reduced voltage. The notes' solution: use a high-k dielectric to increase the capacitance per unit area. The film can then be thick enough for low leakage and high reliability while still storing enough charge on a shrinking area at a falling voltage.

"256 MB" and "4 GB" mean megabits and gigabits

The notes write 256 MB here and "memories up to 4 GB" for Ta₂O₅ on the same page (p. 54), following Wolf p. 151, which also writes MB. A capital B denotes a byte of eight bits; a lower case b denotes a bit. DRAM generations are named by the number of bits on one chip, and these are the 256 Mb and 4 Gb generations. The notes themselves use the bit unit elsewhere: the roadmap table of p. 56 lists DRAM capacity as 1 Gb, 4 Gb, 8 Gb, and p. 59 speaks of the 1 Gb and 4 Gb generations. The numbers are the notes'; only the unit needs reading as bits.

The notes (p. 54) then name the two families that followed. Ta₂O₅, with k = 25, is used with good success for memories up to 4 Gb, and can be deposited by CVD, metal organic CVD (MOCVD), sputtering or anodization (§5). Beyond it, "ferroelectrics or near-ferroelectrics come into picture": combinations of barium and strontium with titanate (BST), lead zirconium titanate (PZT) and other perovskite metal oxides, with k values ranging from a few hundred to a few thousand that depend strongly on the composition and the crystalline structure (§6–§7). The next example shows how large the required k is.

A stacked cell at 0.15 µm — why k must reach the tens

The capacitor area. Wolf (p. 162) describes a 0.15 µm DRAM whose storage node is a pedestal 0.15 µm × 0.45 µm in footprint and 0.3 µm high. The dielectric covers the top and the four sides: A = 0.15 × 0.45 + 2 × (0.15 + 0.45) × 0.3 = 0.0675 + 0.36 = 0.43 µm².

The capacitance density. For 30 fF: 30 fF ÷ 0.43 µm² = 70 fF/µm². Wolf quotes about 80 fF/µm² as the requirement for this cell, which on this area gives 34 fF; the two agree to within the rounding of the target.

The equivalent oxide thickness. 80 fF/µm² = 0.080 F/m². From (7b.3), EOT = 3.9ε0/(C/A) = 3.9 × 8.854×10⁻¹² F/m ÷ 0.080 F/m² = 4.3×10⁻¹⁰ m = 0.43 nm.

The physical thickness for each dielectric. t = EOT × k/3.9. ONO would have to be about 0.4–0.8 nm (k between 3.9 and 7), against Wolf's 5 nm minimum. Ta₂O₅ at k = 25: 0.43 × 25/3.9 = 2.8 nm. BST at the notes' film value k = 90 (§7): 0.43 × 90/3.9 = 9.9 nm.

This simple 0.3 µm-high stack needs an EOT of about 0.4 nm. Only a dielectric with k near 100 does that in a film ten nanometres thick; Ta₂O₅ would have to be under 3 nm.

The cell is Wolf's illustration of the simplified stack that a titanate dielectric would permit (pp. 162–163), not the geometry Ta₂O₅ cells used. Wolf (p. 159) puts the minimum practical EOT of Ta₂O₅ at about 1.5 nm, so a Ta₂O₅ cell at the same node has to find the missing capacitance in area instead — a storage node many times taller, roughened with HSG (§5.5). Numbers from Wolf; the calculation is not in the notes.

A later stacked cell has a storage-node pedestal 0.1 µm × 0.3 µm in footprint and 0.4 µm high, and must hold 25 fF. What capacitance density and EOT does it need, and how thick would the film be in Ta₂O₅ (k = 25) and in BST (k = 90)?

Area covered by the dielectric: top 0.1 × 0.3 = 0.03 µm², sides 2 × (0.1 + 0.3) × 0.4 = 0.32 µm², total 0.35 µm². Capacitance density: 25 fF ÷ 0.35 µm² = 71 fF/µm² = 0.071 F/m². EOT = 3.9 × 8.854×10⁻¹² F/m ÷ 0.071 F/m² = 4.8×10⁻¹⁰ m = 0.48 nm. Physical thickness t = EOT × k/3.9: Ta₂O₅, 0.48 × 25/3.9 = 3.1 nm; BST, 0.48 × 90/3.9 = 11 nm. The taller pedestal almost compensates for the smaller footprint, but the EOT is still far below Ta₂O₅'s practical 1.5 nm, and only the titanate reaches it with a film thick enough to hold down tunnelling.

4 · Electrodes

A capacitor dielectric is never used alone: it sits between two electrodes, and the interfaces with those electrodes are part of the capacitor. A dielectric with high k gains nothing if an electrode reacts with it and leaves a low-k layer between them. The move to high-k capacitor dielectrics therefore forced a move to new electrode materials, and most of the integration difficulty of the rest of this module lies in the electrodes rather than in the dielectric.

4.1 SIS, MIS and MIM

Doped polysilicon has been the material of the DRAM capacitor's bottom electrode, the storage node, and either polysilicon or a metal has formed the plate electrode (notes p. 55). The notes name the three combinations that result:

  • SIS — polysilicon–insulator–polysilicon: silicon for both electrodes. This is the structure of the ONO capacitors of §3 (notes p. 52).
  • MIS — polysilicon–insulator–metal: silicon storage node, metal plate.
  • MIM — metal–insulator–metal: metal for both electrodes.

Polysilicon has one decisive fault in contact with a high-k film: it forms a layer of SiO₂ at the polysilicon/high-k interface, either as a native oxide present before the dielectric is deposited or during later processing steps — the anneals that the dielectric itself needs, or the CVD of the interlevel dielectric (ILD) deposited over the finished capacitor (notes p. 55). Silicon oxidises readily, and a high-k oxide deposited or annealed in oxygen supplies the oxygen. This interfacial SiO₂ increases the equivalent thickness of the overall capacitor dielectric, which in turn reduces the cell capacitance.

The mechanism is series capacitance: the interfacial SiO₂ and the high-k film are two capacitors in series, so their equivalent oxide thicknesses add, EOTtotal = tSiO₂ + thigh-k(3.9/k), and the layer with the lowest k dominates — the result derived for the gate stack in M07 §4. The higher the k of the main film, the more a given interfacial oxide costs. Take 10 nm of a dielectric with k = 90, whose EOT is 10 × 3.9/90 = 0.43 nm, and suppose 1 nm of SiO₂ forms at one electrode. The EOT becomes 1.43 nm and the capacitance falls to 0.43/1.43 = 30% of its intended value: the single nanometre of oxide has undone more than two-thirds of the benefit of the high-k film.

As a result, a number of other electrode materials have been considered. The notes (p. 55) list them: Al, iridium, platinum, ruthenium, TaN, TiN and W — "metals or near metallic electrode materials", the last group being the conducting nitrides of tantalum and titanium, and tungsten. If both electrodes are metal, the capacitor is MIM.

4.2 The platinum-group metals

Platinum, iridium and ruthenium belong to the platinum-group metals (PGMs), the six noble metals of groups 8–10 in periods 5 and 6 of the periodic table (ruthenium, rhodium, palladium, osmium, iridium and platinum). They are "noble" in the chemical sense: they resist chemical attack and corrosion. The notes (p. 55) credit Pt, Ir and Ru with good chemical corrosion properties, thermal stability and diffusion-barrier properties, and Wolf (p. 153) adds low resistivity. Those are the properties an electrode needs next to an oxide dielectric that is deposited and annealed in oxygen, but the three metals meet the oxygen differently. Platinum does not oxidise under these conditions. Ruthenium oxidises easily (Wolf p. 153), and iridium also forms an oxide; but RuO₂ and IrO₂ conduct like metals — "oxides of these metals are also conductive" (Wolf p. 167) — so the oxide either metal grows at its surface adds no low-k layer in series with the dielectric. §4.3 takes that idea further.

Each has a problem of its own, which the notes (p. 56) list:

  • Platinum — adhesion and etch residue problems, and cost. The chemical inertness that makes platinum a good electrode also means it bonds poorly to most surfaces, so it needs an adhesion ("glue") layer beneath it (Wolf p. 167). It forms no volatile compounds in a plasma, so material removed during a dry etch tends to redeposit on the pattern as residue rather than being pumped away (§9). And it is expensive: Wolf (pp. 153–154) estimates about $1000 per 200 mm wafer to sputter 50 nm of platinum at 40% deposition efficiency. In CVD only about 20% of the precursor deposits, and after later processing on the order of only 1% of the starting material remains on the finished wafer; the rest is removed by plasma etch or chamber cleaning, and for a metal this expensive it must be reclaimed.
  • Ruthenium — forms RuO₄, a toxic by-product of the etch process. Ruthenium is etched in oxygen-containing plasmas precisely because it forms the volatile tetroxide RuO₄; the property that makes it etchable is the one that makes the exhaust hazardous (see the box in §4.3).
  • Susceptible to pinholes. In Wolf's text (p. 153) this belongs to ruthenium, which is susceptible to pinholes and notching because it oxidises easily.

4.3 Conducting oxides and barriers

A second route avoids interfacial oxidation by making the electrode an oxide to begin with. Several metal oxides conduct like metals. The notes list RuO₂ as a bottom electrode with ρ = 35 µΩ·cm (p. 59), and IrO₂ and the conducting perovskite oxides SrRuO₃ and (La,Sr)CoO₃ as top electrodes (p. 64). Because such an electrode is already fully oxidised, it does not grow a dielectric oxide at its interface with the high-k film during the thermal steps that follow (Wolf p. 158). The 35 µΩ·cm is about thirteen times the resistivity of aluminium (2.7 µΩ·cm), and lies within the 30–40 µΩ·cm measured at room temperature on single crystals of RuO₂; deposited films are usually higher. Wolf's text (p. 158) calls this number a conductivity; its unit, µΩ·cm, makes it a resistivity, as the notes write it.

Whatever the electrode, it must not react with the silicon of the plug beneath it. Platinum forms silicides with silicon, so a platinum electrode needs a diffusion barrier between it and the polysilicon plug; the notes (p. 63) name a Ti/TiN bilayer, which also serves as the glue layer platinum needs. TiN appears in both roles throughout this module: as a barrier and, since it conducts, as an electrode in its own right.

RuO₄ is a volatile oxidiser, not a fuel

The notes (p. 64) state that RuO₂ is easy to etch but that one of the by-products is RuO₄, "MP 25.4°", and that it "is both flamable and toxic gas", following Wolf (p. 168). The melting point is right: ruthenium tetroxide melts at 25.4 °C, so at room temperature it is a volatile solid or liquid close to its melting point and gives off vapour readily. Its toxicity is also right. "Flammable" is the wrong word for its other hazard. RuO₄ does not burn; it is a powerful oxidiser, classified as one under the GHS hazard system, and it oxidises virtually any organic material it meets, which can ignite that material. The hazard is the reverse of flammability — it supplies oxygen rather than fuel.

5 · Tantalum pentoxide capacitors

Tantalum pentoxide, Ta₂O₅, was the first high-k dielectric to replace ONO in the DRAM capacitor. The notes (pp. 54, 58) give it k = 25 — roughly three and a half times that of silicon nitride and six times that of SiO₂ — and it had two practical attractions: the industry had been developing techniques for depositing it since the early 1980s, and a Ta₂O₅ capacitor could replace an ONO capacitor without major process-integration problems (Wolf pp. 151, 154–155). Its principal drawback for DRAM is a relatively high leakage current, and most of the process steps below exist to bring that leakage down.

Three values of k for Ta₂O₅ in the notes

The notes' pages on the DRAM capacitor (pp. 54 and 58) give Ta₂O₅ k = 25. The table of dielectrics that opens the chapter (p. 49) gives 26, and the roadmap table (p. 56) lists 22 for Ta₂O₅ capacitors in 1999. All three are inherited from Wolf, who uses 25 in his text, 26 in his Table 4-1 and 22 in his Table 4-2. Reported values run from about 22 (Robertson's 2004 review) to the mid-twenties (Wilk, Wallace & Anthony's 2001 review lists 26), and films vary with phase, deposition, crystallinity and the electrode beneath them (below, a film crystallised on ruthenium reached 65). The capacitor pages of the notes use 25; the other two are the notes' figures in their own contexts.

5.1 Deposition, and why the regime matters

The notes (p. 58) list four ways to deposit Ta₂O₅:

  1. LPCVD — low-pressure chemical vapour deposition: precursor vapours flow over the heated wafer at reduced pressure and react on its surface to leave the film.
  2. MOCVD — CVD in which the tantalum arrives in a metal-organic precursor molecule.
  3. Sputtering of Ta followed by oxidation — a tantalum metal film is deposited by sputtering and then converted to oxide.
  4. Anodic oxidation — a deposited tantalum film is made the anode of an electrochemical cell and oxidised electrolytically.

For the DRAM dielectric, LPCVD is the most common (Wolf p. 154). The films are formed by the oxygen-assisted thermal decomposition of an organometallic precursor (notes p. 58), either tantalum ethylate, Ta(OC₂H₅)₅, called TAETO — "ethylate" is an older name for ethoxide, so this is tantalum pentaethoxide — or tantalum tetraethoxy dimethylamino ethoxide, Ta(OC₂H₅)₄(OCH₂CH₂N(CH₃)₂), called TATDMAE. Each molecule already carries its tantalum bonded to oxygen; the added O₂ burns off the organic groups at the wafer surface and completes the oxide. The notes' conditions are a pressure of 1–4 Torr and a temperature of 450–485 °C. Both precursors are liquids at room temperature and are vaporised at about 170 °C and carried into the chamber by argon (Wolf p. 156).

The temperature window is chosen for a reason the notes leave implicit. Any CVD film grows through two steps in series. The precursor must first be transported from the gas to the surface — across the gas layer above the wafer, and down into any trench or opening — and it must then react on the surface. Whichever step is slower sets the deposition rate. The surface reaction is thermally activated, so its rate rises exponentially with temperature; the transport depends on diffusion through the gas and changes only weakly with temperature.

Wolf (p. 156) gives the numbers for TAETO. Between 450 and 485 °C the deposition is reaction-rate limited, with an activation energy of 1.3 eV; above 485 °C it becomes mass-flow (transport) limited. At 467 °C (740 K), equation (7b.4) gives ΔT×2 = 8.617×10⁻⁵ × 740² × 0.693 ÷ 1.3 = 25 K: the rate doubles for every 25 °C, which is Wolf's figure, and which is why the temperature of a reaction-limited process has to be held tightly.

The regime decides the shape of the film in a trench. Step coverage, or conformality, is the ratio of the film thickness on a sidewall or at the bottom of a feature to its thickness on the flat top surface, usually given in per cent; 100% is a perfectly conformal film. When the surface reaction is the slow step, precursor molecules arrive faster than they are consumed, so the gas everywhere — including the bottom of a deep trench — carries nearly the same precursor concentration, and the film grows at the same rate on every surface. When the reaction is fast, a molecule reacts close to where it first strikes, so the mouth of a trench receives more than the bottom and the film grows thick at the top and thin below. A DRAM capacitor, folded into trenches or around tall pedestals, needs conformal coverage, and so the Ta₂O₅ deposition must be carried out in the reaction-rate-limited regime (Wolf p. 156).

5.2 Protecting the bottom electrode, and the notes' "interfacial SiO₂"

The Ta₂O₅ is deposited on the doped-polysilicon storage node, and §4 showed what an oxide on that polysilicon costs. The notes (p. 58) describe the preparation: Ta₂O₅ is deposited onto an interfacial layer that is achieved by rapid thermal nitridation of the polysilicon. Rapid thermal nitridation (RTN) is a short, lamp-heated anneal of the surface in a nitriding gas — Wolf's example is 900 °C for 60 s in NH₃ (p. 156). It does two things. It converts any native oxide already on the polysilicon into a nitride, and the nitride layer it forms is a diffusion barrier to oxygen, so no new oxide can grow on the polysilicon during the anneals that follow.

The RTN layer is a nitride, formed to prevent SiO₂

The notes' sentence reads: "Ta₂O₅ is deposited on to an interfacial layer of SiO₂ that is achieved by rapid thermal (polysi) nitridation." Nitridation cannot produce SiO₂. The source the notes condense (Wolf p. 156) states the opposite purpose: the RTN step exists "to eliminate the possibility of such an oxide layer" on the polysilicon, by nitriding the native oxide and forming a nitride that blocks oxygen. The interfacial layer under the Ta₂O₅ is therefore a thin silicon nitride (or oxynitride where native oxide was nitrided), and its job is to keep SiO₂ out. Even this nitride lowers the capacitance somewhat, because it is a low-k layer in series (Wolf p. 158) — one reason metal and conducting-oxide bottom electrodes were pursued (§5.4). M07 §4 shows the size of the effect: Wolf's typical 10 nm Ta₂O₅ film on polysilicon has an EOT of 2.5 nm, of which only 1.56 nm is the Ta₂O₅ itself.

5.3 Densifying the film

As deposited, the Ta₂O₅ exhibits poor leakage-current characteristics, because it is oxygen deficient and amorphous (notes p. 58). An oxygen-deficient oxide contains oxygen vacancies, and a vacancy leaves metal orbitals unbonded, which introduces electron states inside the band gap — the same kind of defect that forms oxide traps in SiO₂ (M05b). Electrons hop between such states through the film, which is trap-assisted conduction (M06 §3.3). The film also contains carbon and hydrogen left by the organometallic precursor (Wolf p. 157).

The remedy is to densify the film: Ta₂O₅ is usually densified using an O₂ anneal or RTA in oxygen at 600–700 °C, and this annealing also crystallises the amorphous as-deposited film (notes p. 58). Wolf (pp. 156–157) presents the mechanism as the accepted interpretation rather than an established result: the oxygen is thought to fill the vacancies and restore the stoichiometry, and the crystallisation is also thought to reduce the leakage. The anneal also drives out some of the hydrocarbon residues and water, which shrinks the film and reduces its EOT. Wolf describes two versions. The conventional anneal is a furnace step at 500 °C in ozone generated by ultraviolet light. The rapid thermal version oxygenates at 600 °C in an oxygen plasma and then ramps above 700 °C to crystallise the film; it has been reported to reduce the leakage at 1 V by up to six orders of magnitude. Its peak temperature must stay below about 800 °C: above that, silicon diffuses through the nitride barrier on the polysilicon and reacts with the Ta₂O₅ to form SiO₂, and the EOT becomes too large.

The thermal budget remains constrained after the capacitor is finished. Furnace steps at 800 °C for an hour or more, such as the reflow of a borophosphosilicate-glass interlevel dielectric or a source/drain activation anneal, make the leakage through the Ta₂O₅ rise steeply, and one reported process kept every step after the Ta₂O₅ deposition at or below 500 °C (Wolf p. 159).

5.4 Electrodes for Ta₂O₅

The top electrode needs protection too. A barrier layer of TiN or WN is used between the Ta₂O₅ and a top polysilicon electrode, to prevent the polysilicon from reacting with the Ta₂O₅ and forming a silicide; the other approach is to use TiN as the top electrode itself (notes p. 58). A TiN plate over Ta₂O₅ on a polysilicon storage node is an MIS capacitor; Wolf (p. 155) describes this replacement of the polysilicon plate by TiN as the change that accompanied the move to Ta₂O₅.

To remove the polysilicon, and the nitride it needs, from the storage node as well, the bottom electrode can be Pt or RuO₂ (ρ = 35 µΩ·cm), or the whole capacitor can be Ru/Ta₂O₅/Ru, with ruthenium deposited by sputtering (notes p. 59). In the Ru/Ta₂O₅/Ru capacitor the annealed Ta₂O₅ reached k = 65, and Wolf (p. 158) reports the speculation that the film crystallised on the ruthenium in a form with a much higher k than on other electrodes; the same capacitors leaked much less after a forming-gas anneal at 400 °C.

Platinum for Ta₂O₅: a top electrode in the source

The notes (p. 59) list the bottom-electrode materials as Pt and RuO₂, and that is the course's list. The passage they condense (Wolf p. 158) reports two alternatives to the nitrided-polysilicon storage node: in one report TiN was the bottom electrode and Pt the top; in another, reactively sputtered RuO₂ was the bottom electrode. So the source's bottom electrodes are TiN and RuO₂, and platinum appears in it only as a top electrode.

5.5 Aluminium-doped Ta₂O₅, etching, and the limit of Ta₂O₅

Another technique for depositing Ta₂O₅ is metalorganic solution deposition (MOSD), in which a solution of metal-organic compounds is coated onto the wafer and then heated to decompose the organics and form the oxide. Because the composition is set by what is dissolved in the solution, the film can be doped with aluminium. A film with the composition 0.9Ta₂O₅–0.1Al₂O₃ exhibited k = 45 and a low leakage current density of 6×10⁻⁸ A/cm² (notes p. 59).

Aluminium-doped Ta₂O₅: 45 in the notes, 42 in the source

The notes' value is k = 45; the last digit sits at the clipped right margin of p. 59, and reads as 5. Wolf (p. 159), whom the notes follow, gives 42, and adds the condition for the leakage figure: 6×10⁻⁸ A/cm² up to an applied field of 1 MV/cm (printed there as "1 MeV/cm", another slip). A later paper on MOSD films of this composition, crystallised on platinum, reports 42.8 at 100 kHz (Desu, Joshi & Desu, 2003). The notes give 45; the published value is 42. Either way the doped film's k is well above undoped Ta₂O₅'s 25.

Ta₂O₅'s last generation: 4 Gb in the notes, "may be" 1 Gb in the same source

The notes' statement is the course's: HSG with Ta₂O₅ serves the 1 Gb and 4 Gb generations (0.15 µm node). Wolf prints that sentence and, immediately after it, one that puts the limit possibly at 1 Gb; the two do not agree, and the notes keep the first. 6 · Perovskites and ferroelectricity

The notes introduce the titanates in a single sentence (p. 54): ferroelectrics or near-ferroelectrics come into the picture — combinations of barium and strontium with titanate (BST), lead zirconium titanate (PZT) and other perovskite metal oxides — with k values from a few hundred to a few thousand that depend strongly on the composition and the crystalline structure. Every term in that sentence is assumed rather than taught, and the rest of the chapter depends on them: why BST is paraelectric for DRAM, why PZT is ferroelectric for FeRAM, why the titanium content of a film matters to one per cent. This section supplies them.

Background — beyond the notes

Everything in this section is context the notes assume. It is standard solid-state physics (for example Kittel, Introduction to Solid State Physics, 8th ed., ch. 16, on dielectrics and ferroelectrics) together with Wolf's own remarks on the Curie point (p. 163) and on ionic polarisation (p. 162). The notes' own statements resume in §7.

6.1 The perovskite structure

A perovskite is a compound with the formula ABO₃ and a particular crystal structure, named after the mineral perovskite, CaTiO₃. A is a large cation — Ba²⁺, Sr²⁺ or Pb²⁺ in the materials here — and B is a small, highly charged cation, here Ti⁴⁺ or Zr⁴⁺. The charges balance: +2 + 4 = +6 against three O²⁻. In the ideal, cubic form of the structure the B cation sits at the centre of a cube, the six oxygens sit at the centres of the six faces, and the A cations sit at the eight corners (Figure 8). Each titanium is therefore surrounded by an octahedron of six oxygens, the TiO₆ octahedron; neighbouring octahedra share their corner oxygens, so the crystal is a three-dimensional framework of Ti–O–Ti chains, and the large A cations sit in the cavities between the octahedra, each with twelve oxygen neighbours.

The large k comes from ionic polarisation. In it, as M07 §2 explains, an applied field pulls the cation sublattice of a partly ionic solid one way and the anion sublattice the other, and the resulting displacement of charge is a dipole moment per unit volume. Wolf (p. 162) attributes BST's large dielectric constant to exactly this, "the strong ionic polarization in the material". In a titanate perovskite two things make that polarisation unusually large. The restoring force that holds the Ti⁴⁺ ion at the centre of its octahedron is unusually weak, so a given field displaces the titanium sublattice, relative to the oxygens, much further than the ions of an ordinary oxide are displaced. And the charge that moves with each displaced ion — its dynamical, or Born, effective charge — is larger than its nominal ionic charge, about +7 for titanium against its formal +4, because the Ti–O bond is partly covalent: titanium 3d and oxygen 2p orbitals hybridise, and the electron density shifts along the bond as the ions move (Cohen, 1992; Zhong, King-Smith & Vanderbilt, 1994). A large displacement carrying a large effective charge is a large dipole, and k reaches hundreds or thousands.

6.2 Ferroelectric and paraelectric

The weak restoring force has a further consequence. As such a crystal is cooled, the restoring force for the titanium displacement weakens, and at a critical temperature it vanishes: below it, the titanium moves off-centre spontaneously, with no field applied, and the oxygens shift the opposite way. The cubic cell distorts (in BaTiO₃ it becomes tetragonal, slightly elongated along the displacement), and every cell acquires a permanent dipole. Neighbouring cells align theirs, so a macroscopic region — a domain — carries a spontaneous polarisation. A material in this state is ferroelectric, and the temperature at which the transition occurs is the Curie point, Tc. Above it the titanium is centred on average, there is no spontaneous polarisation, and the material is paraelectric (Figure 9). For BaTiO₃, Tc is about 120 °C.

A paraelectric perovskite just above its Curie point still has a very large k, because the restoring force that is about to vanish is already weak; the permittivity peaks at Tc and falls as the temperature rises above it, following the Curie–Weiss law, k ∝ 1/(T − T0) with T0 close to Tc. This is what the notes' phrase "near-ferroelectrics" describes: a material operated a little above its Curie point, where k is large but there is no spontaneous polarisation.

The difference shows in how the polarisation responds to an applied field (Figure 10). In a paraelectric the polarisation is a single-valued function of the field: it rises with the field, flattens at high field, and returns to zero when the field is removed. In a ferroelectric it traces a hysteresis loop. A field large enough to reverse the domains — beyond the coercive field Ec — switches the polarisation, and when the field is removed a remanent polarisation Pr remains, of either sign depending on the direction of the last switching field. The capacitor's charge then depends on its history, not only on its voltage.

6.3 Composition sets the Curie point

The Curie point is a property of the composition, and it can be moved by substitution on the A site. Replacing barium with strontium lowers Tc roughly in proportion to the strontium fraction; SrTiO₃ itself does not become ferroelectric at any temperature. Wolf (p. 163) states the design rule: strontium is added to BaTiO₃ to bring the Curie point below room temperature, which ensures paraelectric operation. This is the reason for the two uses the notes name:

  • DRAM uses a paraelectric perovskite, BST. A DRAM cell must store a charge that follows its voltage and gives it back on a read; a remanent polarisation would add charge that depends on the cell's history. A composition just on the paraelectric side of its Curie point gives the largest k without hysteresis.
  • FeRAM uses a ferroelectric perovskite — PZT, lead zirconate titanate, Pb(Zr,Ti)O₃ (the notes write "lead zirconium titanate"), or SBT (§10) — whose two remanent states store a bit with no power applied.

Composition controls k as well as the phase, which is why the notes say the dielectric constants of these materials depend strongly on their composition. Near the barium-rich end of the DRAM range, bulk Ba0.7Sr0.3TiO₃ has its Curie point close to room temperature; thin films of that composition show a Curie–Weiss response above about 300 K with the ferroelectric transition itself suppressed (Basceri et al., 1997).

7 · Barium strontium titanate films

Barium strontium titanate was, when the notes' source was written, the candidate of most promise for the capacitor dielectric of future high-density DRAMs (Wolf p. 162). With §6 in hand, its formula and its properties can be read directly. This section covers what the notes say about the films themselves — their composition and k, how k varies with frequency, and the microstructural conditions a film must meet to reach a high k. How the films are deposited is §8; how they are built into a cell is §9.

7.1 Composition and dielectric constant

The notes (p. 60) write BST as BaxSr1−xTiO₃ with 0.5 < x < 0.7. The subscript x is the fraction of the A sites of Figure 8 occupied by barium, the rest being strontium, so a DRAM film is between half and seven-tenths barium on the A site. That range places the bulk Curie point near or below room temperature (§6.3): the films are paraelectric, and close enough to their Curie point for a large k. BST films are polycrystalline — made of many small crystalline grains, each with the perovskite structure (Wolf p. 162).

For those films the notes give k = 90 (p. 60). Wolf states it more precisely: k = 88–90 in the film thickness range used in DRAM capacitors. That qualification explains why the chapter gives three different numbers for BST.

BST appears in the notes with k = 300, 250 and 90

The table that opens the chapter (p. 49) lists BaSrTiO₃ at 300; the roadmap table (p. 56) requires k = 250 of BST in 2005; and the BST pages (p. 60) give 90 for the films actually used in DRAM capacitors. They are not in conflict once each is read in its context. The 300 is a tabulated figure for the material, carried over from Wolf's Table 4-1; the 250 is a roadmap requirement, not a measurement; the 90 is what a DRAM-thickness film delivers. All three are the notes' values. The reason the film value is so much lower is that k in these materials depends on thickness and microstructure, not on composition alone: the permittivity of BST films falls as the film is made thinner (measured on Ba0.7Sr0.3TiO₃ films from 24 to 160 nm by Basceri et al., 1997). A widely used description treats a thin, low-permittivity layer at each electrode interface as a capacitor in series with the interior of the film — the series arithmetic of §4 — so that the thinner the film, the more those layers dominate; small grains and imperfect crystallinity lower the interior value as well. For BST films in a DRAM capacitor, the notes' value is 90.

7.2 Frequency dependence

A dielectric constant is measured by applying an alternating voltage and measuring the charge that follows it, and in general the result depends on the frequency. Each polarisation mechanism can follow the field only up to some characteristic frequency, and any slower process in a film — the motion of defects or of charge at grain boundaries — stops contributing once the field alternates faster than it can respond, so k falls as the frequency rises. This fall is called dispersion. The permittivity of perovskite-oxide films in particular tends to decrease at high frequencies (notes p. 60; Wolf p. 162). A DRAM capacitor is written and read in nanoseconds, so its capacitance must hold at the rates of circuit operation, not only at the low frequencies of a laboratory capacitance meter.

BST's dispersion is small. For MOCVD-deposited BST films, the notes give a decrease in k of 2% from 100 Hz to 10 MHz and 7% from 200 MHz to 2 GHz; Wolf (p. 162), the source, states both as upper bounds, "by < 2%" and "by < 7%", and judges them sufficient for future DRAM generations. BST's standing as the leading candidate rested on two separate properties: its high dielectric constant, and the small frequency dependence of that dielectric constant (Wolf p. 162).

High k and small dispersion are two separate reasons

The notes (p. 60) link the two causally: "Because of the high-k value, BST films exhibit relatively small frequency dependence." The passage they condense does not. Wolf (p. 162) says BST films are the leading candidates "because of their high dielectric constant … and because the value of k in BST films exhibits a relatively small frequency dependence" — two independent reasons joined by "and". A large k does not imply a flat frequency response: the size of the permittivity and how well it holds up with frequency are separate properties of a film, and the measured dispersion is a property that had to be established by measurement. The notes' numbers (2%, 7%) stand; the causal link does not.

7.3 What a film needs to reach high k

To obtain the high k values, the notes (p. 61) state, the films must be free of impurities, have the correct stoichiometry, and be crystalline. Each follows from §6. The large k is a property of the ordered perovskite framework, with its titanium ions loosely held in corner-sharing octahedra; an amorphous film has no such framework and does not show it. That is why the adducted precursors, whose films grown at low temperature are mostly amorphous, could not serve the conformal low-temperature process, and why that process needed the non-adducted precursors, which give perovskite BST as deposited (§8.3). Impurities — carbon and hydrogen from organometallic precursors are the obvious ones — disrupt that framework, and so does a cation ratio away from the perovskite's one A per B.

The stoichiometry that matters most is the titanium content. The electrical properties are determined by the amount of titanium in the film, expressed as a percentage of all the cations (notes p. 63):

Ti% = [Ti][Ba] + [Sr] + [Ti] × 100
(7b.5)
[Ti], [Ba], [Sr] amounts of each cation in the film, in any common unit (atoms per unit volume, atomic per cent, moles) — only their ratio enters · Ti% titanium as a percentage of all cations (%). Oxygen is not counted. A stoichiometric perovskite, one A cation (Ba or Sr) per Ti, gives exactly 50%.

The highest capacitance density is found with a slight excess of titanium, about 51%, and it is critical that the amount of titanium incorporated during deposition is tightly controlled (notes p. 63). Wolf (p. 166) quantifies the sensitivity: k drops by about 40% when the titanium content rises from 51% to 53%. In terms of the ratio of titanium to A cations, 51% is 0.51/0.49 = 1.04 — four per cent more titanium than A sites — and 53% is 0.53/0.47 = 1.13. A two-point change in the percentage that is easy to overlook is a nine-point change in the Ti:A ratio, and it removes two-fifths of the dielectric constant.

The last parameter the notes name is surface roughness: increased surface roughness correlates with a lower k and higher leakage currents (notes p. 63). Wolf (pp. 166–167) adds how roughness arises and how it is seen. It can be inherited from a rough underlayer or produced when the film grows as separate islands rather than layer by layer; rough films look hazy to the eye under bright light; and the growth-mode roughness is very sensitive to the deposition conditions, with less haze generally at deposition temperatures above 600 °C.

8 · Depositing BST

A BST film for a DRAM must satisfy §7's conditions — crystalline, stoichiometric to within a per cent of titanium, free of impurities, smooth — and it must also coat the top and sides of a storage node conformally. Those demands pull the deposition temperature in opposite directions, and the notes' pp. 61–62 are about how that conflict was handled. The method that won is metal-organic CVD, for reasons of conformality and composition control.

8.1 Four methods, and why MOCVD

The notes (p. 61) list four deposition methods:

  • PVD by RF sputtering — physical vapour deposition in which argon ions from a plasma knock atoms out of a solid target of the material, and the atoms travel to the wafer. The plasma is driven at radio frequency because the target is an insulator, on which a DC discharge would charge up.
  • Thermal MOCVD — metal-organic precursor vapours react on the heated wafer surface, as in §5.
  • Sol-gel — a solution of metal-organic compounds is spun onto the wafer, converted to a gel, and then fired to form the oxide.
  • Pulsed laser ablation — short, intense laser pulses vaporise material from a target, and the resulting plume deposits on the wafer.

The deciding property is conformality. MOCVD realises high conformality, 80%, compared with sputtering and sol-gel, 60% (notes p. 61); Wolf (p. 163) gives these as bounds — MOCVD can exceed 80%, while sputtered and sol-gel films are typically below 60%. The difference follows from how material arrives. Sputtered atoms travel in nearly straight lines from the target, so the walls of a narrow feature shadow its lower parts; a spun-on liquid flows toward low areas rather than following the surface; a CVD precursor reaches every surface as a gas and reacts there, and in the right regime (§5.1) it reacts equally everywhere.

Sputtering has a second difficulty, which the notes compress into one line: the life of the sputtering target in a composite target, as the sputtering rates of the different elements vary. To sputter BST, the target must itself contain barium, strontium, titanium and oxygen, and Wolf (p. 163) states the consequence without a mechanism: because the elements sputter at different rates, the useful lifetime of such a target is too short. For both reasons, BST film deposition is most commonly done by MOCVD.

The mechanism lies beyond the notes. When a multicomponent target first sputters, the faster-sputtering elements leave its surface first, and the top few nanometres become enriched in the slower ones until a steady state is reached; from then on the sputtered flux carries the target's own composition. The film's composition therefore depends on how faithfully that steady state is held and on what happens to each species after it leaves the target: the elements leave with different angular distributions and scatter differently in the gas, and in oxide sputtering energetic negative oxygen ions from the target resputter the growing film (Ohring, ch. 5). Any change in the target surface or the discharge over the target's life shifts the film composition, and a BST film needs its titanium content held to about one per cent (§7.3).

8.2 Delivering the precursors

The MOCVD precursors for BST are solids at room temperature. They are dissolved in an organic solvent for delivery as a liquid to a vaporiser; the liquid is flash vaporised, and then transported as a gas to the CVD chamber (notes pp. 61–62). Flash vaporisation means that the liquid is metered in small quantities onto a hot surface and vaporised at once, instead of a reservoir of precursor being held hot to generate vapour continuously. The notes give the reason: it minimises the time the precursors spend at elevated temperature, which minimises their decomposition before they reach the wafer. The price, Wolf adds (p. 164), is chemical: the solvent enters the reactor with the precursor.

The precursors themselves are adducts of barium and strontium β-diketonates together with organometallic titanium compounds (notes p. 62; the notes write "β-diketones"). A β-diketone is an organic molecule with two C=O groups separated by one carbon atom; losing a hydrogen from that central carbon turns it into the β-diketonate anion, which grips a metal ion through both oxygens. The one used for BST is THD, 2,2,6,6-tetramethyl-3,5-heptanedionate, and each Ba²⁺ or Sr²⁺ carries two of them, written Ba(THD)₂ and Sr(THD)₂. Barium and strontium are large ions, and two THD ligands leave room around them; an adduct fills that room with an additional neutral molecule bound to the metal — tetraglyme or PMDTA in the precursors Wolf lists — to give a discrete, volatile molecule. The titanium source is (THD)₂Ti(i-Pr-O)₂, titanium carrying two THD and two isopropoxide groups (Wolf p. 164).

8.3 The temperature trade-off

The notes (p. 62) state the conflict directly. Low-temperature CVD processes for BST give better conformality than high-temperature ones, and a trade-off occurs between a high deposition temperature, to ensure good crystallinity of the perovskite phase, and a low temperature, to achieve adequate conformality. The conformality half is the regime argument of §5.1. The Arrhenius plot of BST deposition from the non-adducted precursors of the next paragraph shows two regimes with a crossover near 480 °C (Figure 11; Wolf pp. 164–165). Above it, the deposition rate is nearly independent of temperature and growth is limited by mass transport from the vapour; the precursor reacts as soon as it arrives and has no time to diffuse into narrow features, so conformality is poor. Below it, growth is limited by the surface reaction; the precursor molecules can diffuse a long way over the surface before they react, and the film is conformal. The crystallinity half is the other side of the same coin: the perovskite phase forms more readily the hotter the surface.

Wolf's account (pp. 164–165) also shows that the two ends of the trade-off belong to two different precursor chemistries. Adducted β-diketonates dissolved in butyl acetate give BST films of good quality at deposition temperatures around 550 °C, but their conformality there is typically poor and may not be acceptable for a three-dimensional capacitor; at low temperature the same precursors give films that are mostly amorphous. A second set of precursors — the non-adducted β-diketonates, dissolved in tetrahydrofuran — produces perovskite BST in the low-temperature regime, with capacitance densities above 100 fF/µm² on planar capacitors, leakage currents in the 10⁻⁸ A/cm² range, and 100% conformality at 480 °C. Low deposition temperature has one more advantage when the bottom electrode is ruthenium: it keeps the ruthenium surface from oxidising in the oxygen of the BST deposition.

480–550 °C and 80–100% are two regimes, not one window

The notes (p. 62) write that several precursors have been used in the temperature range of 480–550 °C "to achieve better conformality (80–100%) acceptable for 3-D capacitor fabrication". The range and the conformality belong to different processes. At the 550 °C end, with adducted precursors, the films are crystalline but poorly conformal. At the 480 °C end, with non-adducted precursors, conformality reaches 100% — and 480 °C is the crossover below which the non-adducted deposition is reaction-limited (Wolf pp. 164–165). Read literally, the notes' sentence would place a conformal process at 550 °C, where Wolf reports poor conformality for the adducted chemistry — consistent with transport-limited growth, though Wolf's crossover is measured for the non-adducted precursors only. The notes' numbers stand as the range of temperatures used; the conformal process is the low-temperature one.

8.4 The post-deposition anneal

Films deposited at low temperature are then annealed to achieve the desired capacitance and leakage-current behaviour, and the annealing is done after the top electrode has been deposited, at 500–700 °C, in oxidising (N₂O, O₂) or inert (N₂) ambients (notes p. 62). Annealing after the top electrode has a specific purpose: if the top electrode is deposited by a method that bombards the BST surface with energetic species, such as sputtering, the anneal also heals the damage it did (Wolf p. 165). The temperature depends on how the film was grown. Films grown at the higher temperature, about 550 °C, with adducted precursors need only about 550 °C in oxygen; low-temperature depositions need temperatures closer to 700 °C. The main benefit is a large reduction in leakage; the capacitance also rises, by roughly 25% (Wolf pp. 165–166).

9 · Integrating BST

A BST film contains a large amount of oxygen and has to be annealed in oxygen-containing ambients at 500–700 °C (§8.4). Everything in contact with it during those steps must survive them, and after deposition the whole stack must be patterned into individual capacitors. The notes' pp. 63–64 collect the consequences under "BST integration issues", and close with a complete process flow.

9.1 Which electrodes survive

The notes (p. 63) rule out the electrode materials used until then. Aluminium cannot be used as the bottom electrode, because many of the process steps needed for integration run at temperatures above 600 °C. TiN is not very stable against oxidising species at temperatures above 500 °C. Wolf (p. 167) adds the third member of the list: polysilicon is also excluded, because it oxidises, either with oxygen that diffuses out of the BST film or with the oxygen of the annealing ambient — the interfacial-oxide problem of §4 in its most severe form.

The aluminium limit: 600 °C in the notes, 500 °C in the source

The notes give the reason aluminium is excluded as process temperatures "higher than 600 °C". Wolf (p. 167), the passage the notes condense, gives the BST anneals as "> 500 °C", the same threshold it gives for TiN. The notes' figure is 600 °C. Either temperature excludes aluminium by a wide margin: it melts at 660 °C, oxidises readily, and in ordinary silicon processing nothing after its deposition is allowed to exceed about 450 °C — the reason the hydrogen passivation anneal of M05b is capped there.

What remains are the platinum-group metals of §4. The notes (p. 63) name the electrode materials as Pt, Ir and Ru, and the oxides of Ir and Ru, which also conduct, and add that dry etching of these metals is challenging. Platinum is the electrode most used for BST in development work, because it does not react with BST and BST grows well on it (Wolf p. 167). It also forms silicides, so it needs a barrier between it and the silicon below; the notes (p. 63) specify a Ti/TiN diffusion barrier, and because platinum adheres poorly the same layer serves as its glue. The whole bottom-electrode stack must then resist oxidation and stay thermally stable through the BST deposition and anneals. Wolf (p. 168) lists TiN, TiAlN and TiSiN among the barriers investigated for the layer between the bottom electrode and the polysilicon plug.

Platinum is a poorer choice for the top electrode, for a reason tied to the end of the process. The last thermal step of a wafer is a forming-gas anneal in hydrogen, which passivates interface traps (M05b); platinum catalyses the reduction of BST by that hydrogen instead of protecting it (Wolf p. 167). The notes (p. 64) list the top-electrode materials: Pt, Ru, RuO₂, IrO₂, and conducting perovskite oxides such as SrRuO₃ and (La,Sr)CoO₃. RuO₂ is easy to etch, with the RuO₄ by-product of §4.

Platinum in the top-electrode list: the notes' addition

The notes' list of top-electrode materials is the course's and begins with Pt. Wolf's corresponding list (p. 168) — Ru, RuO₂, IrO₂, SrRuO₃ and (La,Sr)CoO₃, "especially for the top electrode" — does not include platinum, consistent with his warning on p. 167 that platinum catalyses the reduction of BST in the forming-gas anneal and "is not a good candidate for the top electrode". The two statements on this page come from the two sources, and they disagree on platinum only.

9.2 Patterning without volatile products

Dry etching — plasma etching — normally removes a film by converting it into volatile compounds that are pumped away; the ions of the plasma supply energy and direction, the chemistry supplies the volatility. BST, like the other metal oxides and perovskites and the noble metals, does not have volatile by-products, so its etching must be more physical, like ion milling, and small feature sizes hinder that technique (notes p. 64). Ion milling removes material by sputtering it with energetic inert-gas ions. With no chemistry to help, the removal rate differs little between the film and the mask, and the sputtered material can land again on the sidewalls of the feature being etched; both limit how small and how vertical a feature can be. Wolf (p. 168) lists the approaches under development: dual-frequency plasmas, in which the ion energy is set separately to maximise the physical effect while exploiting whatever chemistry exists, and etching at high substrate temperature, above 350 °C, to improve etch rate and profile.

Ruthenium and RuO₂ are the exceptions. In O₂/Cl₂ plasmas they form RuO₄, which is volatile, and they etch chemically with fully anisotropic profiles, so a storage node of ruthenium can be patterned with vertical sidewalls (Wolf p. 168). That is why the process flow the notes give is built around ruthenium. The notes (p. 64) list the tools that introducing these materials requires: a sputtering system for electrode deposition, an MOCVD reactor for the high-k layer, and a reactive etching system for patterning.

9.3 The Ru/BST/Ru capacitor, step by step

The notes (p. 64) give the process flow for Ru/BST/Ru capacitors, after Wolf's Fig. 4-14, as a list of steps beside four cross-sections, redrawn in Figure 12. The capacitor is stacked, as in Figure 4, on polysilicon plugs that connect each storage node down to its pass transistor.

  1. Polysilicon plug formation. Each plug fills a contact hole through the interlevel dielectric down to a transistor's source/drain.
  2. Barrier metal TiN/Ti deposition. The barrier keeps the ruthenium from reacting with the polysilicon of the plug during the BST deposition and anneals.
  3. Ru deposition — the storage-node metal, deposited by sputtering as the tooling list specifies.
  4. SiO₂ deposition — a layer that will become a hard mask (panel a).
  5. Lithography — the storage-node pattern is printed in photoresist.
  6. SiO₂ etch — the pattern is transferred into the SiO₂. An organic photoresist would itself be removed by the oxygen-containing plasma that etches ruthenium, so the SiO₂ carries the pattern instead.
  7. Ru and barrier metal etch — the ruthenium and the TiN/Ti are etched through between the hard-mask blocks, leaving one storage node over each plug (panel b).
  8. SiO₂ removal — the hard mask is stripped.
  9. BST deposition, low-temperature two-step CVD (panel c): a first deposition of a thin buffer layer, a first anneal, a second deposition of the main layer, and a second anneal. The low temperature gives the conformal coverage of §8.3 over the top and sides of each ruthenium storage node, and keeps the ruthenium from oxidising during deposition.
  10. Ru deposition — the top electrode, the common cell plate, over the whole array (panel d).

10 · Ferroelectric memory

The third application the notes name for a high-k dielectric is the capacitor of a non-volatile ferroelectric memory, FeRAM (pp. 49, 57). A non-volatile memory keeps its data with the power removed, which a DRAM cannot: its charge leaks away within the retention time of §1. A ferroelectric capacitor stores its bit not as a quantity of charge but as the direction of a remanent polarisation, one of the two stable states at zero field in Figure 10, and that polarisation persists without power. The notes call FeRAM an emerging area.

10.1 The materials

The notes (p. 57) name two ferroelectric dielectrics — PZT, lead zirconium titanate, and SBT, which they expand as strontium bismuth titanate — and give k = 1000–1500. Wolf (p. 154), the source, attaches the 1000–1500 to PZT alone; SBT's value is far lower (below). PZT — in standard usage lead zirconate titanate — is the perovskite Pb(Zr,Ti)O₃, a solid solution of lead zirconate and lead titanate in which Zr⁴⁺ and Ti⁴⁺ share the B site of Figure 8 and lead occupies the A site. Unlike the BST used for DRAM, its composition is chosen to place the Curie point well above the operating temperature, so it is ferroelectric in use (§6.3). Wolf states the consequence: because the ferroelectric polarisation is retained almost indefinitely, no refresh is needed, and early FeRAMs were used in game cartridges, where they held saved data without a battery.

SBT is strontium bismuth tantalate, and its k is not 1000–1500

The notes (p. 57) expand SBT as "Strontium Bismuth Titanate", as Wolf (p. 154) does. The ferroelectric known as SBT is SrBi₂Ta₂O₉, strontium bismuth tantalate: it contains tantalum, not titanium. It is a layered perovskite — perovskite-like slabs separated by bismuth oxide layers — rather than the simple ABO₃ of Figure 8. It became important for FeRAM because capacitors made from it with platinum electrodes do not suffer fatigue, the gradual loss of switchable polarisation after many switching cycles, which PZT on platinum does (Paz de Araujo et al., 1995).

The notes' list places k = 1000–1500 beside both materials; Wolf gives it for PZT only. SBT is a low-permittivity ferroelectric: its films are reported with k of a few hundred at most, typically about 180–300 depending on thickness, grain size and processing. The notes' 1000–1500 is the course's figure for the pair; it describes PZT.

10.2 The stacked FeRAM cell

The notes (p. 57) draw an FeRAM cell, Figure 13, redrawn from Wolf's Fig. 4-6. It is a 1T1C cell like the DRAM cell of §1, with a stacked ferroelectric capacitor in place of the DRAM capacitor. The pass transistor sits in a p-well, with a bit-line contact to one n⁺ region and a word-line gate over the channel. A polysilicon plug rises from the other n⁺ region to the capacitor, which is built as a stack: a barrier layer on the plug, a platinum bottom electrode, the ferroelectric layer, and a platinum top electrode, with a contact above. Field oxide isolates the cell from its neighbour.

Reading the cell differs from DRAM in one respect, beyond the notes. The bit is read by driving the capacitor with a voltage pulse through the plate and sensing the charge that flows onto the bit line: if the pulse reverses the polarisation, the switching releases a large charge, and if the polarisation already points the way the pulse drives it, only a small one. The two charges distinguish "1" from "0". A read that switches the polarisation destroys the stored state, so, as in a DRAM, the sense amplifier writes the value back (Scott & Paz de Araujo, 1989).

The notes (p. 57) list the considerations for integrating these capacitors: controlling the interfacial reactions between the dielectric and the electrode materials, maintaining the thermal stability of the electrodes, and developing plasma-etching processes. Wolf (p. 154) observes that they are the same challenges met in bringing high-k dielectrics into the DRAM, and they are the three problems of §4 and §9: electrodes that do not form low-k or reacted layers against an oxide processed in oxygen, barriers and electrodes that survive the anneals, and patterning of noble metals and perovskites whose etch products are not volatile.
