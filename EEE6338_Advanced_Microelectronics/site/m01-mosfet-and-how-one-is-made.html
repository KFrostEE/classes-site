Module 01 · Background

The MOSFET and How One Is Made

The metal–oxide–semiconductor field-effect transistor: what the structure is, how a gate voltage creates a conducting channel, and the sequence of process steps that produces one.

Prerequisites none Read time ~45 min Sources Lecture 1; Streetman figs. 8-9 to 8-11; Plummer ch. 6, 10

This course is about fabrication. Its subject is the sequence of physical and chemical steps that turns a disc of silicon into several hundred million working transistors, and its organising question is what breaks in that sequence each time the transistor is made smaller. Answering that question requires a working model of the device itself: what each region does, which dimension controls which electrical parameter, and which process step establishes it.

This module supplies that model. It is background rather than new material — the course assumes it and does not teach it, and it is the one module here built from the lecture and from standard device texts rather than from the class notes. A reader who has taken a semiconductor devices course will already have all of it and can go directly to M02. Everything that follows in the course is a modification of the structure and the process described here, so the two are presented together: first the device and the physics that makes it switch, then the mask-by-mask sequence that builds it.

Assumed background

This module takes as given: the distinction between conduction and valence bands and the band gap between them; electrons and holes as the two mobile carrier species; donor and acceptor doping, and the resulting n-type and p-type material; majority and minority carriers; drift and diffusion as the two transport mechanisms; carrier mobility; and the depletion region of a reverse-biased pn junction. Silicon has a band gap of 1.12 eV at room temperature and an intrinsic carrier concentration ni of 1.5×10¹⁰ cm⁻³.

That value of ni is the classical textbook figure, and it is the one the class notes and every calculation in this course use. The currently accepted measured value is 9.65×10⁹ cm⁻³. The difference is not cosmetic: it shifts φF by about 11 mV and therefore 2φF by about 23 mV in the threshold-voltage calculation of M03. Use 1.5×10¹⁰ cm⁻³ for consistency with the course, and know that the smaller value is the physically correct one.

1 · The MOS capacitor

The gate of a MOSFET is a capacitor, and every property of the transistor that matters in this course follows from the behaviour of that capacitor. It is built by growing an insulating layer of silicon dioxide on a silicon surface and placing a conducting electrode on top. The three layers give the structure its name: metal, oxide, semiconductor.

The distinguishing feature of this capacitor is that one of its two plates is a semiconductor rather than a metal. A metal plate holds any charge placed on it in a sheet a fraction of a nanometre thick, because its carrier density is around 10²² cm⁻³ and the charge needed is trivially available. Silicon doped at 10¹⁵ to 10¹⁶ cm⁻³ has six or seven orders of magnitude fewer carriers, so the charge that balances the gate must be gathered from a layer hundreds of nanometres deep, and the silicon side of the capacitor develops an internal electric field and a position-dependent potential. That field is the mechanism the transistor uses.

Because the oxide is an insulator, no steady current flows between the gate and the silicon. The gate acts purely through the electric field it imposes, which is the origin of the term field-effect. The capacitance per unit area of the structure follows the parallel-plate result, with the oxide as the dielectric:

Cox = εox ε0tox
(1.1)
Cox oxide capacitance per unit area (F/cm²) · εox relative permittivity of SiO₂, 3.9, dimensionless · ε0 permittivity of free space, 8.85×10⁻¹⁴ F/cm · tox oxide thickness (cm). This course works in centimetres throughout, so ε0 takes the F/cm value and a thickness quoted in ångströms must be converted with 1 Å = 10⁻⁸ cm.

Capacitance per unit area is the form used everywhere in this course, and the division by area is performed immediately rather than carried through the algebra. A capacitance for a specific device is recovered by multiplying by the gate area at the end.

2 · What a gate voltage does to the silicon

Consider a p-type substrate, in which holes are the majority carriers and the fixed, ionised acceptor atoms carry a matching negative charge. Applying a voltage to the gate induces a compensating charge in the silicon, and the sign of that voltage determines which of three physically distinct responses occurs.

A negative gate voltage draws holes toward the surface. The hole concentration at the oxide interface rises above its bulk value, and the required charge is supplied within a very thin surface layer because holes are abundant. This is accumulation.

A small positive gate voltage repels holes from the surface. They move into the bulk, leaving behind the ionised acceptor atoms, which are fixed in the lattice and cannot move. The surface region is now stripped of mobile carriers and carries a net negative charge from those exposed acceptors. This is depletion, and the layer is a depletion region of exactly the kind found at a reverse-biased pn junction. Increasing the gate voltage widens it, because more exposed acceptor charge is needed to balance the larger gate charge.

A large positive gate voltage does something qualitatively different. Once the surface has been depleted, further increases in gate voltage bend the silicon bands far enough that the surface becomes energetically favourable for electrons — the minority carriers. A thin layer of mobile electrons forms at the oxide interface, on top of the depletion region beneath it. The surface of a p-type substrate has become n-type. This is inversion, and the electron layer is the channel. The gate voltage at which the channel is taken to have formed is the threshold voltage VT; §3 puts it to work, and M03 derives it term by term.

The transition to inversion is gradual in principle and is made definite by a convention. The Fermi potential φF measures how far the Fermi level sits from the middle of the band gap in the neutral bulk, and it is fixed by the doping:

φF = kTq ln (NAni)
(1.2)
φF Fermi potential (V) · kT/q thermal voltage, 0.0259 V at T = 300 K · NA acceptor concentration (cm⁻³) · ni intrinsic carrier concentration, 1.5×10¹⁰ cm⁻³ for silicon. The logarithm is natural, not base ten. For an n-type substrate the Fermi level lies above midgap and the potential changes sign: φF = −(kT/q) ln(ND/ni). The magnitude comes from the same expression with ND in place of NA, but the minus sign has to be put in deliberately — substituting alone returns a positive number for either doping type.

Strong inversion is defined as the condition in which the bands at the surface have bent by twice this amount. At that point the electron concentration at the surface equals the hole concentration in the bulk, so the surface is as strongly n-type as the substrate is p-type:

φs = 2φF
(1.3)
φs surface potential, the total band bending from the bulk to the oxide interface (V) · φF Fermi potential from (1.2) (V). This is a definition adopted by convention, not a sharp physical threshold; inversion charge exists below it and grows continuously through it. Its value is that it makes the threshold voltage a well-defined quantity, and the factor of two appears directly in the threshold expression as the term 2φF.

Figure 3 shows only the semiconductor, which is where the physics of interest happens, but it leaves out two thirds of the structure. The complete picture puts the gate and the oxide on the same energy axis as the silicon, and it is worth having because it makes visible something the semiconductor-only diagram cannot: where the applied gate voltage actually goes. A gate voltage divides between the oxide and the silicon, and every quantity in the threshold voltage equation of M03 is a statement about one part or other of that division.

Three conventions make the full diagram readable. Vertical position is electron energy, so up is higher energy for an electron and therefore lower electrostatic potential — a positive voltage on the gate pushes the gate's bands down. Horizontal position is distance through the structure, gate on the left, then oxide, then silicon. And within the oxide, the slope of the band edge is proportional to the electric field: the vertical axis carries energy rather than potential, so the gradient of the band edge is qℰ and not ℰ itself. Nothing in reading the figure depends on the factor of q — a steeper slope still means a larger field, and a flat band edge still means no field at all — provided the vertical drop across the oxide is read as qVox, an energy, which divided by q gives the voltage Vox.

Two features of this diagram carry most of its value. First, the gate voltage is shared: VG − VFB = Vox + ψs, so raising the gate voltage past threshold adds almost entirely to Vox rather than to ψs. The reason is the exponential dependence of inversion charge on surface potential: the electron density at the surface goes as exp(qψs/kT), so once the inversion layer exists, a further millivolt of surface potential supplies a large amount of additional charge. Further gate charge is therefore balanced by more inversion electrons rather than by deeper depletion, and ψs creeps rather than climbs. That is the sense in which the surface potential is pinned near 2φF, and why the depletion width is treated as stopping at Wm, the maximum depletion width used in M03 §4. Both are excellent approximations rather than exact statements — ψs does continue to rise slowly above threshold, by of order a few kT/q per decade of inversion charge.

Second, the oxide band edge in these diagrams is drawn as a straight line, which encodes an assumption: that the oxide carries no charge, so the field inside it is uniform. Real oxides carry charge, and charge inside the film makes the line depart from straight — a thin sheet of charge produces a sharp kink at its position, while charge spread through the film curves the line continuously. Either way the departure shifts the gate voltage needed to reach inversion. That is the threshold voltage shift, and locating the charge inside the oxide is the subject of M05 §6. The same diagram redrawn with the oxide conduction band as a barrier to be crossed rather than a region to drop voltage across is where M06 §1 begins.

3 · The n-channel MOSFET

A MOS capacitor that can invert its surface becomes a transistor once two heavily doped n-type regions are added, one at each end of the channel. These are the source and the drain. They are written n⁺ because their doping, typically 10¹⁹ to 10²⁰ cm⁻³, is three to five orders of magnitude above the substrate. Their purpose is to supply electrons to the channel and collect them from it, which a p-type substrate cannot do on its own.

With the source and substrate grounded and a positive voltage on the drain, the structure contains two back-to-back pn junctions. The drain-side junction is reverse biased, its depletion region blocks carrier flow, and the current between source and drain is negligible regardless of how large the drain voltage becomes. The transistor is off.

Raising the gate voltage above the threshold voltage VT inverts the surface between the two n⁺ regions. The channel is now n-type and continuous with the source and drain, so the blocking junction no longer exists along that path: there is an unbroken n-type route from source to drain. Electrons drift along it under the drain field, and drain current flows. The transistor is on.

Because the channel is created by adding electrons to a region that had none, this device is called an enhancement-mode n-channel MOSFET. Both the threshold voltage and the amount of charge in the channel above threshold are set by the capacitor of §1, which is why Cox appears in every current expression that follows.

4 · The drain characteristic

Once a channel exists, the charge per unit area available to carry current at any point along it is set by how far the local gate-to-channel voltage exceeds threshold. Near the source that excess is VGS − VT. Near the drain the channel has been pulled up toward the drain potential, so the excess is VGS − VT − VDS. The channel is therefore thickest at the source end and thinnest at the drain end, and integrating the drift current along its length gives the drain current in the linear region:

When VDS reaches VGS − VT, the gate-to-channel voltage at the drain end falls to exactly the threshold and the inversion charge there goes to zero. The channel is said to pinch off at that end. Raising the drain voltage further does not increase the current: the extra voltage is dropped across the short depleted region that has appeared near the drain, while the rest of the channel continues to see the same conditions. 5 · The p-channel MOSFET

Every polarity in the preceding sections can be reversed. Starting from an n-type substrate, in which electrons are the majority carriers, and forming two p⁺ regions for the source and drain, a negative gate voltage repels electrons from the surface, depletes it, and eventually inverts it to p-type. The channel then consists of holes, and the device is a p-channel MOSFET.

The two device types are not electrically symmetric, for one material reason. Hole mobility in silicon is roughly a third of electron mobility — 500 cm²/V·s against 1450 cm²/V·s for bulk drift mobility in lightly doped material at 300 K. Both fall in an inversion layer, but the ratio between them survives. By equation (1.5), a p-channel device of the same dimensions therefore delivers roughly a third of the current at the same gate overdrive. In circuit layout this is compensated by making the p-channel device wider, and that asymmetry persists into every CMOS process in this course.

Doping species in silicon

Two elements do nearly all the work. Phosphorus and arsenic are group-V donors and produce n-type silicon; phosphorus diffuses quickly and was the standard choice for diffused source and drain regions, while arsenic moves far less at the same thermal budget, which later made it preferable for shallow junctions. Boron is the group-III acceptor and produces p-type silicon. The difference in speed is not a matter of atomic mass. Substitutional dopants diffuse by pairing with point defects, and the species differ in which defect carries them: boron and phosphorus move largely with silicon self-interstitials, which is fast, while arsenic and antimony move predominantly by a vacancy mechanism, which is slow. Indium and antimony appear later in the course as low-diffusivity substitutes for boron and phosphorus, chosen because a profile implanted with them stays where it was put through the anneals that follow — see M09.

6 · CMOS and the inverter

Neither device type is used alone in modern digital circuits. Complementary MOS, or CMOS, places an n-channel and a p-channel transistor on the same die and wires them so that one conducts whenever the other does not. The elementary CMOS circuit is the inverter, and it is built by connecting both gates to the input, both drains to the output, the p-channel source to the positive supply rail VDD, and the n-channel source to ground.

With the input at ground, the n-channel gate-to-source voltage is zero, which is below its positive threshold, so that device is off. The p-channel gate sits at VDD below its source, which is beyond its negative threshold, so that device is on. The output is pulled up to VDD. With the input at VDD the situation reverses exactly: the n-channel device conducts, the p-channel device does not, and the output is pulled down to ground.

In both stable states one transistor in the pair is off, so no continuous path exists from supply to ground and the circuit draws current only while switching. That property is what made CMOS displace every earlier logic family, and it is also what makes the off-state leakage current a central concern later in this course: when a transistor that is nominally off conducts anyway, the defining advantage of CMOS erodes. A chip contains hundreds of millions of these pairs, and a leakage current that is negligible in one device is not negligible when multiplied by that number.

7 · Building an n-channel MOSFET

The device described above is produced by a sequence of steps in which layers are grown or deposited over the whole wafer and then selectively removed. Nothing is built up locally; every pattern is made by covering the whole surface and taking material away where it is not wanted. The sequence below is the classic three-mask aluminium-gate process. It is obsolete — later modules replace almost every step in it — but it establishes the vocabulary, and each replacement in the rest of the course is easier to justify against this baseline.

7.1 · Oxidation

Silicon reacts with oxygen to form silicon dioxide, and the resulting layer adheres to the wafer, is chemically stable, and is an excellent insulator. The wafer is loaded into a quartz tube in a resistively heated furnace at 1000–1100 °C and exposed to an oxidising ambient. Two chemistries are used:

ProcessReactionRelative rateUsed for
Dry oxidation Si + O₂ → SiO₂ Slow Thin gate oxides, where quality matters more than throughput
Wet oxidation Si + 2H₂O → SiO₂ + 2H₂ Ten to twenty times faster at the same temperature Thick masking and field oxides

Wet oxidation is faster for a reason worth stating precisely, because the obvious explanation is the wrong one. The two oxidants diffuse through silicon dioxide at comparable rates; what separates them is solubility. Silicon dioxide dissolves about 3×10¹⁹ cm⁻³ of water against about 5×10¹⁶ cm⁻³ of molecular oxygen at 1100 °C, because water reacts with the glass network to form Si–OH pairs while oxygen must sit interstitially. That difference in the concentration available at the outer surface — a factor of six hundred — makes the gradient driving oxidant across the film far steeper, and it is large enough to overcome the fact that two water molecules are consumed for every one of oxygen. The reaction takes place at the silicon surface underneath the oxide already grown, not at the outer surface, which means the oxidant must cross the existing layer for growth to continue. Growth therefore slows as the film thickens, and it consumes silicon as it proceeds — the interface moves downward into the wafer. The quantitative model for this, and the reason it fails for the very thin oxides that matter in this course, is the subject of M05.

Steam for wet oxidation is generated by bubbling oxygen through deionised water held near 95 °C. The resulting ambient is mostly H₂O with some residual O₂ and is called wet O₂; it is the ambient the standard wet-oxidation rate constants are measured in. A nitrogen carrier, which gives a nominally pure steam ambient, was also used and is what Lecture 1 described. Modern furnaces mostly abandon the bubbler altogether and burn hydrogen and oxygen at the back of the tube to make water directly, which is called pyrogenic steam. The purity of that water matters. Ordinary tap water carries dissolved sodium, magnesium, zinc and other metal ions, and its resistivity is only 1–5 kΩ·cm; deionised water is passed through ion-exchange columns until it reaches about 18 MΩ·cm, close to the theoretical maximum for pure water at room temperature. Sodium in particular is mobile in silicon dioxide, and sodium contamination of a gate oxide produces a threshold voltage that drifts under bias.

7.2 · The masking sequence

Patterning is performed by photolithography. A light-sensitive polymer called photoresist is spun onto the wafer as a liquid and baked to a solid film. A mask — a transparent plate carrying opaque patterns — is aligned over the wafer and the assembly is exposed to ultraviolet light. Exposure changes the solubility of the resist where light reached it, and a developer then removes the soluble regions, leaving a resist stencil that reproduces the mask pattern. The layer underneath is etched wherever the stencil leaves it exposed, and the remaining resist is stripped.

Silicon dioxide is removed with buffered oxide etch, a solution of hydrofluoric acid buffered with ammonium fluoride. Hydrofluoric acid attacks SiO₂ but not silicon; the ammonium fluoride replenishes fluoride ions as they are consumed, so the etch rate stays constant instead of falling away as the acid is depleted, which is what makes a controlled, reproducible etch possible.

7.3 · The full sequence

Beginning with a p-type wafer doped at 10¹⁵ to 10¹⁶ cm⁻³:

StepOperationPurpose
1Grow ≈1 µm of SiO₂ by wet oxidation at 1000–1100 °CDiffusion mask
2Spin photoresist; expose through mask 1; develop; etch the oxide in buffered oxide etch; strip resistOpen windows over the future source and drain
3Diffuse phosphorus at 1000–1150 °CForm the n⁺ source and drain. Dopant enters only through the windows
4Strip all remaining oxide; grow ≈0.1 µm of SiO₂ by dry oxidationThe gate dielectric. Grown dry for interface quality
5Photoresist; mask 2; etch contact windows through the thin oxideExpose bare silicon over the source and drain for metal contact
6Evaporate aluminium over the entire wafer in a vacuum systemConductor for gate and contacts
7Photoresist; mask 3; etch the aluminiumSeparate the blanket metal into source, gate and drain electrodes

Two features of this sequence carry through the whole course. The first is that the oxide is the diffusion mask: dopant atoms diffusing at 1100 °C move slowly enough through SiO₂ that a micron of it blocks them for the duration of the drive-in, while they pass freely into the silicon exposed in the windows. One material therefore provides both the gate insulator and the barrier that confines the diffusion — though the oxide is itself patterned by the resist, not by itself. The second is that each mask must be aligned to the pattern left by the one before it. Alignment marks etched at the first step are used to register each subsequent exposure. Mask 3 defines the aluminium, and therefore where the gate electrode sits; a misregistration between mask 1 and mask 3 places the gate off-centre over the channel, leaving a gap at one end that no gate voltage can invert, and the device then fails to conduct at all. Alignment tolerance is one of the limits that forced the abandonment of this process, and its replacement — the self-aligned gate, in which the gate itself defines where the source and drain go — is treated in M10.

Wet etching undercuts the mask

Buffered oxide etch is a liquid, and it removes material at the same rate in every direction. While the etch proceeds downward through the oxide it also proceeds sideways underneath the resist stencil, by the same distance. Etching through 1 µm of oxide therefore widens the opening by about 1 µm on each side, so a 2 µm window in the resist yields a 4 µm window in the oxide. The error is tolerable when features are tens of microns across and fatal when they are one micron across. The directional plasma processes that replace wet etching for fine features appear in M10.

8 · Channel length, channel width, and why the length shrinks

Two dimensions of the finished device set its electrical behaviour, and they are measured in the plane of the wafer surface rather than in the cross-sections drawn so far. The channel length L is the source-to-drain separation, measured along the direction of current flow. The channel width Z is the perpendicular dimension, measured across the current path.

Equations (1.4) and (1.5) show that drain current is proportional to Z/L. Reducing the length therefore increases the current a single device can deliver, which charges the capacitance of the next stage faster and makes the circuit switch faster. It simultaneously reduces the area the device occupies, so more devices fit in a given piece of silicon. Both effects push in the same direction, and the history of the industry follows from that coincidence: the channel length has been reduced in every generation since the first commercial MOS integrated circuits.

The figures down to 0.13 µm are physical channel lengths. The ones after it are technology node names, and the two quantities separated at roughly that point — with the physical gate length running shorter than the label. At the 90 nm node the printed gate length was about 50 nm; at 65 nm it was about 35 nm; at 45 nm about 25–35 nm. From the 22 nm generation onward the label detached from any physical dimension of the transistor altogether and became a marketing name, and the physical gate length has been roughly flat near 20–30 nm ever since, because short-channel effects made further reduction unprofitable. Scaling continued through device geometry and wiring pitch instead.

This matters here because L in equations (1.4) and (1.5) is the physical channel length, not the node name. Substituting "45 nm" for L in a current calculation for a 45 nm-node device gives the wrong answer.

Nothing in this module explains why that progression was difficult. Equations (1.4) and (1.5) contain L only as a divisor and would predict that shrinking it is purely beneficial. It is not, and the reason is that several other quantities must be changed alongside it: the oxide must thin, the substrate doping must rise, the junctions must become shallower, and the supply voltage must fall. Each of those changes breaks something else. The structure of the entire course is the catalogue of those breakages and their fixes, and M02 lays out that catalogue.

A process engineer proposes to grow the 0.1 µm gate oxide by wet oxidation instead of dry, to save furnace time. What is wrong with this?

Wet oxidation grows the film through the transport of water molecules across it, and the resulting oxide is less dense and contains more defects and incorporated hydrogen than one grown in dry oxygen. For a thick field oxide, whose job is only to insulate and to mask diffusion, that does not matter. For the gate oxide it matters greatly: this is the layer across which the full gate field is applied, it forms the interface along which the channel carriers travel, and its defect density sets both the breakdown voltage and the density of interface traps. The furnace time being saved is real — 0.1 µm takes on the order of an hour to grow dry at 1000–1100 °C against a few minutes wet — but it is bought back in interface quality, breakdown field and trap density, and there is no other layer in the device where those three matter as much.

Summary

  • The MOSFET gate is a capacitor with a semiconductor for one plate. Cox = εoxε0/tox, always per unit area, always in centimetre units in this course.
  • A gate voltage drives the silicon surface into accumulation, depletion, or inversion. Only inversion creates a channel. Strong inversion is defined by φs = 2φF.
  • Above threshold the channel connects the n⁺ source and drain, and drain current flows. It rises with VDS, then saturates once the channel pinches off at the drain end.
  • Drain current scales as Z/L, which is why the channel length was reduced in every generation from 1970 until it stalled near 20–30 nm. Below the 130 nm node the technology node name and the physical gate length are different numbers.
  • Hole mobility is about a third of electron mobility, so p-channel devices are made wider to match n-channel drive.
  • CMOS pairs the two types so that one is always off, and draws current only while switching. Off-state leakage attacks that property directly.
  • The classic process uses three masks. Oxide is grown wet when thick and fast matters, dry when thin and good matters, and it serves as both gate dielectric and diffusion mask.
  • Each mask must be aligned to the previous one, and wet etching undercuts the resist by roughly the depth etched. Both limits are removed later in the course.
