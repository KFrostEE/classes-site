Module 03

The Threshold Voltage Equation

The four terms that set the gate voltage at which a MOSFET turns on: where each one comes from physically, what controls it in processing, and how the four combine into a number.

Prerequisites M01, M02 Read time ~50 min Sources Class notes p. 2; Lectures 2, 3, 4

The threshold voltage is the gate voltage at which the silicon surface beneath the gate oxide reaches strong inversion and a conducting channel connects source to drain. Everything a MOSFET does electrically is referred to it: the drain current depends on VGS − VT, the supply voltage must exceed it with margin, and the off-state leakage is set by how far below it the gate sits when the device is meant to be off.

It is also the point where device physics and process engineering meet. The threshold voltage is not a design parameter that can be specified directly; it is the sum of four physically distinct contributions, each established by a different step in the fabrication sequence. Three of the nine chapters of this course exist because of one term or another in the equation below. Chapters 3 and 4 are both about the oxide capacitance Cox, and Chapter 3 is additionally where the interface charge Qi is accounted for — that charge is the reason the silicon–silicon dioxide system displaced every competitor. Chapter 5 is largely about the depletion charge Qd.

This module takes the equation apart term by term, establishes the physics behind each, and then assembles a complete numerical answer. The last two sections deal with the two levers process engineering has over the result: the choice of gate material, and the threshold-adjust implant.

VT = φMS − QiCox − QdCox + 2φF
(3.1)
VT threshold voltage (V) · φMS gate-to-semiconductor work function difference (V) · Qi interface charge per unit area (C/cm²), a charge, not a number density · Qd depletion charge per unit area (C/cm²), signed · Cox oxide capacitance per unit area (F/cm²) · φF Fermi potential of the body (V), signed. Every quantity in this course is expressed in centimetre units: ε0 = 8.85×10⁻¹⁴ F/cm, not 8.85×10⁻¹² F/m.

The first two terms dominate. The third and fourth always carry the same sign as each other, so they add rather than cancelling — together they come to about a volt for an n-channel device, and they partially offset the first two, which are each of order a volt or more and of the opposite sign. Every term is reachable by processing, but not equally: the oxide thickness moves both charge terms at once, the gate material moves the first, and the channel doping moves the last two. The sections follow the order of the equation.

1 · The work function difference, φMS

A gate voltage of zero does not mean a flat band structure. Two dissimilar materials in contact equalise their Fermi levels, and if their work functions differ, a built-in potential appears across the structure. Some of the applied gate voltage is consumed cancelling that built-in potential before any of it acts on the channel. The first term of equation (3.1) is exactly that consumed voltage.

1.1 The work function

The work function of a material is the energy required to remove an electron from it to a point just outside its surface with zero kinetic energy. That reference point is the vacuum level. In a metal, electrons fill the available states up to the Fermi level, so the work function is measured from the Fermi level to the vacuum level.

The concept comes from the photoelectric effect, which is also how work functions are measured. Light of frequency ν incident on a metal surface ejects electrons only if the photon energy hν exceeds the work function; below that threshold, no electrons are emitted regardless of how intense the light is. Einstein's explanation, that light delivers its energy in quanta of hν rather than continuously, is what makes the threshold behaviour intelligible. For aluminium the work function is 4.1 eV, which corresponds to a photon in the ultraviolet.

E [eV] = 1.24λ [µm]
(3.2)
E photon energy (eV) · λ free-space wavelength (µm). The constant is hc/q expressed so that energy in electronvolts pairs with wavelength in micrometres; its exact value is 1.2398. Use it in both directions — to find the photon energy a given wavelength carries, and to find the longest wavelength a material of given band gap can absorb.

Equation (3.2) is used constantly in this course, and not only for work functions. Silicon has a band gap of 1.12 eV, so photons of wavelength longer than 1.24/1.12 = 1.107 µm cannot excite an electron across the gap and silicon is transparent to them; at shorter wavelengths it absorbs. Gallium nitride, with a band gap near 3.4 eV, begins absorbing only below 1.24/3.4 = 0.365 µm, in the ultraviolet. The same relation sets the exposure wavelengths of lithography in M11 and M12.

1.2 Why silicon has no single work function

A metal's work function is a constant of the material. A semiconductor's is not, because the Fermi level in a semiconductor moves with doping. In n-type silicon the Fermi level lies close to the conduction band edge; in p-type silicon it lies close to the valence band edge. The distance from the Fermi level up to the vacuum level therefore differs between the two, and differs again with the doping concentration within each type.

Because the work function is not fixed, a different quantity is used as the reference for silicon: the electron affinity, the energy separating the conduction band edge from the vacuum level. Unlike the work function, it is a property of the material alone at the doping levels used in a body region, because the conduction band edge does not move with doping there. For silicon the electron affinity is 4.05 eV. Above roughly 10¹⁸ cm⁻³ the statement stops being exact: heavy doping narrows the band gap by tens of millielectronvolts, and by about 100 meV at 10²⁰ cm⁻³, which is the regime a degenerately doped polysilicon gate occupies. The 4.05 and 5.17 eV limits below are the non-degenerate end points.

The two limits in the figure bound the whole range. When silicon is doped n-type so heavily that the Fermi level reaches the conduction band edge, its work function equals the electron affinity, 4.05 eV. When it is doped p-type so heavily that the Fermi level reaches the valence band edge, its work function is the electron affinity plus the band gap, 4.05 + 1.12 = 5.17 eV. Every practical doping puts the work function somewhere between those two values.

φMS = φM − φS
(3.3)
φM work function of the gate material (eV, used as V) · φS work function of the silicon body (eV, used as V). Energies in electronvolts are used directly as potentials in volts, because dividing an energy in eV by the electronic charge gives the same number in volts. For a p-type body, φS = χ + Eg/2q + φF with χ = 4.05 V and Eg/2q = 0.56 V; for an n-type body the last term is subtracted. In this course φMS is read from a published graph of work function difference against doping rather than computed.

Reading φMS from a graph

The course reads this term off a plot of φMS against substrate doping, with separate curves for aluminium, n⁺ polysilicon and p⁺ polysilicon gates. For an aluminium gate on p-type silicon at NA = 10¹⁵ cm⁻³ the graph gives approximately −0.9 V, and that is the value used in the worked example below.

Evaluating equation (3.3) directly from the course's own constants — φM(Al) = 4.1 V, χ = 4.05 V, Eg/2q = 0.56 V, φF = 0.288 V — gives φS = 4.898 V and φMS = −0.80 V. The 0.1 V gap is systematic rather than a reading error: any curve built on φM(Al) = 4.1 V passes through −0.80 V at this doping, so a published curve reading −0.9 V assumes a slightly different aluminium work function. The figure 4.1 V is itself a MOS convention rather than a measured constant — the measured polycrystalline aluminium work function is about 4.28 eV, which would move φMS to −0.62 V, further from the graph rather than closer. Read the graph for the course's answer, and use the formula to know what the graph assumes.

2 · The oxide capacitance, Cox

The gate and the channel are the two plates of a capacitor and the gate oxide is its dielectric. Two of the four terms of equation (3.1) are a charge divided by this capacitance, so it appears twice and it is the quantity the whole of Chapters 3 and 4 exists to control.

Cox = εrε0tox
(3.4)
Cox oxide capacitance per unit area (F/cm²) · εr relative permittivity of the dielectric, 3.9 for SiO₂ (dimensionless) · ε0 permittivity of free space, 8.85×10⁻¹⁴ F/cm · tox oxide thickness (cm). The area has already been divided out. The parallel-plate capacitance of a real gate is Cox times its area; every equation in this course uses the per-unit-area form, so a gate of 2 cm² has twice the capacitance and the same Cox.

Ångströms to centimetres

Oxide thicknesses are quoted in ångströms and every other quantity in this course is in centimetres. One ångström is 10⁻⁸ cm, so 1000 Å is 10⁻⁵ cm and 20 Å is 2×10⁻⁷ cm. Substituting the number of ångströms directly into equation (3.4) produces an answer wrong by eight orders of magnitude, and the error is invisible because the result still has the units of a capacitance.

Equation (3.4) states the two ways to raise Cox, and both are chapters of this course. The first is to reduce tox, which M05 and M06 pursue until tunnelling ends it. The second is to raise εr by replacing SiO₂ with a different material — tantalum pentoxide, Ta₂O₅, was an early candidate with a relative permittivity of 20 to 25, some six times that of SiO₂. That is the subject of M07.

3 · The interface charge term, Qi/Cox

Charge sitting at the oxide–silicon boundary induces an image charge in the silicon whether or not any gate voltage is applied. The gate must supply a voltage that cancels it before it can begin inverting the surface, and for charge located at that boundary the voltage required is exactly the charge per unit area divided by the capacitance per unit area.

Position matters, and the qualification is not a technicality. A sheet of charge sitting at distance x from the gate inside an oxide of thickness tox shifts the threshold by only the fraction x/tox of what the same sheet would do at the silicon interface, and charge resting against the gate itself shifts it not at all. What enters equation (3.1) is therefore the charge weighted by its distance from the gate — its centroid — and the unweighted form is exact only for charge at the interface. For the interface charge this section is about, the two are the same thing. M05 resolves Qi into four components that sit at four different depths, and their positions are exactly what distinguishes them experimentally.

The charge is positive in practice — it consists chiefly of trapped positive charge and of positive ions — so the term enters equation (3.1) with a minus sign and drives the threshold voltage negative for both device types. In a p-channel device this adds to an already negative threshold; in an n-channel device it fights against the intended positive threshold. Reducing Qi is therefore a first-order manufacturing objective, and M05 resolves it into four physically distinct components and explains where each originates.

Qi = q Ni
(3.5)
Qi interface charge per unit area (C/cm²) · q electronic charge, 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ C · Ni areal density of interface charges (cm⁻²), typically 5×10¹⁰ to 10¹³ cm⁻² depending on process quality. Sources and lectures quote the density, in cm⁻², while equation (3.1) requires the charge, in C/cm². The conversion is one multiplication and it is the single most common route to a wrong answer here.

4 · The depletion charge term, Qd/Cox

Before an inversion layer can form, the gate must first push the majority carriers away from the surface and expose the fixed ionised dopant atoms left behind. Those exposed dopant ions constitute the depletion charge, and supporting it consumes gate voltage in exactly the same way the interface charge does.

The depletion region stops widening once the surface reaches strong inversion, because from that point any further gate voltage is balanced by inversion carriers rather than by additional exposed dopant. The width at that point is the maximum depletion width Wm.

Wm = √4εsφFqNA
(3.6)
Wm maximum depletion width (cm) · εs permittivity of silicon, 11.8 × 8.85×10⁻¹⁴ = 1.044×10⁻¹² F/cm · φF Fermi potential magnitude (V) · q electronic charge (C) · NA body doping (cm⁻³); use ND for an n-type body. The factor 4 arises because the surface potential at strong inversion is 2φF, not φF. Use the magnitude of φF here; the sign is carried by Qd and by the 2φF term separately.
Qd = −qNAWm   (p-type body)  ·   Qd = +qNDWm   (n-type body)
(3.7)
Qd depletion charge per unit area (C/cm²), signed · NA, ND acceptor and donor concentration (cm⁻³) · Wm maximum depletion width (cm). The sign is the physics: a p-type body depletes to expose ionised acceptors, which carry negative charge; an n-type body exposes ionised donors, which carry positive charge. Since equation (3.1) subtracts Qd/Cox, this term is positive for an n-channel device and negative for a p-channel device.

Both factors in equation (3.7) depend on the doping, but in opposite directions: raising NA increases the charge density and narrows the depletion region. The net effect is that |Qd| rises as the square root of the doping. This is the only lever the body doping gives, and it is a weak one — which is why the professor emphasises that VT is dominated by the first two terms.

5 · The Fermi potential, φF

The Fermi potential is the separation between the Fermi level and the intrinsic level Ei — the position the Fermi level would occupy in undoped silicon, near the middle of the band gap — expressed as a potential. It measures how far doping has moved the Fermi level from midgap.

Strong inversion is defined as the condition where the surface potential equals 2φF: the bands at the surface have bent by twice the Fermi potential, which places the surface as far on the inverted side of midgap as the bulk is on the doped side. That is where the minority carrier concentration at the surface equals the majority carrier concentration in the bulk, and it is the origin of the factor of two in the fourth term.

φF = kTq ln (NAni)   (p-type)  ·   φF = − kTq ln (NDni)   (n-type)
(3.8)
φF Fermi potential (V), signed · kT/q thermal voltage, 0.0259 V at T = 300 K · NA, ND body doping (cm⁻³) · ni intrinsic carrier concentration of silicon, 1.5×10¹⁰ cm⁻³ in this course. The logarithm is natural, not base ten. The minus sign for n-type must be written explicitly: substituting ND into the logarithm returns a positive number for either doping type, and only the sign convention distinguishes them.

The value of ni used here is the classical one

This course uses ni = 1.5×10¹⁰ cm⁻³ for silicon at 300 K, the textbook figure. The currently accepted measured value is 9.65×10⁹ cm⁻³. The difference shifts φF by about 11 mV and 2φF by about 23 mV. Use 1.5×10¹⁰ cm⁻³ throughout for consistency with the course, and know that the smaller value is the physically correct one.

6 · Signs, by device type

Two of the four terms change sign between an n-channel and a p-channel device, and keeping track of them is where most of the difficulty in using equation (3.1) lies. The rules are these. The interface charge Qi is positive regardless of device type, so −Qi/Cox is always negative. The work function difference φMS is negative for both aluminium and n⁺ polysilicon gates on either body type. The remaining two terms follow the body doping.

Termn-channel (p-type body)p-channel (n-type body)Why
φMS negativenegative Aluminium and n⁺ poly both have a lower work function than the silicon body
−Qi/Cox negativenegative Interface charge is positive in both cases
−Qd/Cox positivenegative Ionised acceptors are negative; ionised donors are positive
+2φF positivenegative φF is positive for p-type, negative for n-type

All four terms of a p-channel device are therefore negative and add, while an n-channel device has two of each sign that partially cancel. That asymmetry is the reason p-channel thresholds were historically much larger in magnitude than n-channel thresholds, and it is why the earliest MOS technologies needed supply rails far above the 1.5 V of a single cell: metal-gate p-channel logic of the late 1960s and early 1970s ran from rails in the region of −12 V to −24 V, set by a threshold of several volts that the circuit had to exceed with margin. Reducing the magnitude of that threshold is a substantial part of what the process developments in this course achieved, and M05 identifies the oxide charge that caused most of it.

7 · A complete calculation

The following is the calculation worked in Lecture 2, with every intermediate quantity carried in centimetre units.

n-channel MOSFET, aluminium gate

Given p-type body with NA = 10¹⁵ cm⁻³; interface charge density Ni = 5×10¹¹ cm⁻²; oxide thickness tox = 1000 Å; aluminium gate. Find VT.

Fermi potential. φF = 0.0259 × ln(10¹⁵ / 1.5×10¹⁰) = 0.0259 × ln(6.667×10⁴) = 0.0259 × 11.107 = 0.288 V. Positive, because the body is p-type. Hence 2φF = 0.575 V.

Work function difference. Read from the graph for an aluminium gate on p-type silicon at 10¹⁵ cm⁻³: φMS = −0.9 V.

Oxide capacitance. Convert first: 1000 Å = 1000 × 10⁻⁸ cm = 10⁻⁵ cm. Cox = (3.9 × 8.85×10⁻¹⁴) / 10⁻⁵ = 3.45×10⁻⁸ F/cm².

Interface charge term. Convert the density to a charge: Qi = 5×10¹¹ × 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ = 8×10⁻⁸ C/cm². Then Qi/Cox = 8×10⁻⁸ / 3.45×10⁻⁸ = 2.32 V, entering the equation as −2.32 V.

Maximum depletion width. Wm = √[ 4 × (11.8 × 8.85×10⁻¹⁴) × 0.288 / (1.6×10⁻¹⁹ × 10¹⁵) ] = √(1.202×10⁻¹² / 1.6×10⁻⁴) = √(7.51×10⁻⁹) = 0.867×10⁻⁴ cm, that is 0.867 µm.

Depletion charge term. |Qd| = 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ × 10¹⁵ × 0.867×10⁻⁴ = 1.39×10⁻⁸ C/cm². Then |Qd|/Cox = 0.402 V. The body is p-type so Qd is negative, and the term enters as +0.402 V.

Sum. VT = −0.9 − 2.32 + 0.402 + 0.575. (The lecture rounds the last two to 0.403 and 0.576; the total is −2.24 V either way.)

VT = −2.24 V

The result is negative, which means this n-channel device conducts at zero gate voltage and must be driven negative to turn off. It is a depletion-mode device, and it is not what a logic circuit needs.

The remedy is almost entirely the interface charge term. At −2.32 V it is larger than the other three combined, it is the one term that is a defect density rather than a material property, and it shrinks both as processing cleans up and as Cox rises — M05 is the account of how it was reduced by orders of magnitude. The threshold-adjust implant of section 9 supplies the remainder. Changing the gate material does not help here: n⁺ polysilicon on this body gives φMS = −(Eg/2q + φF) = −0.85 V, slightly more negative than aluminium's −0.80 V, so it moves the n-channel threshold the wrong way. Its value is for the p-channel device, where §8 takes it up.

7.1 The same device as a p-channel

Repeating the calculation for a p-channel device on an n-type body with ND = 10¹⁵ cm⁻³ and the same oxide, interface charge and gate material flips the last two terms. φF becomes −0.288 V, so 2φF = −0.575 V. Qd is now ionised donors and is positive, so −Qd/Cox = −0.402 V. Only the interface term is unchanged at −2.32 V. All four terms are negative and add to a large negative threshold.

The quoted answer and what the terms give

The answer stated in Lecture 3 is −4.919 V. Reproducing that total from the three terms above requires φMS = −1.62 V.

Evaluating φMS from the course's own constants gives a different figure. For n-type silicon, φS = χ + (kT/q)ln(Nc/ND) with Nc = 2.8×10¹⁹ cm⁻³, which is 4.05 + 0.265 = 4.315 V, so φMS = 4.1 − 4.315 = −0.215 V and the total comes to −3.51 V.

A φMS of −1.62 V is not reachable by any choice of gate. The silicon work function at ND = 10¹⁵ cm⁻³ is 4.315 V, so −1.62 V would require a gate whose work function is 2.70 eV — lower than caesium, the lowest of the elements at about 2.1 eV, and far below anything used in a process. The real options are aluminium at −0.22 V, n⁺ polysilicon at −0.27 V and p⁺ polysilicon at +0.85 V. The gap therefore cannot be closed by reading the graph differently; some other input to the lecture calculation must have differed.

Use the professor's −4.919 V as the course's answer. The reason for stating the discrepancy is that the sign analysis, not the arithmetic, is what the calculation is for: every term negative, a threshold several times larger in magnitude than the n-channel device on the same wafer, and a supply requirement set by the p-channel device. That conclusion holds at either number.

8 · Why the gate material changed

Section 1 established that φMS is a property of the pair of materials, and section 7 showed it contributing about a volt to a threshold that needs to be a few tenths of a volt. An aluminium gate offers no way to adjust it, because aluminium has one work function.

Polysilicon is silicon, so its work function moves with doping within the 4.05 to 5.17 eV range established in section 1.2, and doping the gate therefore becomes a way of setting φMS without touching the body doping or the oxide. In practice the range is not continuously available: the gate has to be doped degenerately, above about 10²⁰ cm⁻³, or it depletes under bias and adds a capacitance in series with the oxide. That pins the Fermi level at one band edge or the other, so the process offers two work functions rather than a continuum.

Work-function choice is not why polysilicon displaced aluminium. That was the self-aligned gate: polysilicon survives the source–drain implant and its activation anneal, so the gate can be patterned first and then mask the implant, which removes the alignment tolerance and the overlap capacitance that a separately aligned metal gate forces on the designer. The work-function argument became important later, when dual-doped gates were needed for surface-channel p-channel devices.

n⁺ polysilicon, doped heavily with phosphorus, has a work function near the conduction band edge and gives the n-channel device a favourable φMS. The same gate on a p-channel device does not, which is the argument that produces dual-doped polysilicon: p⁺ polysilicon over the p-channel device gives a work function near the valence band edge and a surface-channel device, at the cost of boron penetration through the gate oxide. M02 §8 states the trade, and M06 treats boron penetration as an oxide problem.

9 · Threshold adjustment by implantation

Of the four terms, three are fixed once the wafer, the oxide and the gate material are chosen. The depletion charge term is the exception: it depends on the doping in the first fraction of a micrometre of silicon, and that doping can be changed after the rest of the structure is determined, by ion implantation — firing dopant ions at the wafer with enough energy to embed them at a controlled depth.

The implant is performed through a thin oxide rather than into bare silicon, and that oxide is called a screen. Its main purpose is to stop channelling. Silicon is a crystal, and along certain directions the lattice presents open corridors between rows of atoms down which an ion can travel much further than it would through randomly arranged material. An ion arriving through an amorphous oxide is deflected before it reaches the silicon, so it enters at an effectively random angle and the deep, poorly controlled channelled tail is suppressed. The screen also keeps the bare silicon surface from being sputtered and contaminated by the beam.

The oxide is not free. An ion spends part of its range crossing it, so the implanted peak ends up shallower below the silicon surface by roughly the screen thickness, and the implant energy is raised to compensate. Silicon and silicon dioxide stop ions at comparable rates, which is what makes that compensation a simple one. Implant energies for a threshold adjustment are in the range of 25 keV to 100 keV, and greater energy means greater depth, because the ion travels further before collisions with lattice atoms bring it to rest.

The species is boron, for both n-channel and p-channel devices, and the reason is the sign table of section 6. In an n-channel device the body is p-type and −Qd/Cox is already positive; adding boron raises NA, increases |Qd| and makes that positive term larger, moving VT upward, which is what an n-channel device needs. In a p-channel device the body is n-type and the same term is negative; boron is an acceptor, so it partially compensates the donors, reduces the net donor concentration, reduces |Qd|, and makes that negative term smaller in magnitude. Both changes move the threshold in the wanted direction.

Implanting phosphorus into the p-channel device would do the opposite: as a donor it would raise the net donor concentration and drive the already too-negative threshold further negative. One implant step with one species therefore serves both device types, which is why boron appears at this stage of every CMOS flow.

Net doping after a compensating implant

When boron of concentration NA is implanted into silicon already doped n-type at ND, the net doping is the difference ND − NA, and the subtraction is of the concentrations, not of the exponents. Implanting 10¹⁵ cm⁻³ of boron into 10¹⁶ cm⁻³ n-type silicon leaves

10¹⁶ − 10¹⁵ = 10×10¹⁵ − 1×10¹⁵ = 9×10¹⁵ cm⁻³,

not 10¹. Bring both numbers to the same power of ten before subtracting. The professor names this as an error he has seen repeatedly, and it destroys every subsequent quantity: φF, Wm, Qd and VT all depend on the result.

Implantation is a violent process at the atomic scale. An incoming ion displaces silicon atoms from their lattice sites as it slows, and at the doses used the near-surface layer is left amorphous — the crystalline order destroyed. An anneal after the implant restores the crystal and moves the implanted dopant onto substitutional lattice sites, where it is electrically active; an atom sitting between lattice sites contributes no carrier. M09 treats the damage, the anneal, and the placement of the implant peak in full.

An n-channel device has VT = −2.24 V. Halving the oxide thickness changes two terms. Which two, and does the threshold move up or down?

Halving tox doubles Cox to 6.9×10⁻⁸ F/cm². Both charge terms are divided by it, so both halve in magnitude: −Qi/Cox goes from −2.32 V to −1.16 V, and −Qd/Cox goes from +0.402 V to +0.201 V. φMS and 2φF do not contain Cox and are unchanged.

The new total is −0.9 − 1.16 + 0.201 + 0.575 = −1.28 V. The threshold moves upward, towards zero, by 0.96 V. The interface term shrank by 1.16 V while the depletion term shrank by only 0.20 V, so the large negative term loses much more than the small positive one — which is why thinning the oxide is an effective threshold remedy as well as a drive-current remedy.

Summary

  • VT = φMS − Qi/Cox − Qd/Cox + 2φF, and the first two terms dominate.
  • The work function is measured from the Fermi level to the vacuum level. Silicon has no single work function because its Fermi level moves with doping, so the electron affinity, 4.05 eV, is the fixed reference. The silicon work function ranges from 4.05 eV to 4.05 + 1.12 = 5.17 eV.
  • E [eV] = 1.24/λ [µm]: silicon absorbs below 1.107 µm, GaN below 0.365 µm.
  • Cox = εrε0/tox, per unit area, in centimetre units, with ε0 = 8.85×10⁻¹⁴ F/cm. Convert ångströms to centimetres first: 1 Å = 10⁻⁸ cm.
  • Qi = qNi. Sources quote the density in cm⁻²; the equation needs the charge in C/cm².
  • Wm = √(4εsφF/qNA), then Qd = ∓qNWm: negative for a p-type body (ionised acceptors), positive for an n-type body (ionised donors).
  • φF = ±(kT/q)ln(N/ni), positive for p-type and negative for n-type, with the minus sign written explicitly. kT/q = 0.0259 V and ni = 1.5×10¹⁰ cm⁻³ in this course.
  • For an n-channel device the four terms are −, −, +, +; for a p-channel device they are −, −, −, −. That asymmetry is why early MOS circuits needed six-volt supplies.
  • The reference calculation: NA = 10¹⁵ cm⁻³, Ni = 5×10¹¹ cm⁻², tox = 1000 Å, aluminium gate → φF = 0.288 V, Cox = 3.45×10⁻⁸ F/cm², Wm = 0.867 µm, Qd = 1.39×10⁻⁸ C/cm², VT = −2.24 V.
  • Polysilicon replaced aluminium because its work function is a process variable. The threshold-adjust implant uses boron for both device types, and net doping after compensation is a subtraction of concentrations, not exponents.
