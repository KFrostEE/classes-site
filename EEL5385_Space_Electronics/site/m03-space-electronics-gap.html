Module 03

Space Electronics: Requirements, Heritage, and the Technology Gap

What the space environment demands of a part, why qualified parts run generations behind commercial ones, and how the industry works within that.

Prerequisites M01, M02 Read time ~35 min Sources Decks 1–2; Lectures 1–2; syllabus week 2

M02 described a technology that doubles its transistor count every two years and is manufactured at 13.5 nm wavelength in machines costing a hundred million dollars each. The processor flying on the James Webb Space Telescope was designed in 2001, holds about ten million transistors, and runs at roughly a hundred megahertz. Both statements are true at the same time, and the distance between them is the subject of this module.

Three things create that distance, and they are separable. The environment imposes requirements no commercial part is designed to meet — vacuum, a thermal regime with no convection, radiation, launch loads, and no possibility of repair. The economics of manufacturing make it unattractive to build parts that meet them. And the risk structure of the industry rewards reusing a part that has already flown over qualifying one that has not. This module takes each in turn, and closes on the RAD750 as a case in which all three are visible at once. The radiation requirement is stated here and deferred: it is the subject of M04 onward, which is the bulk of the course.

1 · What changes when a part leaves the ground

A terrestrial integrated circuit operates inside a set of conditions so reliably present that they are rarely stated: an atmosphere that conducts heat away, a magnetic field and 10 metres of water equivalent of air overhead that remove almost all energetic particles, a temperature range of tens of degrees, mechanical quiet, and an owner who can replace it. Every one of those is absent in space.

ConditionWhat it does to electronicsWhere it is treated
Vacuum Removes convection and gas-phase conduction, leaving solid conduction to carry heat to an external surface and radiation to carry it away from there. Causes outgassing of polymers, which then condense on cold optical surfaces. Permits cold welding of bare metal contacts. §2, M18
Thermal extremes and cycling Carrier populations, leakage and mobility all change with temperature (equations 1.1, 1.3). Repeated cycling fatigues solder joints and bond wires through thermal-expansion mismatch. §2, M17, M18
Ionizing radiation Deposits charge, shifts thresholds, raises leakage, and can destroy a part with a single particle. M04–M16
Launch loads Quasi-static acceleration, random vibration across a broad spectrum, acoustic pressure inside the fairing, and pyrotechnic shock at stage separation. All mechanical, all capable of cracking a die or a joint. §4, M19
Limited power Every watt must be generated by a solar array or a radioisotope source and then radiated away as heat. Power is a hard budget, not a cost. §3, M20
Micrometeoroids and debris Hypervelocity impacts pit exposed surfaces and can sever a conductor or perforate a radiator. M19, M21
No repair Removes every mitigation that depends on intervention. Reliability must be designed in and then demonstrated on the ground. §5–§7, M19

These are not independent. A part that runs hot leaks more, and leakage generates heat, so a thermal margin and an electrical margin are the same margin. A thicker shield against radiation adds mass, which costs launch energy, which reduces the power budget available for the payload. Space electronics is an exercise in coupled constraints, and the three that dominate are stated in the industry as mass, power and volume.

2 · Heat with no air

The most common misreading of the thermal environment is to assume that space, being cold, cools a spacecraft. It does not, because cooling requires a mechanism. On the ground a chip loses heat by conduction into its package and board and by convection into moving air. In vacuum there is no fluid to convect and nothing to conduct into beyond the spacecraft itself, so the only significant path from the vehicle to its surroundings is thermal radiation from an external surface. (Venting and sublimation reject a little energy as mass, and on a planetary surface the argument inverts entirely — see the Venus case below, where the atmosphere conducts heat in.)

A body in sunlight absorbs solar flux over its projected area and radiates over its whole surface. Equating the two gives its equilibrium temperature.

For a sphere, Aproj/Arad = 1/4, so with αs = ε the result is 278 K, or 5 °C — above freezing, in what is called cold space. With no internal dissipation only the ratio αs/ε matters, and it is a surface property rather than a bulk one, which is why thermal control is done with coatings, paints and multi-layer insulation. Add internal power Pint to the numerator and that ceases to be true: ε then sets the absolute rejection capability, which is why a radiator is specified as high-ε and low-αs rather than by a ratio. On the formula sheet as (3.1).

Equation (3.1) also sets the range of thermal environments a mission may encounter. Because irradiance falls as the inverse square of distance, a spacecraft at Jupiter receives about 1/27 of the flux at Earth and its passive equilibrium temperature falls accordingly; one approaching the Sun receives correspondingly more. The extremes named in the course description follow directly:

  • Parker Solar Probe flies close enough that the Sun-facing surface of its carbon-foam-cored carbon–carbon shield reaches roughly 980 °C at closest approach, while the spacecraft body behind it is held near 30 °C. The shield was qualified to about 1,370 °C, and that larger number is the one usually quoted; it is a test limit, not a flight condition. Either way the electronics never see the high temperature, and the thermal design keeps the two sides of one structure roughly a thousand degrees apart.
  • The Venus surface sits at about 465 °C under 92 bar — some 91 Earth atmospheres — of an atmosphere that is 96.5 % carbon dioxide. The Venera landers of the 1970s and early 1980s returned data for between 23 and 127 minutes. Those are lower bounds on survival rather than times to failure: the landers had no direct-to-Earth link and relayed through an orbiter or a receding flyby bus, so for most of them transmission ended when the relay went below the horizon, not when the lander died. NASA's LLISSE concept targets 60 days using silicon carbide devices that operate at ambient temperature rather than being cooled — a change of approach rather than a better thermal design, and the subject of M17.
  • Infrared astronomy runs the opposite way. A detector observing thermal infrared must be colder than the signal it measures. The James Webb Space Telescope's sunshield alone cools the near-infrared instruments to about 37–40 K, entirely passively. Its mid-infrared instrument needs below 6.7 K — the −266 °C quoted in the syllabus is 7.00 K, and is a ceiling rather than a set point — and that last thirty kelvin cannot be reached by radiating alone, so MIRI carries a mechanical cryocooler. Passive radiative cooling has a floor near 40 K, and the cost of going below it is an entire active cryogenic subsystem. That is the sharper version of the lesson.

3 · Power as a hard budget

Every watt a spacecraft consumes must first be generated, and generation is limited by the area of the solar array and, through equation (3.1), by distance from the Sun. Every watt consumed must then be radiated away, which requires radiator area. Power is therefore constrained twice, and both constraints convert directly into mass.

This is why equation (1.8) reads differently for space. A commercial processor maximises performance subject to a package's ability to remove 200 W. A space processor maximises performance subject to a few watts, and by (1.8) the way to operate at a few watts is to run at low frequency and modest transistor count. A part that delivers a hundred megahertz at five watts is a rational point on that curve. Lecture 2 noted that the RAD750's power consumption is more than an order of magnitude below that of contemporary commercial processors of the same era, and that this was a design objective rather than a shortfall.

4 · What qualification means

A part destined for flight must be shown to survive the environment before it is launched, because there is no opportunity afterward. That demonstration is qualification, and it is performed against a requirement set that is specified per mission and per orbit.

Two points about this table matter for the rest of the course. First, the requirements differ by where the part is mounted, not only by mission. A solar cell is exposed directly to space and therefore faces a wider temperature range and a higher radiation dose than a processor inside a shielded avionics box; a battery dissipates internally and has its own, narrower, thermal requirement. Second, the radiation rows are the only ones that require knowledge of the environment in a quantitative form that this course must build from scratch — hence M04 through M07, which exist to produce the numbers those rows consume.

Part quality grades

Electronic components are supplied in tiers that differ in screening rather than in design: commercial parts are tested at one temperature and sampled; industrial and automotive grades extend the temperature range and tighten sampling; military grades add burn-in and lot traceability; and space grades add radiation characterisation, full lot testing, destructive physical analysis on samples, and hermetic packaging. Cost rises steeply at each step — a space-grade part typically costs two to three orders of magnitude more than its commercial equivalent, not the ten-thousandfold that a clean order of magnitude per step would imply — and the last step also restricts the supplier to whoever is willing to serve a market of thousands of units.

5 · Why the economics do not close

Given that a radiation-hardened part can be designed, the question is why one is rarely designed on a current process. The answer given in Lecture 2, by an industry participant with decades in the field, is arithmetic rather than technical.

A leading-edge fabrication plant is built to produce of order a hundred thousand wafers a month — the industry calls a site above that threshold a gigafab — and its economics depend on that volume: the capital cost is fixed and enormous, so profit per wafer is low and profit overall comes from quantity. At a few hundred good die per wafer that is 108 to 109 die a year. The entire annual demand for a radiation-hardened processor is of order ten thousand units, which really is a fraction of one day's output. Carry the page's own figures through: a volume gap of 105, against a price premium of 103 — $200,000 against a few hundred dollars — leaves the radiation-hardened customer worth about a hundredth of the revenue of the commercial one, in exchange for a custom process flow, custom test, and a long qualification programme. The proposal loses money at every stage, and it is declined.

What exists instead are foundries operating on mature processes that are fully depreciated, where a small run is economically viable and where the process itself is often better suited to the application. Lecture 2 described a visit to such a manufacturer, producing power and space electronics on a mature node patterned with 193 nm lithography, with no plan to move to a finer one — because its products need voltage handling, thermal performance and radiation tolerance rather than density, and a thicker oxide on a coarser process delivers the first two directly.

6 · Technology readiness and flight heritage

Economics explains why advanced radiation-hardened parts are not manufactured. A second mechanism explains why even the ones that do exist are adopted slowly.

Technology readiness level is a nine-point scale, defined in the NASA Systems Engineering Handbook, that states how far a technology has progressed from basic principles to demonstrated operation. Level 1 is an observed physical principle; level 6 is a prototype demonstrated in a relevant environment; level 8 is a completed system qualified through test and demonstration, on the ground or in space; level 9 is a system proven through successful mission operations. Programmes specify a minimum TRL for the components they will accept, and a part that has never flown cannot reach the top of the scale by any amount of ground testing.

Flight heritage is the record of how many times a part has flown and how successfully. A part with long heritage carries a known failure history, established test procedures, existing board designs, and engineers who have used it. Selecting it removes a large block of programme risk and schedule. Selecting an unflown part adds that risk back, and adds the cost of qualifying it, in exchange for performance that the mission may not require. For missions where failure is unrecoverable and expensive, the choice is usually made for heritage — a position that Lecture 2's industry participants described as near-universal in civil space.

The consequence is a lock-in. Heritage accumulates on parts that are already flying, which makes them more attractive still, which delays the point at which a successor accumulates any. Manufacturers of long-flown devices report trying to retire them and being unable to, because programmes keep selecting them.

7 · The RAD750 as a case

One part shows all three mechanisms at once, and Lecture 2 used it as the worked example.

The RAD750 is a radiation-hardened microprocessor derived from the commercial PowerPC 750 architecture. BAE Systems designed it, at Manassas, Virginia, under licence to the IBM/Motorola PowerPC 750 core, and manufactures it there; it was introduced in 2001 and first flew in 2005 on Deep Impact. (The course, following a widely repeated error, credits the design to IBM. IBM designed the commercial 750 core, and the Manassas site was IBM Federal Systems before passing to Loral, Lockheed Martin and then BAE — which is where the confusion comes from.) It contains about 10.4 million transistors on a die of roughly 130 mm², manufactured on 250 nm and later 150 nm processes. It runs between roughly 110 and 200 MHz and consumes about 5 W. It operates from −55 °C to +125 °C and tolerates a total ionizing dose in the range of hundreds of kilorads to about a megarad, depending on variant. Those are the processor's numbers; the name also covers the single-board computer built around it, which draws about 10 W, is rated −55 to +70 °C, and is qualified to about 100 krad(Si). The course quotes a unit price of $200,000 in 2002 — in fact the listed price of the predecessor RAD6000, which BAE has said the RAD750 is comparable to; either way the flight unit costs of order 105 dollars, and about twice that in present-day terms. It flies on the James Webb Space Telescope, the Curiosity rover, the Juno orbiter and many others.

Read as a commercial processor the RAD750 is obsolete. Read against its requirement set it is not: it trades frequency and transistor count, which the mission does not need, for power, temperature range and dose tolerance, which the mission cannot do without. The trade is visible in equation (1.8): dropping the clock from 3.3 GHz to 130 MHz reduces dynamic power by the same factor.

A mature node is not chosen for its gate oxide

It is tempting to credit the 250 nm process with helping the radiation response because its gate oxide is thick. The opposite is true for total ionizing dose. The threshold shift from charge trapped in an oxide scales roughly as the square of the oxide thickness, so a thicker gate oxide traps more charge per unit dose and shifts further — which is why the hundreds-of-nanometre isolation oxides, not the gate oxides, dominate total-dose failure in any given technology. Below roughly 10 nm a gate oxide becomes nearly dose-immune, because trapped holes tunnel back out faster than irradiation supplies them. Modern thin gate oxides are radiation-hard by accident of scaling.

What a mature node genuinely buys on the radiation side is single-event margin: a larger node holds more critical charge, so it takes a bigger deposit to upset it, and its generous feature sizes leave room for the guard rings and annular transistors that hardening by layout requires. It is also cheap on a depreciated line, tolerates higher supply voltage, and carries decades of heritage. The thick oxide is what delivers the voltage handling mentioned in §5 — not the dose tolerance. M14 and M15 develop both halves.

That it is still specified for new missions twenty-five years after design is the heritage argument of §6 operating exactly as described.

8 · The alternatives

The gap is real but it is not accepted passively, and three responses are in use. They appear again in M16, where the radiation physics needed to evaluate them is available.

The course names five companies as working examples. Two of the descriptions need correcting against what the companies actually do: Magics Technologies is a radiation-hardened IC and ASIC design house; Apogee Semiconductor applies its radiation-hardened-by-design layout kit inside commercial foundries, so it belongs with Magics rather than in a category of its own; Coherent Logix makes the HyperX many-core processor, which is a software-programmable SoC offered as an alternative to radiation-hardened FPGAs rather than an FPGA itself; and VPT and Renesas Electronics both supply radiation-hardened power conversion and control — VPT is a DC–DC converter house, not a commercial-parts test house. That leaves the upscreening approach below without a named example; it is the business of test-and-screening specialists and radiation test laboratories.

  • Radiation-hardened by design. Build on a standard commercial process, and obtain tolerance from circuit and layout choices — redundant storage cells, error detection and correction, triple redundancy on critical logic, guard structures against latch-up. This is what makes a part like the RAD750 possible without a custom process, and it costs area, power and speed rather than fabrication access.
  • Upscreened commercial parts. Buy commercial devices, test them against the mission's radiation and temperature requirements, and fly the ones that pass from a single traceable production lot. This is now common for short-duration low-Earth-orbit missions and for constellations where a failed satellite is replaceable. It transfers effort from design to test, and its weakness is that the manufacturer may change the process without notice, invalidating the characterisation.
  • System-level tolerance. Accept that individual parts will be disturbed, and design the system to detect and recover — watchdog timers, periodic memory scrubbing, redundant processors voting on results, safe modes. This is how a constellation of commercial-grade satellites achieves availability without radiation-hardened parts in every socket.
A mission proposes replacing a heritage processor with a commercial part that is a hundred times faster, a tenth the power per operation, and a thousandth the cost. Beyond radiation tolerance, what must be established before this is a sound trade?

Several things, and none of them is about performance. The part must hold its specifications across the full mounted temperature range, not the commercial 0–70 °C or the industrial −40 to +85 °C, and must survive thermal cycling in vacuum at that range. Its package must be qualified for launch vibration, acoustic loading and shock, and its non-metallic materials must meet outgassing limits. Its supply must be traceable to a single production lot, because a process change at the foundry invalidates any characterisation performed on earlier material — and commercial manufacturers change processes without notification. Its absolute power may matter more than its power per operation, since the spacecraft budget is in watts rather than in watts per instruction. And the programme must accept a part at a lower technology readiness level, which means budgeting for the qualification campaign and for the schedule risk if it fails. The performance advantage is the easy part of the argument; the cost of establishing everything else is what the hundred-fold price premium on a heritage part is actually buying.

Summary

  • Space removes convection, adds radiation and launch loads, constrains power hard, and removes repair. These constraints are coupled: shielding costs mass, mass costs power, power costs heat rejection.
  • In vacuum the only heat path out of a spacecraft is radiation from an external surface. A sphere at 1 AU with αs = ε sits at 278 K; thermal control is done by choosing αs/ε with coatings.
  • Solar irradiance follows an inverse-square law, which sets the thermal environment of every mission — Parker Solar Probe's shield reaches ~980 °C at closest approach (qualified to ~1,370 °C), while passive cooling bottoms out near 40 K for an infrared telescope.
  • Qualification specifies temperature range, thermal vacuum, random vibration, acoustic, shock, total dose, single-event tolerance and outgassing — differently for each mounting location.
  • Radiation-hardened parts are not built on leading-edge processes because the volume is five orders of magnitude below what a gigafab requires, and even a thousand-fold price premium recovers only three of them.
  • Technology readiness level and flight heritage make adoption slow independently of economics: TRL 9 requires flight, and heritage accumulates only on parts already flying.
  • The RAD750 — 10.4 million transistors, 250/150 nm, ~5 W, −55 to +125 °C, hundreds of krad — is not a slow processor but a processor optimised against a different objective, and it is still flying because of heritage. Its mature node buys single-event margin and layout room, not total-dose tolerance: threshold shift grows as the square of oxide thickness.
  • The three responses to the gap are hardening by design, upscreening commercial parts, and system-level fault tolerance.
