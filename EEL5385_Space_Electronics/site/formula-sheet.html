1 · What to do, by what is asked

Asked for…StrategyWhere
Carrier concentration in doped silicon Majority carrier ≈ the dopant concentration. Get the minority carrier from np = ni2. Check the dopants are fully ionized (true at 300 K, not at cryogenic temperature). (1.3)
Fermi-level position, or a carrier density, from the doping Measure from the intrinsic level: n = ni exp[(EF − Ei)/kT], and for p-type φF = (kT/q) ln(Na/ni) — 0.298 to 0.476 V for 1015 to 1018 cm−3, 59.5 meV per decade. Check the material is non-degenerate and fully ionized. Hot enough that ni approaches the doping: charge neutrality, with ni(T) from (1.2). (1.4), (1.3), (1.5)
The field, or which way a carrier drifts, from a band diagram The field is the slope of the bands divided by q and points up the slope; 1 eV across 1 µm is 104 V/cm. A positive voltage lowers the bands. Electrons roll down Ec; holes rise along Ev. (1.7), (1.12)
Flat-band or threshold voltage of a MOS structure Get φF from the body doping, then φms ≈ −(Eg/2q + φF) for an n⁺-polysilicon gate on p-type silicon, then VFB = φms − Qox/Cox, then add 2φF and the depletion-charge term over Cox. Cox in F/cm² from tox in cm. Check: 1017 cm−3 under 10 nm gives ≈ 0.34 V. (1.10), (1.11), (1.4)
Whether a MOS surface is in accumulation, depletion or inversion Compare the gate voltage with VFB and Vth. On a p-type body: below VFB, accumulation (holes at the surface); between them, depletion, weakly inverted once the bending exceeds φF; above Vth, strong inversion, ψs = 2φF. With an n⁺-polysilicon gate VFB is about −1 V, so zero gate voltage is already depletion. (1.10), (1.11)
How a device changes with temperature Work out whether the quantity tracks ni or ni2. Leakage and reverse saturation current go as ni2, doubling roughly every 5 K; the generation current goes as ni and doubles every ~10 K, and it is the one that dominates measured silicon leakage. Mobility falls with temperature above ~100 K. Subthreshold swing is proportional to T outright. (1.2), (1.9), (1.15)
Depletion width, or the sensitive volume of a junction Get Vbi from the doping, add the reverse bias, then use the depletion-width expression. Multiply by junction area for the volume. Width goes as the square root of total voltage. (1.8)
Current from a charge deposited in a short time I = Q/t with Q = Nq. Then power P = IV, then power density P/A over the track cross-section. Keep the area in cm² and watch that 100 nm = 10−5 cm. (8.3)
Transistor drive current, or the effect of a geometry change Use the saturation expression. For a fin or nanosheet, first convert the geometry to an effective width, then substitute. (1.14), (2.2)
Whether a process can print a feature CD = k1λ/NA with k1 ≥ 0.25 for a single exposure. If the required CD needs k1 < 0.25, the answer is multiple patterning or a shorter wavelength. Check depth of focus separately. (2.3)
Equilibrium temperature of a surface in space Balance absorbed solar power against radiated power. Only the ratio αs/ε and the area ratio matter. Scale the irradiance to the orbital distance first. Add internal dissipation to the absorbed side. (3.1)
Activity of a source now, or after a stated time Get the mass of the isotope (scale the element mass by isotopic abundance), divide by its atomic mass, multiply by NA and by ln2/t1/2 with the half-life in seconds. For elapsed time, halve once per half-life rather than exponentiating. (4.4)
Dose delivered, or equivalent dose to a person Energy deposited over absorber mass, stated for a named material. Multiply by the quality factor only for biological risk — never for a device. (4.5)
Which shield material for which species Neutrons: maximise 4Mm/(M+m)² → hydrogen-rich. Gammas: maximise Z → lead. Betas: minimise Z to suppress bremsstrahlung, then a high-Z layer behind it. Ions: check the range — a shield that stops a particle inside the die is worse than none. (4.1), (9.1)
Whether a particle is trapped, and where it mirrors Gyroradius must be small against the scale over which B varies; use the momentum form for electrons. The mirror point depends only on equatorial pitch angle, not on mass or energy. (6.2)
Orbital speed, period, or time spent inside a belt Add RE to the altitude first. Circular: (7.1). Elliptical: use vis-viva at the belt-crossing radius — an eccentric orbit crosses fast and dwells slow, so exposure is the time integral, not the peak flux. (7.1), (7.2)
Carriers or charge liberated by a particle Divide deposited energy by the pair-creation energy — 99.42 eV for the course's own arithmetic, 3.6 eV for anything physical — then multiply by q. Only energy actually deposited counts. (8.2)
Peak current, power or power density from one strike Charge over collection time (~1 ps) for current, times bias for power, over the track cross-section for power density. The course's chain lands at 500 MW/cm². (8.3)
X-ray output of an electron beam or a beta shield f = 0.0007ZE with E in MeV gives the fraction radiated, spread over a continuum ending at the electron energy. It is not a photon line. (9.1)
Displacement count, or damage from a known fluence Maximum recoil energy from (4.1), then NRT for the displacement count. For an environment, integrate NIEL against the fluence spectrum to get displacement damage dose. (11.1), (11.2)
Power or delay of a logic block Dynamic power is αCV2f; static power is VIoff; delay is CV/I. Lowering frequency reduces the first and not the second. (1.17)
Shielding thickness for a photon beam, or how much of a beam a layer stops Look up μ/ρ for the material at that photon energy in the NIST table, multiply by ρ and by thickness in cm, then P = 1 − e−exponent. For a compound, mass-weight the elemental coefficients first. To attenuate by a factor n, solve μx = ln n. Add a buildup factor for anything more than about one mean free path. (10.7–8), (10.9)
Which photon interaction applies, or whether pair production matters Locate the photon energy and the target Z on the dominance map. In silicon: photoelectric below ~50 keV, Compton from there to ~15 MeV, pair production above. Exceeding the 1.022 MeV threshold does not make pair production significant. (10.6)
Dose from a photon field, rather than attenuation Use μen/ρ, not μ/ρ. The two differ by more than a factor of two in silicon at 1 MeV, because Compton scattering carries energy out of the volume. (10.7–8)
Whether a charged particle reaches a buried layer, or where it stops Get the range from a PSTAR-style table at that energy, divide by ρ for a depth, and use the projected range rather than the CSDA range. For an ion other than a proton, evaluate the proton table at E/M and scale by M/z². (12.6–7), (12.8)
Charge deposited by an ion in a sensitive volume Take LET in MeV·cm²/mg, multiply by 10.4 fC/µm for silicon, then by the path length through the volume. Compare against the node's critical charge. (12.5)
Energy an ion deposits in a layer, from TRIM's ionization output Add the ion and recoil ionization curves (eV/(Å·ion)) and integrate across the layer's depth range: sum the bins times the bin width, one hundredth of the plotting window, in Å. The result is eV per ion. The height of the curve is a density, never an energy. (13.7)
Electron–hole pairs, or charge, from a TRIM run Integrate first, then divide by the pair energy of that layer's material — 3.6 eV in Si, 17 eV in SiO₂ (99.42 eV only to reproduce the course's own arithmetic) — and multiply by q. These are pairs generated, not collected. For an LET read off the curve near the surface: in silicon 1 eV/Å = 0.0429 MeV·cm²/mg = 0.445 fC/µm. (13.7), (13.8)
Dose from a fluence of ions Energy per ion in the layer times fluence, over the layer's mass per area ρt (t in cm), gives eV/g; divide by 6.242 × 1013 for rad. The answer is in rad of that layer's material — rad(SiO₂) for an oxide, which is what (14.3) needs. For a thin sensitive region, integrate over that region alone. (13.9)
Implanted concentration at a depth, from the range distribution Concentration = fluence × range distribution. The plot is already in (atoms/cm³)/(atoms/cm²); RANGE.txt is in atoms/(Å·ion) and needs ×108 first. Low fluence only: TRIM's target never changes. (13.6)
How many ions a TRIM run needs For a mean depth, the error is σR/√N: 500 ions give 4.5 % of the straggling. For a depth profile, each bin's error is 1/√n of its own count: 500 ions over 100 bins leave about 45 % scatter. 1000 ions typically give better than 10 %. Rerun with another seed to see what is noise. (13.4)
Which TRIM damage option to choose, including for sputtering Default to Quick Calculation of Damage (Kinchin–Pease): identical ion ranges and ionization by the ion, and the recommended route to displacements per atom, through the damage energy and (11.1) with the lattice binding energy set to zero. Full cascades give about twice the vacancies. Sputtering needs an option that follows recoils — Surface Sputtering or Monolayer Collision Steps, or Full Damage Cascades — on a thin target. State the Ed used: TRIM's silicon default of 15 eV gives about 1.4 times the vacancies of 21 eV. (13.10), (13.11)
Setting up a compound or gas layer Enter atom fractions (convert a composition by weight through the atomic weights). Then type in the measured density: SiO₂ 2.20, GaAs 5.32, Ga₂O₃ 6.44 (the deck's α-phase value; β is 5.88), dry air 1.205 × 10−3 g/cm³. TRIM's own proposal is kept only for a single element such as Si (2.32). For a gas tick the Gas box, still check the density, and give the width in mm. Every depth scales as 1/ρ. (13.3), (13.5)
Whether an ion's energy goes to ionization or to recoils and heat Evaluate the reduced energy. Large ε (100 keV proton in Si, 79): at least 97 % to ionization, recoils negligible. Small ε (100 keV oxygen 5.4, 190 keV phosphorus 3.8, 1 keV argon 0.014): a large Recoils column and phonons of tens of percent. The mass factor of (13.2) says how much one collision can transfer. (13.12), (13.2)
Dose in rad(SiO₂) versus rad(Si) For photons, the ratio of mass energy-absorption coefficients: 1.003 for cobalt-60, so the two are interchangeable; 0.558 at 10 keV, so 1 rad(SiO₂) ≈ 1.8 rad(Si). Charged-particle equilibrium required. For an ion beam, compute each layer's dose directly. (14.1), (13.9)
Threshold-voltage shift from a dose Convert the dose to rad(SiO₂). Holes generated per area: g0Dtox with g0 = 8.1 × 1012 cm−3 rad(SiO₂)−1 and tox in cm. Times the yield fy(Eox, particle), times the trapped fraction fot, then ΔVot = −qΔNot/Cox for charge at the interface (centroid form otherwise). Add ΔVit with the sign of the channel type: + for n-channel, − for p-channel. (14.3)–(14.8)
Which oxide dominates a part's total-dose response Compare tox²: tenfold thicker means a hundredfold larger shift (measured exponents 1.5 to 2). Below about 10 nm tunnelling neutralizes trapped holes and a gate oxide is nearly immune, so in a modern commercial part the 100–1000 nm isolation oxide dominates, through edge leakage in n-channel transistors. (14.12)
Off-state current increase from a threshold shift One decade per S of shift: ratio = 10−ΔVth/S with S in V/decade. At 80 mV/decade, −0.1 V gives ×18, −0.3 V gives ×5.6 × 103. Equivalently, each 59.5 meV of barrier lowering multiplies the current by ten at 300 K; DIBL multiplies it by 10DIBL·ΔVDS/S. (14.11), (1.13), (1.16)
Mobility loss, drive-current change or a stretched subthreshold slope Mobility falls as 1/(1 + αΔNit); drive goes as µ(VGS − Vth)²; interface traps add Cit = qDit to the swing bracket. Oxide charge only translates the transfer curve; interface traps also stretch it. (14.9), (14.10), (1.14)
Whether an irradiated n-channel transistor recovers or rebounds Follow the two terms separately in time. Oxide-trapped charge anneals linearly in log t; interface traps do not anneal at room temperature and keep forming for thousands of seconds, so the net shift moves positive and can overshoot. Compare exposures at equal time since irradiation began. A p-channel transistor cannot rebound. (14.8), (14.13)
Output of an on-chip charge pump after its transistors shift Each millivolt of pass-transistor threshold shift costs N + 1 millivolts of output; the function needing the highest voltage (erase) fails first. (14.2)

2 · Facts that hold across problems

  • This subject works in centimetres. Concentrations in cm−3, mobilities in cm²/V·s, fields in V/cm, permittivities in F/cm, current densities in A/cm². Mixing in metres is the most common source of errors of 102, 104 or 106.
  • Charge is conserved and quantised. Any count of carriers converts to a charge by multiplying by 1.602 × 10−19 C, and any charge delivered over a time is a current. Most single-event estimates are exactly this chain.
  • Check the regime before the algebra. Is the semiconductor in equilibrium (then np = ni2) or not? Is the transistor above or below threshold? Is the field low enough that v = µℰ still holds? Each of these has a different governing expression.
  • Exponentials dominate. Where a quantity contains exp(−E/kT), an order-of-magnitude estimate of the exponent is worth more than a precise prefactor. A factor of two in Nc is irrelevant next to a 0.1 eV error in E.
  • Order-of-magnitude first. The lectures' worked estimates deliberately round to one significant figure and then check the exponent. A power density of 108 W/cm² is a physically meaningful result; 4.87 × 108 is false precision on inputs known to a factor of two.
  • Sanity checks. Carrier concentrations cannot exceed the atomic density, 5 × 1022 cm−3. Depletion widths in ordinary doping are 0.01–10 µm. Drift velocity cannot exceed ~107 cm/s. An equilibrium temperature at 1 AU is near 278 K unless the surface is deliberately engineered.
  • TRIM transports ions and nothing else. It has no photons: the dose of an X-ray or cobalt-60 exposure follows from the attenuation law and µen/ρ of (10.7–8), never from TRIM. It has no electron transport, takes neutrons only as recoil lists from another program, omits nuclear reactions (significant above about 5 MeV per nucleon), and stops at energy deposition, before any charge collection. Its target is amorphous, at 0 K and at zero dose: no channeling, no annealing, no build-up. A check on any run: a single homogeneous layer must return a mean stopping depth close to the tabulated projected range.
  • The quick damage mode does not follow recoils. Ion ranges and ionization by the ion match the full-cascade mode; displacements are estimated analytically; sputtering is impossible in it. A TRIM vacancy count is a count of displacement events for a stated threshold and damage option, not of defects that survive.
  • A TRIM depth plot is a density, per ion per unit depth. Integrate across a layer for a total, multiply per-Å values by 108 for per-cm, and multiply by the fluence for a volume density. Every depth scales as 1/ρ, so a compound or gas layer with an untyped density is wrong everywhere.
  • A dose must name its material. rad(Si) and rad(SiO₂) agree to 0.3 % under cobalt-60 and differ by a factor of about 1.8 under 10 keV X-rays. Oxide-charge physics runs in rad(SiO₂); standards and mission requirements are stated in rad(Si).
  • Thick oxides are worse. The oxide-trapped-charge shift grows roughly as tox²; below about 10 nm tunnelling neutralizes trapped holes and a gate oxide is nearly immune. In a modern commercial part the isolation oxide, not the gate oxide, sets the total-dose tolerance.
  • The interface-trap sign depends on the channel type. ΔVot is negative for both types; ΔVit is positive for n-channel and negative for p-channel. An n-channel transistor can show a small net shift that hides large amounts of both, and can rebound positive as the oxide charge anneals; a p-channel transistor cannot.
  • Bias and time belong to the dose. Positive gate bias is the worst case, because it drives holes and protons to the silicon. MOS gate oxides show a time dependence, not a true dose-rate dependence, so compare exposures at equal time since irradiation began. Bipolar linear circuits (ELDRS), and some thick isolation oxides, show a true low-dose-rate enhancement, which makes a high-rate test non-conservative.
  • Pairs generated are not charge collected. Initial recombination removes a fraction that depends on field and on track density, and trapping and collection remove more; every pair count from (8.2) or (13.8) is an upper bound on collected charge.

3 · Semiconductors and devices (M01)

f(E) = 11 + exp (E − EFkT)
(1.1)
f(E) probability that an allowed state at energy E holds an electron (dimensionless, 0 to 1); 1 − f(E) is the probability that it is empty, which in the valence band means it holds a hole · E energy of the state (eV) · EF Fermi level, the energy of half occupancy (eV) · k in the eV form, 8.617 × 10−5 eV/K · T absolute temperature (K); kT = 0.02585 eV at 300 K. More than 3kT above EF it equals the Boltzmann tail exp[−(E − EF)/kT] to within 5 %, and more than 3kT below it equals 1 to the same accuracy. Occupancy falls from 95 % to 5 % across 6kT, about 0.15 eV at 300 K; in pure silicon Ec sits 0.56 eV above EF, where f = 4 × 10−10. Thermal equilibrium only. M01 §2
ni = √NcNv · exp (−Eg2kT)
(1.2)
ni intrinsic carrier concentration (cm−3) · Nc = 2.86 × 1019 cm−3, Nv = 3.10 × 1019 cm−3 with Eg = 1.124 eV for Si at 300 K — one consistent set; do not mix tabulations · kT = 0.02585 eV at 300 K, so use k = 8.617 × 10−5 eV/K, not the joule form. Returns 1.08 × 1010 cm−3. The measured value long quoted at 300 K is 1.00 × 1010 cm−3; a 2003 reanalysis with band-gap narrowing gives 9.65 × 109 (Sze & Ng); older textbooks carry 1.45 × 1010. The course uses 1.0 × 1010 cm−3 throughout, 8 % below what the formula returns with this constant set. Nc and Nv themselves scale as T3/2, a small correction beside the exponential. Near room temperature ni roughly doubles for every 10 K. M01 §2
n p = ni2     n ≈ Nd  (n-type)    p ≈ Na  (p-type)
(1.3)
n, p electron and hole concentrations (cm−3) · Nd, Na donor and acceptor concentrations (cm−3). Valid in thermal equilibrium only, with the dopants fully ionized and Nd or Na ≫ ni. Fails under illumination, under bias, and whenever radiation-generated carriers are present — which is most of this course. Worked point: n-type at 1016 cm−3 gives p = 104 cm−3, twelve orders of magnitude below the majority population. M01 §3
n = ni exp (EF − EikT)    p = ni exp (Ei − EFkT)    φF = kTq ln Nani
(1.4)
n, p electron and hole concentrations (cm−3) · EF Fermi level and Ei intrinsic level, within about 1 meV of midgap in silicon at 300 K (eV) · kT in eV, 0.02585 eV at 300 K · φF bulk Fermi potential of p-type material, (Ei − EF)/q in the neutral bulk (V) · kT/q thermal voltage, 0.02585 V · Na acceptor concentration (cm−3). An energy in eV divided by q is the same number in volts. For n-type material the magnitude is (kT/q) ln(Nd/ni) with EF above Ei. With ni = 1.0 × 1010 cm−3, φF = 0.298, 0.357, 0.417 and 0.476 V for Na = 1015, 1016, 1017 and 1018 cm−3; each factor of ten in doping moves EF by kT ln 10 = 59.5 meV. The product of the first two expressions is (1.3). Non-degenerate material only, dopants fully ionized: near 1.4 × 1018 cm−3 of phosphorus at 300 K, EF comes within 3kT of Ec and the Boltzmann tail no longer describes the occupancy. M01 §3
n = Nd2 + √(Nd2)2 + ni2     p = ni2n
(1.5)
n, p electron and hole concentrations (cm−3) · Nd donor concentration, all ionized (cm−3) · ni intrinsic concentration at the temperature in question, from (1.2) (cm−3). Follows from charge neutrality, n = p + Nd, with np = ni2. Reduces to n = Nd when ni ≪ Nd and to n = ni when ni ≫ Nd; at ni = Nd it gives n = 1.62 Nd. Covers the extrinsic-to-intrinsic transition only; freeze-out needs incomplete donor ionization as well. For p-type exchange n and p and use Na. Regime markers for 1015 cm−3 of phosphorus: ionized fraction about 15 % at 50 K, one half at 65 K, 90 % at 92 K; extrinsic from roughly 150 K to 450 K; ni = Nd near 545 K (germanium, at the same doping, near 390 K). M01 §4
vd = µℰ      J = q(nµn + pµp)ℰ
(1.6)
vd drift velocity (cm/s) · µ mobility (cm²/V·s) · ℰ electric field (V/cm, not V/m) · J current density (A/cm²) · q = 1.602 × 10−19 C. Silicon at 300 K, lightly doped: µn ≈ 1,450, µp ≈ 505 cm²/V·s (Sze & Ng; tabulations differ by up to 10 %, 1,350–1,500 and 450–505, so name the source). Both fall with doping and with temperature across the range used here; in doped material the trend reverses below roughly 100 K. Fails at high field, where velocity saturates near 107 cm/s in silicon. M01 §5
ℰ = −dVdx = 1q dEidx      E = −qV
(1.7)
ℰ electric field (V/cm) · V electrostatic potential (V) · x position (cm) · Ei intrinsic level, or equally Ec or Ev, which move in parallel (J, or eV) · q = 1.602 × 10−19 C · E potential energy of an electron at potential V (J, or eV). With energies in eV, (1/q) dEi/dx is the slope in eV/cm read as V/cm: bands rising 1 eV across 1 µm hold 104 V/cm. A positive voltage lowers the bands; the field points up their slope. Electrons roll down Ec, toward high potential; holes rise along Ev, with the field. Applies wherever the potential varies — depletion region, gate oxide, channel. M01 §5
Vbi = kTq ln NaNdni2      W = √2εs(Vbi + VR)q (1Na + 1Nd)
(1.8)
Vbi built-in potential (V) · kT/q = 0.02585 V at 300 K · W depletion width (cm) · VR reverse bias, taken positive (V) · εs = 11.7 × 8.854 × 10−14 = 1.036 × 10−12 F/cm for silicon · doping in cm−3. Worked point: Na = Nd = 1016 cm−3 gives Vbi = 0.71 V, W = 0.43 µm unbiased and 1.22 µm at 5 V reverse. W grows as √(Vbi + VR), tending to √VR only once the reverse bias is large compared with Vbi. M01 §6
I = I0 [ exp (qVnkT) − 1 ]
(1.9)
I diode current (A), positive forward · I0 ideal diffusion saturation current (A), 10−12 to 10−15 A for a small silicon junction; measured reverse current is larger because depletion-region generation dominates it in silicon at and below room temperature · V applied voltage (V), negative in reverse · n ideality factor, 1 for pure diffusion current and → 2 when depletion-region generation dominates · kT/q = 0.02585 V at 300 K. I0 ∝ ni2 and therefore doubles roughly every 5 K. The familiar “leakage doubles every 10 °C” belongs to the generation current, which goes as ni and dominates measured silicon reverse current near 300 K. Beyond about −0.1 V the exponential is negligible and I → −I0. M01 §6
VFB = φms − QoxCox      Cox = εoxtox
(1.10)
VFB flat-band voltage (V) · φms gate work function minus body work function (V); for an n⁺-polysilicon gate on p-type silicon φms ≈ −(Eg/2q + φF), about −0.98 V at Na = 1017 cm−3 · Qox fixed oxide charge per unit area, taken as a sheet at the Si/SiO₂ interface (C/cm²), positive for positive charge · Cox oxide capacitance per unit area (F/cm²) · εox = 3.9 × 8.854 × 10−14 = 3.45 × 10−13 F/cm · tox oxide thickness (cm), so 10 nm gives Cox = 3.45 × 10−7 F/cm². Positive oxide charge makes VFB more negative. Charge spread through the oxide counts in proportion to its distance from the gate, so a sheet at the interface counts fully — the centroid form is (14.6). M01 §7
ψs = 2φF      Vth = VFB + 2φF + √2εsqNa · 2φFCox
(1.11)
ψs surface potential, the band bending between the neutral bulk and the surface divided by q (V), positive when the bands bend down · φF from (1.4) (V) · Vth threshold voltage (V) · VFB from (1.10) (V) · εs = 1.036 × 10−12 F/cm · q = 1.602 × 10−19 C · Na body doping (cm−3) · Cox from (1.10) (F/cm²). The square root is the depletion charge per unit area at threshold (C/cm²). The three terms: flatten the bands, bend them to inversion, drop across the oxide. Worked point: Na = 1017 cm−3, 10 nm oxide, n⁺-polysilicon gate, no oxide charge → 2φF = 0.83 V, oxide term 0.48 V, VFB = −0.98 V, so Vth ≈ 0.34 V. Long channel, uniform doping. Oxide charge shifts VFB, and so Vth, by −Qox/Cox. M01 §7
Ec(x) = Ec0 − qψ(x)      Ev(x) = Ev0 − qψ(x)
(1.12)
Ec(x), Ev(x) band edges at position x along the surface from source to drain (eV) · Ec0, Ev0 their values where ψ = 0 (eV) · ψ(x) electrostatic potential at the surface at x (V) · q elementary charge; with energies in eV, qψ in eV equals ψ in volts. This is E = −qV of (1.7) applied point by point: it draws the source–channel–drain barrier. Deck 8 labels ψ(x) the built-in potential; under bias it also carries the gate and drain voltages. M01 §8
ID ∝ exp (−EBkT)
(1.13)
ID drain current (A) · EB barrier height, Ec at the top of the source-end barrier minus EF in the source (eV) · kT in eV, 0.02585 eV at 300 K. Lowering EB by kT ln 10 = 59.5 meV at 300 K multiplies the current by ten. Holds while the barrier top lies more than a few kT above the source Fermi level: the off-state and subthreshold region. Above threshold the current is limited by channel charge and velocity instead, (1.14). Anything that lowers the barrier — a hotter crystal, positive oxide charge, DIBL — raises Ioff exponentially. ID,sat = µnCox2 · WL · (VGS − Vth)2      Cox = εoxtox
(1.14)
ID,sat saturated drain current (A) · µn channel electron mobility (cm²/V·s), lower than bulk because of surface scattering · Cox gate oxide capacitance per unit area (F/cm²) · εox = 3.9 × 8.854 × 10−14 = 3.45 × 10−13 F/cm for SiO₂ · tox oxide thickness (cm) · W, L channel width and gate length, entering only as W/L · VGS gate–source voltage (V). Long-channel and saturation only (VDS > VGS − Vth); short devices approach a linear dependence on overdrive because carrier velocity saturates. S = kTq · ln 10 · (1 + CdCox) ≥ 60 mV/decade at 300 K
(1.15)
S subthreshold swing, the gate-voltage change for one decade of drain current (mV/decade) · Cd depletion capacitance per unit area under the channel (F/cm²) · Cox from (1.10). The bracket is ≥ 1, so S cannot fall below (kT/q)·ln 10 = 59.5 mV/decade at 300 K in any conventional MOSFET — a floor set by thermal statistics, not by fabrication. Explicitly proportional to T: the floor is 19.8 mV/decade at 100 K and 99.2 mV/decade at 500 K, before the body factor. Interface traps add a term to the bracket: (14.10). M01 §8
DIBL = −ΔVthΔVDS
(1.16)
DIBL drain-induced barrier lowering (mV/V) · ΔVth change in threshold voltage (mV) · ΔVDS change in drain–source voltage over which it is measured (V), typically from about 0.05 V to the supply voltage. Positive when the threshold falls as VDS rises. It multiplies Ioff by 10DIBL·ΔVDS/S. Intel's 22 nm tri-gate transistors: about 50 mV/V, with S near 70 mV/decade. Short-channel effect: (1.14) and (1.15) are long-channel results. M01 §8

(12.1–2)

(12.3)

Two different "ionization energies" for silicon

The lectures divide an incident particle's energy by 99.42 eV to estimate how many silicon atoms it ionizes, a figure the slide labels as the binding energy of the outermost orbital. It is in fact the binding energy of a silicon 2p core electron; the outermost valence electrons are bound by a few eV, not a hundred. The quantity used throughout the radiation-effects literature is the mean electron–hole pair creation energy, 3.6 eV in silicon and about 17 eV in SiO₂, which is what determines the charge a device actually collects and is larger than the 1.12 eV bandgap because part of the deposited energy goes into lattice vibrations rather than carriers. The two answer different questions and differ by a factor near 28. Use 99 eV where the course's own arithmetic is being reproduced, and 3.6 eV for any estimate of collected charge.

NIST X-Ray Mass Attenuation Coefficients (Hubbell & Seltzer); SiO₂ row computed by Bragg additivity from the Si and O rows with wSi = 0.467 and wO = 0.533. Notice the pattern: at 1 MeV every entry lies within 15 % of every other. Among the light entries that is the Z/A result — Compton dominates and the coefficient barely varies. Lead is a coincidence rather than an instance of it: its Z/A is 0.396 against silicon's 0.498, so Compton alone would put it 21 % lower, and a residual photoelectric term worth about a fifth of its attenuation more than compensates. Lead separates decisively only at low energy, where photoelectric absorption and its Z⁴–Z⁵ dependence take over. Silicon's mass energy-absorption coefficient at 1 MeV is 2.778 × 10⁻², less than half its attenuation coefficient. M10 §7–8

Two things to carry: a 10 MeV proton just crosses a standard 600 µm wafer, and the range follows R ∝ E1.6 to E1.8, so doubling the energy roughly triples the penetration. The peak-to-entrance stopping-power ratio for a single 10 MeV proton is 15.9; averaged over a beam with ~1 % straggling it falls to about 5, and it falls with ion mass — about 1.6 for iron at the same energy per nucleon.
