Formula sheet

Every equation in the course with all its symbols and exact units, the constants needed to evaluate them, and a strategy table keyed by what a problem asks for.

Growing with the site

This sheet covers every module built so far, M01 to M12. Sections are added as modules are built. Some constants and reference values in §7 to §9 already anticipate modules not yet written, because they are the values most often needed mid-problem and are not worth waiting for.

1 · What to do, by what is asked

Asked for…StrategyWhere
Carrier concentration in doped silicon Majority carrier ≈ the dopant concentration. Get the minority carrier from np = ni2. Check the dopants are fully ionized (true at 300 K, not at cryogenic temperature). (1.2)
How a device changes with temperature Work out whether the quantity tracks ni or ni2. Leakage and reverse saturation current go as ni2, doubling roughly every 5 K; the generation current goes as ni and doubles every ~10 K, and it is the one that dominates measured silicon leakage. Mobility falls with temperature above ~100 K. Subthreshold swing is proportional to T outright. (1.1), (1.5), (1.7)
Depletion width, or the sensitive volume of a junction Get Vbi from the doping, add the reverse bias, then use the depletion-width expression. Multiply by junction area for the volume. Width goes as the square root of total voltage. (1.4)
Current from a charge deposited in a short time I = Q/t with Q = Nq. Then power P = IV, then power density P/A over the track cross-section. Keep the area in cm² and watch that 100 nm = 10−5 cm. (8.1)
Transistor drive current, or the effect of a geometry change Use the saturation expression. For a fin or nanosheet, first convert the geometry to an effective width, then substitute. (1.6), (2.2)
Whether a process can print a feature CD = k1λ/NA with k1 ≥ 0.25 for a single exposure. If the required CD needs k1 < 0.25, the answer is multiple patterning or a shorter wavelength. Check depth of focus separately. (2.3)
Equilibrium temperature of a surface in space Balance absorbed solar power against radiated power. Only the ratio αs/ε and the area ratio matter. Scale the irradiance to the orbital distance first. Add internal dissipation to the absorbed side. (3.1)
Activity of a source now, or after a stated time Get the mass of the isotope (scale the element mass by isotopic abundance), divide by its atomic mass, multiply by NA and by ln2/t1/2 with the half-life in seconds. For elapsed time, halve once per half-life rather than exponentiating. (4.4)
Dose delivered, or equivalent dose to a person Energy deposited over absorber mass, stated for a named material. Multiply by the quality factor only for biological risk — never for a device. (4.5)
Which shield material for which species Neutrons: maximise 4Mm/(M+m)² → hydrogen-rich. Gammas: maximise Z → lead. Betas: minimise Z to suppress bremsstrahlung, then a high-Z layer behind it. Ions: check the range — a shield that stops a particle inside the die is worse than none. (4.1), (9.1)
Whether a particle is trapped, and where it mirrors Gyroradius must be small against the scale over which B varies; use the momentum form for electrons. The mirror point depends only on equatorial pitch angle, not on mass or energy. (6.2)
Orbital speed, period, or time spent inside a belt Add RE to the altitude first. Circular: (7.1). Elliptical: use vis-viva at the belt-crossing radius — an eccentric orbit crosses fast and dwells slow, so exposure is the time integral, not the peak flux. (7.1), (7.2)
Carriers or charge liberated by a particle Divide deposited energy by the pair-creation energy — 99.42 eV for the course's own arithmetic, 3.6 eV for anything physical — then multiply by q. Only energy actually deposited counts. (8.2)
Peak current, power or power density from one strike Charge over collection time (~1 ps) for current, times bias for power, over the track cross-section for power density. The course's chain lands at 500 MW/cm². (8.3)
X-ray output of an electron beam or a beta shield f = 0.0007ZE with E in MeV gives the fraction radiated, spread over a continuum ending at the electron energy. It is not a photon line. (9.1)
Displacement count, or damage from a known fluence Maximum recoil energy from (4.1), then NRT for the displacement count. For an environment, integrate NIEL against the fluence spectrum to get displacement damage dose. (11.1), (11.2)
Power or delay of a logic block Dynamic power is αCV2f; static power is VIoff; delay is CV/I. Lowering frequency reduces the first and not the second. (1.8)
Shielding thickness for a photon beam, or how much of a beam a layer stops Look up μ/ρ for the material at that photon energy in the NIST table, multiply by ρ and by thickness in cm, then P = 1 − e−exponent. For a compound, mass-weight the elemental coefficients first. To attenuate by a factor n, solve μx = ln n. Add a buildup factor for anything more than about one mean free path. (10.7–8), (10.9)
Which photon interaction applies, or whether pair production matters Locate the photon energy and the target Z on the dominance map. In silicon: photoelectric below ~50 keV, Compton from there to ~15 MeV, pair production above. Exceeding the 1.022 MeV threshold does not make pair production significant. (10.6)
Dose from a photon field, rather than attenuation Use μen/ρ, not μ/ρ. The two differ by more than a factor of two in silicon at 1 MeV, because Compton scattering carries energy out of the volume. (10.7–8)
Whether a charged particle reaches a buried layer, or where it stops Get the range from a PSTAR-style table at that energy, divide by ρ for a depth, and use the projected range rather than the CSDA range. For an ion other than a proton, evaluate the proton table at E/M and scale by M/z². (12.6–7), (12.8)
Charge deposited by an ion in a sensitive volume Take LET in MeV·cm²/mg, multiply by 10.4 fC/µm for silicon, then by the path length through the volume. Compare against the node's critical charge. (12.5)

2 · Facts that hold across problems

  • This subject works in centimetres. Concentrations in cm−3, mobilities in cm²/V·s, fields in V/cm, permittivities in F/cm, current densities in A/cm². Mixing in metres is the most common source of errors of 102, 104 or 106.
  • Charge is conserved and quantised. Any count of carriers converts to a charge by multiplying by 1.602 × 10−19 C, and any charge delivered over a time is a current. Most single-event estimates are exactly this chain.
  • Check the regime before the algebra. Is the semiconductor in equilibrium (then np = ni2) or not? Is the transistor above or below threshold? Is the field low enough that v = µE still holds? Each of these has a different governing expression.
  • Exponentials dominate. Where a quantity contains exp(−E/kT), an order-of-magnitude estimate of the exponent is worth more than a precise prefactor. A factor of two in Nc is irrelevant next to a 0.1 eV error in E.
  • Order-of-magnitude first. The lectures' worked estimates deliberately round to one significant figure and then check the exponent. A power density of 108 W/cm² is a physically meaningful result; 4.87 × 108 is false precision on inputs known to a factor of two.
  • Sanity checks. Carrier concentrations cannot exceed the atomic density, 5 × 1022 cm−3. Depletion widths in ordinary doping are 0.01–10 µm. Drift velocity cannot exceed ~107 cm/s. An equilibrium temperature at 1 AU is near 278 K unless the surface is deliberately engineered.

3 · Semiconductors and devices (M01)

ni = √NcNv · exp (−Eg2kT)
(1.1)
ni intrinsic carrier concentration (cm−3) · Nc = 2.86 × 1019 cm−3, Nv = 3.10 × 1019 cm−3 with Eg = 1.124 eV for Si at 300 K — one consistent set; do not mix tabulations · kT = 0.02585 eV at 300 K, so use k = 8.617 × 10−5 eV/K, not the joule form. Returns 1.08 × 1010 cm−3; the accepted measured value is 1.00 × 1010 cm−3, which is what the course uses. Both Nc and Nv themselves scale as T3/2, which is a small correction beside the exponential. M01 §2
np = ni2     n ≈ Nd  (n-type)    p ≈ Na  (p-type)
(1.2)
n, p electron and hole concentrations (cm−3) · Nd, Na donor and acceptor concentrations (cm−3). Equilibrium only. Fails under illumination, under bias, and whenever radiation-generated carriers are present — which is most of this course. M01 §3
vd = µE      J = q(nµn + pµp)E
(1.3)
vd drift velocity (cm/s) · µ mobility (cm²/V·s) · E field (V/cm) · J current density (A/cm²). Silicon at 300 K, lightly doped: µn ≈ 1,450, µp ≈ 505 cm²/V·s (Sze & Ng; tabulations range 1,350–1,500 and 450–505). Both fall with doping and with temperature across the range used here; in doped material the trend reverses below about 100 K. Fails above ~104 V/cm, where velocity saturates near 107 cm/s. M01 §4
Vbi = kTq ln NaNdni2      W = √2εs(Vbi + VR)q (1Na + 1Nd)
(1.4)
Vbi built-in potential (V) · kT/q = 0.02585 V at 300 K · W depletion width (cm) · VR reverse bias, taken positive (V) · εs = 1.036 × 10−12 F/cm for silicon · doping in cm−3. Worked point: Na = Nd = 1016 cm−3 gives Vbi = 0.71 V, W = 0.43 µm unbiased and 1.22 µm at 5 V reverse. W grows as √(Vbi + VR), not √VR. M01 §5
I = I0 [ exp (qVnkT) − 1 ]
(1.5)
I diode current (A), positive forward · I0 ideal diffusion saturation current (A), 10−12 to 10−15 A for a small silicon junction; measured reverse current is larger because depletion-region generation dominates it in silicon · V applied voltage (V), negative in reverse · n ideality factor, 1 for diffusion-dominated and → 2 for generation-dominated current. I0 ∝ ni2 and therefore doubles roughly every 5 K. The familiar “doubles every 10 °C” belongs to the generation current, which goes as ni. Beyond about −0.1 V the exponential is negligible and I → −I0. M01 §5
ID,sat = µnCox2 · WL · (VGS − Vth)2      Cox = εoxtox
(1.6)
ID,sat saturated drain current (A) · Cox gate oxide capacitance per unit area (F/cm²) · εox = 3.45 × 10−13 F/cm for SiO₂ · tox oxide thickness (cm) · W/L width-to-length ratio, dimensionless. Long-channel and saturation only (VDS > VGS − Vth); short devices tend toward a linear dependence on overdrive because velocity saturates. M01 §6
S = kTq · ln 10 · (1 + CdCox) ≥ 59.5 mV/decade at 300 K
(1.7)
S subthreshold swing (mV/decade) · Cd depletion capacitance per unit area under the channel (F/cm²). The bracket is ≥ 1, so the floor is set by thermal statistics and cannot be engineered away in a conventional MOSFET. Explicitly proportional to T: 19.8 mV/decade at 100 K and 99.2 mV/decade at 500 K, which is why cryogenic electronics switch more sharply and hot electronics leak. M01 §6
td ≈ CLVDDID,sat      Pdyn = αCLVDD2f      Pstat = VDDIoff
(1.8)
td gate delay (s) · CL load capacitance (F) · VDD supply voltage (V) · α activity factor, 0.1–0.2 typically · f clock frequency (Hz) · Ioff off-state current per device (A), multiplied by the device count for a whole chip. Lowering f reduces Pdyn and not Pstat, which is why leakage came to dominate as frequency stalled. M01 §7

4 · Scaling and lithography (M02)

N(t) = N0 · 2t/T,    T ≈ 2 years
(2.1)
N transistors per chip · t elapsed time (years) · T doubling period. Empirical and economic, not physical. Describes count only — speed and power followed from Dennard scaling, which has failed while count has partly continued. M02 §2
Weff,fin = 2Hfin + Wfin      Weff,sheet = 2n(Wsh + Tsh)
(2.2)
Weff effective channel width for substitution into (1.6) (nm) · Hfin, Wfin fin height and thickness (nm) · n sheets in the stack · Wsh, Tsh sheet width and thickness (nm). A 50 nm × 7 nm fin gives Weff = 107 nm from a 7 nm footprint. Fin width is quantised in whole fins; nanosheet width is continuous. M02 §3
CD = k1 λNA      DOF = k2 λNA2      NA = n sin θ
(2.3)
CD critical dimension, smallest resolvable half-pitch (nm) · λ exposure wavelength (nm) · NA numerical aperture, dimensionless · n index of the medium at the wafer: 1.0 dry, 1.44 for water at 193 nm · k1 ≈ 0.6 typical, floored at 0.25 for a single exposure · k2 ≈ 0.5 · DOF depth of focus (nm). Raising NA helps CD linearly and hurts DOF quadratically; only shortening λ improves both. M02 §6
2w0 = (4λπ) (FD) = 2π λNA
(2.4)
The course's form of the resolution limit. 2w0 focused spot diameter to the 1/e2 points (nm) · λ wavelength (nm) · F/D focal length over lens diameter, the f-number — dimensionless, so use any consistent length unit. Worked case from the lecture: D = 10 mm, F = 1 mm, λ = 200 nm → 25 nm (the slide prints 24 nm). Reproduce that, but know it is not a physical spot: f/0.1 implies NA ≈ 5, and NA = n sin θ cannot exceed 1 in air. The exact relation tan θ = D/2F gives NA = 0.98 and a real spot near 130 nm; the diffraction limit here is about λ/2 = 100 nm. NA ≈ D/2F is paraxial only — 5 % high at NA = 0.3, 15 % at NA = 0.5, and divergent below f/1. Where it holds, (2.4) shares the λ/NA form of (2.3) with prefactor 2/π = 0.64, but the two describe different things: one focused Gaussian beam against a resolved half-pitch. Resolution figures quoted in the course: electron-beam ~2 nm isolated features (~5 nm half-pitch), EUV ~8 nm half-pitch by interference lithography — a 0.33 NA production scanner resolves nearer 13 nm. M02 §6

5 · The space environment (M03)

Teq = [ αsSAproj + PintεσArad ]1/4      S(r) = S0 (1 AUr)2
(3.1)
Teq equilibrium temperature (K) · αs solar absorptance (0–1) · S irradiance (W/m²), S0 = 1,361 W/m² at 1 AU · Aproj area projected sunward (m²) · Pint internal dissipation (W) · ε infrared emittance (0–1) · σ = 5.670 × 10−8 W/m²·K⁴ · Arad total radiating area (m²). For a sphere, Aproj/Arad = 1/4; with αs = ε and no internal power this gives 278 K. Metres and kelvin here, unlike the rest of the sheet. M03 §2

Charge, current and power density (M08)

Q = Nq     I = QΔt     P = IV     PA = power density
(8.3)
N number of carriers generated · q = 1.602 × 10−19 C · Q collected charge (C) · Δt collection time (s; the prompt drift component is of order 10−12 s) · V junction bias (V) · A track cross-sectional area (cm²). The course's worked chain, exactly as lectured: one heavy ion creates 106 carriers → Q = 1.6 × 10−13 C → over τ ≈ 10−12 s → I = 0.1 A, far above the reverse-bias current → at −0.5 V, P = 0.05 W → over (100 nm)² = 10−10 cm² → 500 MW/cm². For comparison the lecture gives a Lockheed Martin laser weapon at ~50 MW/cm², a factor of ten lower. (1.6 × 10−13 C in 1 ps is 0.16 A; the course rounds to 0.1 A and carries that through.) Mechanism in M08 and M15.

6 · Radiation, the environment, and interaction (M04–M12)

Radioactive decay and activity (M04)

N = N0 e−λt
(4.2)
N nuclei remaining at time t · N0 nuclei at t = 0 · λ decay constant (s−1) · t elapsed time (s). λ and t must share a time unit. When the elapsed time is a whole number of half-lives, halve repeatedly instead — no exponential needed. M04 §6
λ = ln 2t1/2
(4.3)
λ decay constant (s−1) · t1/2 half-life (s) · ln 2 = 0.693. Convert years first: 1 y = 3.156 × 107 s. The fraction remaining after time t is 2−t/t1/2. M04 §6
A = mma NA ln 2t1/2   =   λN
(4.4)
A activity (Bq = decays/s; 1 Ci = 3.7 × 1010 Bq) · m mass of the radioactive isotope present (g) — not the mass of the element; scale by isotopic abundance first · ma atomic mass of that isotope (g/mol) · NA = 6.022 × 1023 mol−1 · t1/2 half-life (s). Course worked case: 1 g of potassium holds 117 µg of K-40 (ma = 39.964, t1/2 = 4 × 1016 s) → ≈ 30 Bq; an adult body ≈ 4,430 Bq of K-40. M04 §7
D = Eabsorbedm
(4.5)
D absorbed dose (Gy = J/kg; 1 rad = 0.01 Gy) · Eabsorbed energy deposited (J) · m mass of the absorber (kg). Always dose in a stated material — rad(Si) and rad(SiO₂) differ for the same field. Device hardness is quoted as absorbed dose, never as equivalent dose. M04 §9
H = Q · D
(4.6)
H equivalent dose (Sv; 1 Sv = 100 rem) · Q quality factor (dimensionless) · D absorbed dose (Gy). Rad in → rem out; gray in → sievert out. Course table: X-ray, gamma and beta 1; thermal neutrons 5; fast neutrons 10; alpha 20. Biological risk only — meaningless for a semiconductor. M04 §9
(ΔEE)max = 4Mm(M + m)²
(4.1)
Fraction of kinetic energy transferred in a head-on elastic collision. m incident particle mass · M target nucleus mass (any consistent unit — only the ratio enters). Maximised at M = m. Values: hydrogen 1.00, carbon 0.28, silicon 0.133, lead 0.019. Explains why hydrogen-rich material moderates neutrons and lead does not, and sets the maximum primary knock-on energy in M11. M04 §5

Trapping and orbits (M06–M07)

F = q(E + v × B)
(6.1)
F force (N) · q signed charge (C) · E electric field (V/m) · v velocity (m/s) · B flux density (T; Earth's surface field 5 × 10−5 T = 0.5 G). The magnetic term is perpendicular to v, so it does no work: a trapped particle keeps its energy indefinitely. Reversing q reverses the curvature, which is what separates protons from electrons. M06 §2
rg = m v⊥q B = p⊥q B
(6.2)
rg gyroradius (m) · m mass (kg) · v⊥ velocity perpendicular to B (m/s) · p⊥ perpendicular momentum (kg·m/s) — use the momentum form for relativistic particles, p = γmv · q charge magnitude (C) · B flux density (T). In 50 µT a 1 MeV proton gives ≈ 2.9 km and a 1 MeV electron ≈ 95 m. Trapping requires rg small against the scale over which B varies. Mirroring separately conserves mv⊥²/2B. M06 §3
v = GMr    T = 2π r³GM
(7.1)
v circular orbital speed (m/s) · T period (s) · r radius from Earth's centre (m) — add RE to any quoted altitude · GM⊕ = 3.986 × 1014 m³/s². Circular orbits only. Check values: 420 km → 7.66 km/s, 92.8 min; 20,200 km → 3.87 km/s, 11 h 58 min; 35,786 km → 3.07 km/s, 23 h 56 min. M07 §2
v² = GM (2r − 1a)
(7.2)
v instantaneous speed (m/s) · r current radius from Earth's centre (m) · a semi-major axis = ½(rperigee + rapogee) (m) · GM⊕ = 3.986 × 1014 m³/s². Reduces to (7.1) at r = a; the period depends on a alone. Molniya check (600 km / 39,800 km, a = 26,571 km): 10.0 km/s at perigee, 1.5 km/s at apogee. M07 §2

Charged-particle interaction (M08–M09)

F = k q1q2r²
(8.1)
F force (N) · k = 9 × 109 N·m²/C² · q1, q2 charges (C) · r separation (metres — the one place in this course that is not centimetres). Its infinite range is why a charged particle interacts with every atom it passes and an uncharged one must hit a nucleus. M08 §2
Npairs = EdepEpair
(8.2)
Npairs electron–hole pairs generated · Edep energy actually deposited (eV) — a particle that passes through leaves only part of its energy · Epair mean energy per pair (eV). The course uses 99.42 eV for silicon (1 MeV → 104); the measured value is 3.6 eV for Si and ~17 eV for SiO₂ (1 MeV → 2.8 × 105). See the warning in §8. M08 §5
f = 0.0007 Z E      f = (6 × 10−4) Z E1 + (6 × 10−4) Z E
(9.1)
f fraction of an electron's kinetic energy radiated as bremsstrahlung — dimensionless, 0 to 1 · Z atomic number of the target · E electron kinetic energy (MeV). The left form is valid while f ≪ 1; the right form saturates. 1 MeV electron in Si (Z = 14) → 9.8 keV (saturating form 8.3 keV); in W (Z = 74) → ~5 %. Deck 5 p. 23 evaluates this with 0.007 and prints 98 keV — a factor of ten above its own printed formula. f is a fraction spread over a continuum and never predicts a photon line. M09 §4
Emax = e Vtube
(9.2)
Emax highest photon energy in an X-ray tube beam (eV) · e elementary charge · Vtube accelerating potential (V). Numerically, V kilovolts gives an endpoint at V keV — the meaning of “kVp”. The mean beam energy is a third to a half of the endpoint, and filtration raises it (beam hardening) while lowering intensity. M09 §5

Displacement damage (M11)

Nd = 0.8 Tdam2 Ed
(11.1)
Nd atoms displaced by one cascade · Tdam damage energy — the part of the recoil energy going to atomic collisions rather than ionization (eV) · Ed threshold displacement energy (eV; ~21 eV for Si, published values 13–25 eV) · 0.8 the Norgett–Robinson–Torrens efficiency factor. Counts displacements, not survivors — most Frenkel pairs recombine promptly. M11 §3
Dd = NIEL(E) · Φ
(11.2)
Dd displacement damage dose (MeV/g) · NIEL non-ionizing energy loss at the particle energy (MeV·cm²/g) · Φ fluence (cm−2). For a spectrum, integrate NIEL(E) against the differential fluence — NIEL varies over orders of magnitude with energy, so a fluence quoted without a spectrum cannot be converted. Degradation correlates with Dd largely independently of species, which is what makes single-species ground testing meaningful. M11 §4

Photon interaction with matter (M10)

Eph = hcλ = hf      pph = Ephc = hλ
(10.1–2)
Eph photon energy · pph photon momentum (kg·m/s) · h = 6.626 × 10−34 J·s · c = 2.998 × 108 m/s · λ wavelength (m). Use hc = 1239.8 eV·nm: E(eV) = 1239.8 / λ(nm), no unit conversion needed. p = E/c holds only for massless particles; never write p = mv for a photon. 500 nm → 2.48 eV. M10 §1
Te = Eph − Eb
(10.3)
Te photoelectron kinetic energy (eV) · Eb shell binding energy (eV). Requires a bound electron — a free electron cannot absorb a photon and conserve both energy and momentum. Cross-section ∝ Z4–Z5 and ∝ E−3. Si: 2p level 99.4 eV, K edge 1.839 keV (μ/ρ steps 309 → 3192 cm²/g). M10 §3
Δλ = hmec (1 − cos θ)      E′ = Eph1 + (Eph ⁄ mec²)(1 − cos θ)
(10.4–5)
θ photon scattering angle from its original direction · h/mec = 2.426 pm, the Compton wavelength of the electron · mec² = 0.511 MeV — Eph and mec² must share a unit. Recoil electron takes Eph − E′; maximum at θ = 180° is the Compton edge (1.25 MeV → 1.04 MeV electron, 0.212 MeV photon). Per atom σ ∝ Z, so per gram it follows Z/A ≈ 0.5 and hardly varies between light elements. M10 §4
Eph ≥ 2mec² = 1.022 MeV
(10.6)
Threshold for pair production in the field of a nucleus, which is required to absorb the momentum. λ = 1.21 pm at threshold. Excess energy is shared as kinetic energy between electron and positron; an equal split is the average, not a rule. Triplet production, in the field of an atomic electron, needs 4mec² = 2.044 MeV. The positron annihilates into two back-to-back 0.511 MeV photons. σ ∝ Z², rising with energy. Being above threshold is not the same as being significant — at Co-60 energies in silicon, pair production is negligible and Compton dominates. M10 §5
I = I0 e−μx    P = 1 − e−(μ/ρ)ρx    λmfp = 1μ
(10.7–8, 10.10)
I uncollided intensity · P probability one photon interacts in thickness x · μ linear attenuation coefficient (cm−1) · μ/ρ mass attenuation coefficient (cm²/g, from NIST) · ρ density (g/cm³) · x thickness (cm — convert µm by ×10−4). μ/ρ is for attenuation; μen/ρ is for dose — they differ by more than 2× in Si at 1 MeV (6.361 × 10−2 vs 2.778 × 10−2). Two table rows at one energy straddle an absorption edge: lower value below, upper above. Oblique incidence: replace x by x/cos θ. M10 §7
(μρ) = Σ wi (μρ)i      Itotal = B I0 e−μx
(10.9, 10.11)
wi mass fraction of element i (from atomic weights, not atom counts; SiO&sub2; gives 0.467 Si / 0.533 O) · B buildup factor (≥ 1), which accounts for scattered photons that still arrive. B = 1 only for a narrow collimated beam; it grows with shield thickness, so the plain exponential is a lower bound on what gets through a real shield. M10 §7

Stopping power, range and LET (M12)

S = − dEdx      Stotal = Selec + Snuc
(12.1–2)
S linear stopping power (MeV/cm); S/ρ mass stopping power (MeV·cm²/g, what tables list) · Selec energy to target electrons (ionization and excitation) · Snuc energy to target nuclei (elastic, screened Coulomb — not the strong force, not a nuclear reaction). Electronic dominates by 3–4 orders of magnitude for light ions at MeV energies; nuclear is what makes displacements. M12 §1
Selecρ = K ZA z²β² [ ln (2mec²β²γ²I) − β² ]
(12.3)
K = 0.307 MeV·cm²/mol · Z, A of the target · z charge number of the projectile · β = v/c · γ = 1/√(1−β²) · I mean excitation energy, 173 eV for Si. The two dependences that matter: ∝ z² (why heavy ions dominate) and ∝ 1/β² (why the Bragg peak exists). Fails once the projectile is no longer fast compared with the target's own electrons — with I = 173 eV the printed expression already peaks near 216 keV and is meaningless below ~79 keV. Proton stopping in Si really peaks at 549 MeV·cm²/g at 55 keV and falls below that on both sides, becoming proportional to velocity at the bottom (Lindhard–Scharff). Effective-charge loss from electron capture governs heavy ions, not protons. M12 §2
Q′ = LET · ρ · qEehp = 10.4 fCµm per MeV·cm²mg
(12.5)
Q′ charge liberated per unit track length · LET in MeV·cm²/mg (note milligrams — the field's unit) · ρ = 2330 mg/cm³ for Si · Eehp = 3.6 eV in Si. The 10.4 fC/µm figure is silicon-specific; equivalently 96.4 MeV·cm²/mg → 1 pC/µm. LET is the locally deposited part of the energy loss: it excludes delta rays energetic enough to leave the track. M12 §4
RCSDA = ∫0E0 dEStotal(E)      fd = RprojRCSDA
(12.6–7)
RCSDA range in the continuous-slowing-down approximation (g/cm² — divide by ρ for cm) · fd detour factor. RCSDA is path length; Rproj is penetration depth. Protons in Si: fd = 0.995 at 10 MeV, 0.55 at 10 keV; for electrons it is far smaller at all energies. Neither range is a sharp cut-off — straggling spreads the stopping depth. M12 §5
Sion(E) ≈ z² Sp(EM)      Rion(E) ≈ Mz² Rp(EM)
(12.8)
Gets any ion from a proton table · M ion mass number · z ion charge number · compare at equal velocity, meaning equal E/M, never at equal energy. Alpha (M = 4, z = 2): Rα(E) ≈ Rp(E/4). Valid only while both are fully stripped, so it degrades near end of range. Range–energy power law for protons in Si: R ∝ E1.6 to E1.8. M12 §7

Two different "ionization energies" for silicon

The lectures divide an incident particle's energy by 99.42 eV to estimate how many silicon atoms it ionizes, a figure the slide labels as the binding energy of the outermost orbital. It is in fact the binding energy of a silicon 2p core electron; the outermost valence electrons are bound by a few eV, not a hundred. The quantity used throughout the radiation-effects literature is the mean electron–hole pair creation energy, 3.6 eV in silicon and about 17 eV in SiO₂, which is what determines the charge a device actually collects and is larger than the 1.12 eV bandgap because part of the deposited energy goes into lattice vibrations rather than carriers. The two answer different questions and differ by a factor near 28. Use 99 eV where the course's own arithmetic is being reproduced, and 3.6 eV for any estimate of collected charge.
