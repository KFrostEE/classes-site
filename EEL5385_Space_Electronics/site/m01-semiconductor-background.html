Module 01

Semiconductors and Devices: the Working Background

The solid-state physics and device picture on which total ionizing dose and every later radiation effect rest: energy bands, carriers and the Fermi level, doping and its temperature limits, band bending under an applied voltage, the p–n junction, the MOS capacitor, and the MOSFET as a barrier-controlled switch.

Prerequisites none Read time ~75 min Sources Decks 2, 5, 8; Lecture 8; Sze & Ng; Pierret; Hu

Every effect this course studies is ultimately a change to one of a small number of quantities inside a semiconductor device: how many mobile charges there are, where they are, how fast they move, and how long they survive. Radiation creates charge where there should be none and defects where the lattice should be perfect. Temperature changes how many carriers exist and how easily they move. Packaging changes the mechanical and thermal boundary conditions those devices sit inside. None of that can be reasoned about without a working picture of the device itself.

This module supplies that picture. Lecture 8 taught it as the foundation for total ionizing dose, in an order this page follows: the band gap as a consequence of periodicity, dopants and the temperature range over which they control the carrier population, the rule that converts a voltage into an electron energy, and then the MOS structure read through its band diagram — first vertically through the gate, where a positive gate voltage bends the bands and inverts the surface, and then along the channel, where the transistor appears as an energy barrier that the gate and drain voltages raise and lower. The junction, transport and speed material comes from Lectures 2, 4 and 5, which used it before it was taught.

Three things here are load-bearing later and are treated in more detail than a first course would: the reverse-biased p–n junction, which is the object that collects radiation-generated charge in M08 and M15; the MOS band diagram under positive gate bias, on which M14 follows radiation-generated charge through the oxide; and off-state leakage, which is the quantity that both radiation and temperature attack.

1 · From atomic levels to energy bands

An isolated atom holds its electrons in discrete energy levels. An electron may occupy one of those levels or another, and nothing between: absorbing exactly the energy difference moves it up, and falling back emits a photon of exactly that energy. This is why an isolated atom of hydrogen, helium or mercury emits a line spectrum — a set of sharp wavelengths characteristic of that element rather than a continuous band of colour.

Bringing two identical atoms close enough that their electron wavefunctions overlap changes this. The Pauli exclusion principle forbids two electrons in the same quantum state, so each shared level must split into two levels of slightly different energy. Four atoms split it into four. A crystal of silicon holds about 5.0 × 1022 atoms per cubic centimetre, so every atomic level splits into of order 1023 levels spaced so finely that they are indistinguishable from a continuous range of allowed energies. That range is an energy band.

Between the bands lies a range of energies with no allowed states, called the bandgap, Eg. The gap is a consequence of the crystal's periodicity. An electron in a periodic potential behaves as a wave, and a wave whose wavelength is twice the lattice spacing, λ = 2a, meets the Bragg condition and is reflected by every atomic plane at once. Incident and reflected waves superpose into two standing waves of the same wavevector: one piles electron density onto the ion cores, the other between them. Those two arrangements have different potential energies, and that difference is the gap.

The gap follows from periodicity alone and appears for any wave in a periodic medium. Lecture 8 demonstrated it with an elastic wave (Deck 8 p. 5). A finite-element simulation drives a vibration along a beam perforated by a regular row of holes of period a. At 17.8 MHz the wave travels the length of the beam. At 25.5 MHz its amplitude decays along the beam and arrives at the fiftieth period about 70 dB below its input. The dispersion relation of the beam — the frequency of each mode it supports against that mode's wavevector — shows why. The lower band rises until its wavevector reaches the Brillouin-zone boundary, k = π/a, at 24 MHz, where the wavelength is 2a and each period reflects the wave in phase with the reflections from every other period. The next band begins at the same boundary at 27.5 MHz. Between the two no real wavevector exists: a vibration at 25.5 MHz has a complex wavevector, so its amplitude decays exponentially along the beam instead of propagating. At the zone boundary the two standing waves distribute the vibration differently within each period, and their frequency difference is the width of the forbidden range, which acoustics calls a stop band.

Two bands matter. The valence band is the highest band that is full of electrons at absolute zero; its electrons are bound into chemical bonds and carry no net current, because for every electron moving one way there is another moving the opposite way. The conduction band is the lowest empty band; an electron placed in it has empty states immediately above it in energy, so an applied electric field can accelerate it and it carries current. The size of the gap between them separates the three classes of solid.

Silicon at 300 K

Diamond cubic lattice, constant 5.431 Å, 5.0 × 1022 atoms/cm³. Eg = 1.12 eV at 300 K, rising to 1.17 eV at 0 K: the gap narrows monotonically as temperature increases. Relative permittivity 11.7 — Sze & Ng use 11.9 and Pierret 11.8, which moves every depletion width below by under 1 %. The lectures round the gap to 1.1 eV and that value is used in the course's arithmetic.

2 · Electrons, holes, and the Fermi level

At any temperature above absolute zero, lattice vibrations supply energy in a statistical spread, and a small fraction of valence electrons receive more than Eg and are promoted into the conduction band. Each promotion leaves an empty state in the valence band. Because the valence band is otherwise full, a neighbouring electron can move into that empty state, leaving a new empty state behind it. The vacancy propagates through the crystal and responds to an electric field as though it were a particle of charge +q. That quasiparticle is a hole. Within the semiclassical band model it carries exactly the net current and crystal momentum of all the remaining valence electrons, which is what makes it useful; it describes that collective motion rather than any real particle, and silicon in fact has heavy-hole, light-hole and split-off bands whose differing effective masses the single symbol averages over.

Promotion therefore always produces a pair, and this is the single most important fact for everything that follows: ionizing radiation deposits energy in a semiconductor and the energy appears as electron–hole pairs. The mechanism is identical to thermal generation; only the energy source differs.

How many electrons reach the conduction band depends on two things: how many allowed states the band offers near its edge, and the probability that a state at a given energy is occupied. Electrons obey the exclusion principle, so that probability is the Fermi–Dirac function.

f(E) = 11 + exp (E − EFkT)
(1.1)
f(E) probability that an allowed state at energy E holds an electron (dimensionless, 0 to 1); 1 − f(E) is the probability that it is empty, which in the valence band means it holds a hole · E energy of the state (eV) · EF Fermi level (eV) · k Boltzmann constant in the eV form, 8.617 × 10−5 eV/K · T absolute temperature (K); kT = 0.02585 eV at 300 K. More than 3kT above EF the function equals the Boltzmann tail exp[−(E − EF)/kT] to within 5 %, and more than 3kT below it equals 1 to the same accuracy. Thermal equilibrium only. On the formula sheet as (1.1).

The Fermi level EF is the energy at which a state, were one present, would be occupied with probability one half, at any temperature. In a semiconductor it normally lies inside the gap, where no states exist, and serves as the reference from which each band's population is read: the farther a band edge lies from EF, the emptier of electrons (conduction band) or of holes (valence band) that band is. At absolute zero f(E) is a step. At 300 K the step rounds off: occupancy falls from 95 % to 5 % across 6kT, about 0.15 eV, which is small against the 1.12 eV gap. In pure silicon the conduction band edge sits 0.56 eV above EF, where f = 4 × 10−10, so only the exponential tail of the distribution reaches the band. That tail is the reason carrier populations depend exponentially on energy and on 1/T throughout this course.

In a pure crystal the two populations are equal, and their common value is the intrinsic carrier concentration, ni. Its magnitude follows from the Boltzmann tail of equation (1.1) multiplied by the number of states near each band edge. The Fermi level of the pure crystal is the intrinsic level, Ei. It lies within about 1 meV of midgap in silicon at 300 K, because the two bands offer nearly equal numbers of states near their edges, and Deck 8 draws it as the dashed line through the centre of the gap.

ni = √NcNv · exp (−Eg2kT)
(1.2)

Take Nc, Nv and Eg from one self-consistent set: mixing tabulations is the commonest way to get a wrong answer from this expression. With the values above it returns 1.08 × 1010 cm−3. The measured value long quoted at 300 K is 1.00 × 1010 cm−3; a 2003 reanalysis that accounts for band-gap narrowing gives 9.65 × 109, the value Sze & Ng adopt; older textbooks carry 1.45 × 1010; and this course uses 1.0 × 1010 throughout, 8 % below what the formula returns with this constant set. On the formula sheet as (1.2).

Two features of equation (1.2) do most of the work in this course. First, the prefactor is around 1019 cm−3 and the answer is 1010 cm−3, so the exponential suppresses the carrier population by nine orders of magnitude: only about one silicon atom in 5 × 1012 contributes a conduction electron. Second, that exponential contains Eg/kT, so ni roughly doubles for every 10 K rise near room temperature and climbs by orders of magnitude over the temperature range a spacecraft sees. This single dependence is why silicon fails at high temperature and why wide-bandgap materials replace it, which is the subject of M17.

3 · Doping

An intrinsic crystal is a poor conductor and, worse, its conductivity is set entirely by temperature. Devices need carrier populations that are fixed by design. That is achieved by doping — substituting a small fraction of the silicon atoms with atoms from a neighbouring column of the periodic table.

Silicon has four valence electrons and forms four covalent bonds. A phosphorus atom has five. Substituted onto a lattice site it satisfies the four bonds and has one electron left over, bound to the phosphorus ion so weakly that 45 meV releases it — less than twice kT at room temperature, so essentially every such atom is ionized at 300 K. In band terms, phosphorus introduces an allowed level 45 meV below the conduction band edge, and its electron is promoted from there rather than from across the full gap. Phosphorus, arsenic and antimony are donors, and the result is n-type silicon.

Boron has three valence electrons. Substituted onto a lattice site it leaves one bond unsatisfied, and an electron from a neighbouring bond fills it at a cost of 45 meV, leaving a mobile hole behind. Boron introduces a level just above the valence band edge and is an acceptor; the result is p-type silicon.

The level is shallow because the leftover electron is bound to the P⁺ ion by a Coulomb attraction that the surrounding silicon weakens twice over: the lattice's relative permittivity of 11.7 screens the charge, and the electron moves with the conduction-band effective mass, about a quarter of the free-electron mass. Treating the pair as a hydrogen atom with those two substitutions scales hydrogen's 13.6 eV binding energy down to a few tens of meV and stretches the orbit to a radius of about 2 nm, several lattice constants. The state is therefore localized at the dopant in space but lies only just below Ec in energy. The differences between species — 33 meV for lithium, 54 meV for arsenic — come from the potential close to the ion core, where the screened-Coulomb picture fails and each element's own core structure sets the correction.

Typical doping densities run from 1015 cm−3 in a lightly doped substrate to 1020 cm−3 in a contact region. Against silicon's 5.0 × 1022 atoms/cm³, a doping of 1018 cm−3 replaces one atom in 5 × 104, and even 1020 replaces only one in five hundred. The host's bands, gap and bonding survive essentially unchanged; what the dopant adds is a new localized level inside the gap, and it raises the electron population ten orders of magnitude above intrinsic. Doping is a controlled introduction of foreign atoms into an otherwise perfect lattice — the same class of perturbation that displacement damage produces, differing in that the dopant sits substitutionally on a lattice site and produces a shallow level, while a radiation defect generally does neither.

A useful dopant has to pass three tests at once. Its level must be shallow enough for thermal energy to ionize it fully at the operating temperature. It must dissolve substitutionally in silicon to the concentration the device needs, up to about 1020 cm−3 in a contact region. And it must stay where it is put. Deck 8 p. 9 reproduces the standard compilation of measured impurity levels in silicon; several elements pass the first test, and only phosphorus, arsenic and boron pass all three, which is why they are the dopants of silicon technology. Arsenic and phosphorus dissolve to about 1021 cm−3, boron to several times 1020.

The other shallow elements each fail a different test. Antimony dissolves only to 1019–1020 cm−3 and diffuses slowly, which suits it to buried layers rather than contacts. Aluminium, gallium, indium and bismuth dissolve at most to a few times 1019 cm−3, gallium to about 4 × 1019 and indium and bismuth to about 1018. Aluminium is the fastest-diffusing acceptor, and gallium diffuses rapidly through SiO₂, so an oxide layer cannot mask it during processing. Indium's level lies 0.16 eV above Ev, about 6kT at 300 K, so only part of it is ionized — roughly 70 % at 1016 cm−3 and a third at 1017 cm−3 — and the ionized fraction changes with temperature, so the hole density it supplies is not fixed by design. Lithium has the shallowest donor level on the chart but sits between lattice sites rather than on them. As an interstitial it diffuses orders of magnitude faster than any substitutional dopant, and the Li⁺ ion drifts in an applied field at modestly raised temperature. Radiation-detector makers exploit that drift to compensate thick silicon into lithium-drifted detectors; the same property rules lithium out of any device whose doping profile must survive bias.

Elements whose levels lie near midgap, such as gold, are not ionized by thermal energy and do not dope. They act instead as generation–recombination centres, capturing and emitting carriers to and from both bands, which is the role the defects of M11 play.

Doping does not rise without limit. Above about 2–3 × 1020 cm−3, arsenic atoms combine with vacancies into electrically inactive clusters, and boron beyond its solubility forms clusters with silicon interstitials and precipitates, so the chemical concentration keeps rising while the electrically active concentration saturates. Dopant atoms themselves move only by thermal diffusion at processing temperatures of several hundred to over a thousand degrees Celsius. At operating temperature they are fixed; only the carriers they release move, and once released a carrier's motion does not depend on where the individual dopant atoms sit.

At high concentration the dopants also stop behaving as isolated centres. When their bound orbits, a few nanometres across, begin to overlap, the discrete donor levels broaden into an impurity band that merges with the conduction band. In phosphorus-doped silicon this insulator–metal transition occurs at 3.7 × 1018 cm−3; above it the material conducts even at absolute zero. Slightly below that, near 1.4 × 1018 cm−3 at 300 K, EF comes within 3kT of Ec and the Boltzmann tail no longer describes the occupancy. Material beyond these limits is degenerate. The source and drain of a MOSFET, at 1019 to beyond 1020 cm−3, are degenerate, with EF at or above Ec.

In doped material the two carrier populations are no longer equal, but their product is fixed at a given temperature.

n p = ni2     n ≈ Nd  (n-type)    p ≈ Na  (p-type)
(1.3)

It does not hold under illumination, under bias, or while radiation-generated carriers are present — those are exactly the non-equilibrium conditions the rest of the course studies. In n-type silicon doped 1016 cm−3, p = 1020/1016 = 104 cm−3: the minority population is twelve orders of magnitude below the majority, which is why a small injected charge changes it completely. On the formula sheet as (1.3).
Minority carriers are the sensitive quantity

A device is disturbed when a carrier population changes appreciably. Adding 1010 electrons per cubic centimetre to a region holding 1016 majority electrons changes nothing measurable. Adding the same 1010 holes to a region holding 104 of them changes the hole population by six orders of magnitude. Every radiation effect that appears as a current — junction leakage, single-event transients, solar-cell degradation — is a minority-carrier effect for this reason.

Doping moves the Fermi level. Donors add electrons to the crystal, so the energy at which occupancy is one half rises toward Ec; acceptors remove them and it falls toward Ev. Measured from the intrinsic level, the displacement fixes both populations, and it defines the potential that §7 uses to locate the onset of inversion.

4 · Temperature: freeze-out, extrinsic and intrinsic

Doping fixes the carrier population only over a window of temperature. Below the window the dopants keep their carriers bound; inside it the doping alone sets the carrier density; above it thermal generation across the full gap overwhelms the doping. Deck 8 p. 10 asks for the shape of this curve to be remembered, and every temperature effect in M17 is a departure from its flat middle.

At absolute zero every donor electron is bound and n = 0: the crystal is an insulator. As temperature rises, the Boltzmann tail of equation (1.1) reaches the donor level first, because that level lies only 45 meV below Ec, while the valence band lies 1.12 eV below it. The cartoon of Deck 8 p. 7 draws the donors ionized by about 50 K. Ionization is under way there but not complete: for 1015 cm−3 of phosphorus the ionized fraction is about 15 % at 50 K, one half at 65 K and 90 % at 92 K. This low-temperature range, in which n < Nd, is freeze-out.

From roughly 150 K to 450 K essentially every donor is ionized while ni remains far below Nd, so n = Nd: the doping alone sets the population. This extrinsic plateau is the working range of a silicon device. Its carrier density, and with it resistance, junction behaviour and threshold voltage, stays fixed by design across a range of 300 K.

Above the plateau ni climbs exponentially, as equation (1.2) requires, and eventually overtakes the doping. Charge neutrality — electrons balance holes plus ionized donors — then fixes the electron density.

n = Nd2 + √(Nd2)2 + ni2     p = ni2n
(1.5)
n, p electron and hole concentrations (cm−3) · Nd donor concentration (cm−3), all ionized · ni intrinsic concentration at the temperature in question, from equation (1.2) (cm−3). Follows from n = p + Nd with np = ni2. It reduces to n = Nd when ni ≪ Nd and to n = ni when ni ≫ Nd; at ni = Nd it gives n = 1.62 Nd. Covers the extrinsic-to-intrinsic transition only; freeze-out needs the incomplete ionization of the donors as well. For p-type material exchange n and p and use Na. On the formula sheet as (1.5).

For 1015 cm−3, ni reaches Nd near 545 K. Beyond that point the material is intrinsic: the electron density follows ni, the hole density rises to meet it, and n-type and p-type regions converge toward the same composition. A junction then stops rectifying and a MOSFET can no longer be turned off. The conductivity keeps rising with temperature, as a semiconductor's does; what the device loses is control of the carrier density by doping. A more heavily doped region stays extrinsic to higher temperature, because ni must climb further to overtake it.

A narrower gap moves the whole curve down in temperature. Germanium's gap is 0.66 eV and its ni at 300 K is about 2 × 1013 cm−3, three orders of magnitude above silicon's; at the same 1015 cm−3 its ni reaches Nd near 390 K rather than 545 K. Deck 8 p. 12 plots germanium at several compensating acceptor densities; that example, with the rest of the temperature dependence of devices, belongs to M17.

5 · How carriers move

A carrier in a crystal does not accelerate freely. It is scattered by lattice vibrations and by ionized dopant atoms, so under a steady electric field it reaches an average drift velocity proportional to the field. The constant of proportionality is the mobility, µ.

vd = µℰ      J = q(nµn + pµp)ℰ
(1.6)
vd drift velocity (cm/s) · µ mobility (cm²/V·s) · ℰ electric field (V/cm, not V/m — this course and the semiconductor literature work in centimetres throughout) · J current density (A/cm²) · q = 1.602 × 10−19 C. In lightly doped silicon at 300 K, µn ≈ 1,450 cm²/V·s and µp ≈ 505 cm²/V·s (Sze & Ng). Tabulations differ by up to 10 % — 1,350 to 1,500 and 450 to 505 — so name the source whenever a numerical answer turns on it. Both fall as doping rises, because ionized dopants scatter carriers, and both fall as temperature rises across the range this course uses, because lattice vibrations scatter them harder. In doped material the trend reverses below roughly 100 K, where ionized-impurity scattering takes over and mobility rises with temperature instead. Equation (1.6) fails at high field, where velocity saturates near 107 cm/s in silicon. On the formula sheet as (1.6).

Carriers also move down a concentration gradient by random thermal motion, which is diffusion. Both mechanisms operate at once, and both matter here: when a particle strike deposits a dense column of charge, the carriers within the junction's electric field are swept out by drift within picoseconds, while those outside it arrive later by diffusion. That two-stage response is visible in the current transient of M15 and was shown in Lecture 4 as a fast spike followed by a slow tail.

The electron mobility being nearly three times the hole mobility is why n-channel devices are faster than p-channel ones at the same size, and why silicon strained by embedded silicon–germanium is used in production: the strain distorts the band structure and raises the mobility, which Lecture 2 identified as one of the steps that kept scaling going.

A band diagram plots electron energy against position, and an electric field appears in it as a slope. Deck 8 p. 13 reduces the relation to a rule of thumb: the field points from high potential to low, and electron energy is low where the potential is high. Every band diagram in the rest of this module, and the whole MOS analysis of M14, is read with that rule.

The rule follows from the sign of the electron's charge. An electron at a point where the electrostatic potential is V has potential energy −qV, so raising the potential of a region lowers every electron energy in it by the same amount: Ec, Ei and Ev move down together and the gap between them is unchanged. A uniform field, which is a potential falling linearly with distance, therefore tilts the whole diagram upward in the direction the field points. A conduction electron moves down the slope of Ec, toward high potential and against the field. A hole moves up the slope of Ev, because hole energy increases downward on an electron-energy diagram, and so travels with the field.

ℰ = −dVdx = 1q dEidx      E = −qV
(1.7)
ℰ electric field (V/cm) · V electrostatic potential (V) · x position (cm) · Ei intrinsic level, or equally Ec or Ev, which move in parallel (J, or eV) · q elementary charge, 1.602 × 10−19 C · E potential energy of an electron at potential V (J, or eV). With energies in eV, (1/q) dEi/dx is simply the slope in eV/cm read as V/cm: bands that rise 1 eV across 1 µm hold a field of 104 V/cm. The field points up the slope of the bands. Applies wherever the electrostatic potential varies — across a depletion region, through a gate oxide, along a channel. On the formula sheet as (1.7).
Reading any band diagram

A positive voltage applied to a region brings its bands down by q times that voltage; a negative voltage raises them. The field points from high voltage to low, which is up the slope of the bands. Electrons roll down the slope of Ec; holes rise along Ev. The MOS capacitor of §7, the barrier picture of the MOSFET in §8 and the transport of radiation-generated charge through the gate oxide in M14 all follow from these three statements.

6 · The p–n junction

Joining p-type and n-type silicon within a single crystal produces the structure on which almost every device is built, and the one that concerns this course most directly. Electrons diffuse from the n-side into the p-side and holes the other way. Each departure leaves behind an ionized dopant atom that cannot move — negative acceptors on the p-side, positive donors on the n-side. Those fixed charges build an electric field that opposes further diffusion, and the system settles where drift and diffusion cancel exactly.

The region stripped of mobile carriers is the depletion region, and the potential step across it is the built-in potential.

Vbi = kTq ln NaNdni2      W = √2εs(Vbi + VR)q (1Na + 1Nd)
(1.8)
Vbi built-in potential (V) · kT/q thermal voltage, 0.02585 V at 300 K · W depletion width (cm) · VR applied reverse bias (V), taken positive · εs permittivity of silicon = 11.7 × 8.854 × 10−14 = 1.036 × 10−12 F/cm · Na, Nd in cm−3. For Na = Nd = 1016 cm−3, Vbi = 0.71 V and W = 0.43 µm unbiased, widening to 1.22 µm at 5 V reverse. The width grows as the square root of (Vbi + VR), tending to √VR only once the reverse bias is large compared with the built-in potential, so a reverse-biased junction presents a larger sensitive volume to radiation than a forward-biased one — a fact used in M15. On the formula sheet as (1.8).

Applying forward bias — positive to the p-side — opposes the built-in field, narrows the depletion region and allows majority carriers to cross in large numbers, so current rises exponentially. Applying reverse bias does the opposite: it widens the region, deepens the potential step, and leaves only a small current carried by minority carriers that happen to be generated within a diffusion length of the depletion region and are then swept across it.

I = I0 [ exp (qVnkT) − 1 ]
(1.9)

For V more negative than about −0.1 V the exponential vanishes and I → −I0: the current saturates and stops depending on voltage. I0 is proportional to ni2, so it carries twice the temperature sensitivity of equation (1.2) and doubles roughly every 5 K. The familiar rule that leakage doubles every 10 °C belongs to the depletion-region generation current, which is proportional to ni rather than its square and is the term that dominates measured silicon reverse current near 300 K. On the formula sheet as (1.9).

The reverse-biased junction is the structure to keep in mind for the rest of the course. It is designed to pass almost no current, it contains a strong field across a volume of order a cubic micrometre, and it collects any charge generated in that volume. A photodiode exploits this deliberately. A digital circuit does not: every node in a CMOS chip sits on a reverse-biased junction to the substrate, and every one of them is a charge collector whether or not that was intended. Lecture 5 made the consequence explicit — under sufficient carrier generation, a reverse-biased diode stops behaving as a diode and conducts like a resistor.

7 · The MOS capacitor

A MOSFET is built around a capacitor: a conducting gate, a thin layer of insulating silicon dioxide, and the silicon body beneath. Deck 8 cuts the transistor vertically through the gate and reads that stack as a band diagram before considering current at all. Where the gate voltage bends the bands, and how far, decides whether the silicon surface holds holes, nothing, or electrons, and the gate voltage at which it first holds electrons in quantity is the threshold voltage. The same diagram, with charge added to the oxide, is where M14 begins.

In the capacitor of Deck 8 p. 16 the gate is n⁺ polysilicon, doped so heavily that it is degenerate and its Fermi level coincides with its conduction band edge. The body is p-type silicon. The oxide between them has a gap of 8.9 eV, which the figure rounds to 9 eV, and two band offsets fix where its bands sit relative to silicon's. The oxide conduction band lies 3.1 eV above silicon's (3.1–3.2 eV in photoemission measurements), and the oxide valence band lies about 4.4 eV below silicon's (4.35–4.55 eV in photoemission measurements). The offsets and the gap come from different measurements and do not sum exactly. Each offset is a barrier: an electron in silicon needs 3.1 eV to enter the oxide, a hole about 4.4 eV. Neither is crossed thermally when kT is 25.85 meV, which is what makes the oxide an insulator in operation and makes charge placed inside it stay there.

With no voltage applied, gate and body are connected through the external circuit and share one Fermi level. In isolation the gate's Fermi level would sit at its conduction band edge and the body's near its valence band edge, about 1 eV lower. Bringing the two levels into line requires a potential difference between gate and body equal to the difference of their work functions — the energy needed to remove an electron from the Fermi level to the vacuum. That difference, φms, appears partly across the oxide, which tilts, and partly in the silicon, whose bands bend down toward the surface. The zero-bias diagram is therefore already bent.

The gate voltage that exactly cancels this built-in difference leaves the silicon bands flat all the way to the oxide. It is the flat-band voltage, and any fixed charge in the oxide adds to it, because that charge induces an opposite charge in the silicon that the gate must remove.

On the formula sheet as (1.10).

Sweeping the gate voltage carries the capacitor through four regimes, each identified by the charge the silicon surface holds (Deck 8 p. 18). The rule of §5 does all the work: a gate voltage above VFB lowers the surface bands, one below it raises them, and EF in the silicon stays flat because no current crosses the oxide.

At flat band, VG = VFB, the silicon is neutral up to the interface. In accumulation, VG < VFB, the surface bands rise, Ev approaches EF there, and holes — the majority carriers — gather in a thin sheet at the interface to balance negative charge on the gate. In depletion, VFB < VG < Vth, the surface bands fall, holes are pushed away, and the uncovered acceptor ions form a negative space charge of width W that balances the positive gate. Once the surface bending reaches φF, Ei at the surface falls below EF and by equation (1.4) the surface electron density exceeds the hole density: the surface is weakly inverted, still below threshold. In strong inversion, VG > Vth, the bending reaches 2φF and the surface holds a thin n-type layer as dense in electrons as the bulk is in holes. Because that electron density grows exponentially with further band bending, additional gate charge is balanced by electrons in the layer rather than by a wider depletion region, and W stops growing at its maximum. Deck 8 draws the four cases for an ideal capacitor; with an n⁺ polysilicon gate on p-type silicon VFB is about −1 V, so accumulation requires a negative gate voltage and depletion already exists at zero.

Deck 8 states the onset of inversion in words: the surface is inverted once Ei there lies as far below EF as it lies above EF in the bulk. The bulk separation is qφF (equation (1.4) and the p-type panel of the EF figure in §3), so the band bending at the surface must then be 2φF, and the surface electron density equals the bulk hole density Na. The gate voltage needed to reach that condition is the threshold voltage.

ψs = 2φF      Vth = VFB + 2φF + √2εsqNa · 2φFCox
(1.11)
ψs surface potential, the band bending between the neutral bulk and the surface divided by q (V), positive when the bands bend down · φF bulk Fermi potential from (1.4) (V) · Vth threshold voltage (V) · VFB from (1.10) (V) · εs permittivity of silicon, 1.036 × 10−12 F/cm · q = 1.602 × 10−19 C · Na body doping (cm−3) · Cox from (1.10) (F/cm²). The square root is the depletion charge per unit area at threshold (C/cm²), which the gate must support before any inversion charge appears. The three terms are the voltage that flattens the bands, the voltage that bends them to inversion, and the voltage dropped across the oxide. For Na = 1017 cm−3, 10 nm of oxide, an n⁺-polysilicon gate and no oxide charge: 2φF = 0.83 V, the oxide term is 0.48 V, VFB = −0.98 V, so Vth ≈ 0.34 V. Long channel, uniform doping. On the formula sheet as (1.11).

Equations (1.10) and (1.11) together say what oxide charge does to a transistor: a charge Qox shifts VFB, and therefore Vth, by −Qox/Cox. Ionizing radiation creates electron–hole pairs in the oxide; under positive gate bias the holes drift toward the silicon interface, and those that are trapped there add positive Qox. M14 follows that sequence on this diagram. The full threshold-voltage derivation, including the signs for each device type and threshold adjustment by implantation, is in EEE 6338 M03.

8 · The MOSFET

The metal–oxide–semiconductor field-effect transistor is the switch that digital electronics is built from, and it is what Lecture 2's scaling discussion is about. Its structure is four elements: a lightly doped substrate, two heavily doped regions called source and drain, a thin insulating layer of silicon dioxide over the substrate between them, and a conducting gate on top of that oxide. Beneath the gate it is the capacitor of §7; the source and drain add a supply and a sink of electrons at either end of the surface that the gate controls.

Consider an n-channel device: an n-type source and drain in a p-type substrate. With the gate grounded, the path from source to drain crosses two p–n junctions back to back, one of which is always reverse biased, so no current flows. Raising the gate voltage takes the surface beneath the oxide through depletion into inversion, as §7 showed. Above the threshold voltage Vth of equation (1.11) the surface holds enough electrons to be locally n-type — it has been inverted — and this inversion layer is a continuous conducting channel from source to drain, supplied with electrons by the source rather than by slow thermal generation. The transistor is on.

The capacitor picture says when a channel exists; it does not show how current crosses it. For that Deck 8 turns the cut through 90° and follows the silicon surface from source to drain (pp. 19–23). Drawn as three separate pieces, the n⁺ source, the p-type region under the gate and the n⁺ drain have their Fermi levels in different places: at or above Ec in the source and drain, near Ev in the p-type region. Joined into one crystal in equilibrium they share a single Fermi level, since a gradient in EF would drive a current with nothing to sustain it. The bands of the region under the gate therefore sit higher than those of the source and drain by the built-in potential of the n⁺/p junctions, and the conduction band edge becomes a function of position along the channel.

Ec(x) = Ec0 − qψ(x)      Ev(x) = Ev0 − qψ(x)
(1.12)
Ec(x), Ev(x) band edges at position x along the surface from source to drain (eV) · Ec0, Ev0 their values where ψ = 0 (eV) · ψ(x) electrostatic potential at the surface at x (V) · q elementary charge; with energies in eV, qψ in eV equals ψ in volts. This is E = −qV of equation (1.7) applied point by point. Deck 8 labels ψ(x) the built-in potential: with no voltage applied that is what it is, the potential step of the source and drain junctions, and under bias ψ(x) also carries the gate and drain voltages. On the formula sheet as (1.12).

The result is a smooth energy barrier that rises from the source into the channel and falls again at the drain (Deck 8 p. 23). Electrons in the source sit at its foot. How far its top lies above the source Fermi level decides how many of them have the energy to cross, and both the gate and the drain act on it through the rule of §5: a positive voltage brings Ec down (Deck 8 pp. 24–29).

In (a), with the gate low and no drain voltage, the barrier top lies far above the source Fermi level and almost no source electron can reach it. In (b) a positive gate voltage raises the electrostatic potential of the channel and lowers the barrier. Electrons from both source and drain can now enter the channel, but with VDS = 0 the two flows are equal and opposite and ID = 0. In (c) a drain voltage lowers the drain end of the diagram by qVDS and tilts the channel. Electrons that cross the barrier from the source now descend toward the drain, while electrons from the drain face a barrier higher by qVDS, so a net current flows: small and proportional to VDS at low drain voltage, large once the gate holds the barrier low and the drain voltage is high, which is the on-state. In (d) the gate is low and the drain voltage high. The drain end is pulled down, but in a well-designed transistor the top of the barrier near the source stays where it was, the source electrons still cannot cross it, and the device stays off. Turning the transistor on takes both voltages: the gate to lower the barrier and the drain to collect what crosses it.

The current over the barrier is set by the same Boltzmann tail as every carrier population in this module. An electron from the source crosses if it arrives at the top of the barrier with enough energy, and the fraction that does falls by a factor e for every kT of barrier height. Crossing a barrier by thermal energy in this way is thermionic emission.

ID ∝ exp (−EBkT)
(1.13)
ID drain current (A) · EB barrier height, Ec at the top of the source-end barrier minus EF in the source (eV) · kT in eV, 0.02585 eV at 300 K. Lowering EB by kT ln 10 = 59.5 meV at 300 K multiplies the current by ten. Holds while the barrier top lies more than a few kT above the source Fermi level: the off-state and the subthreshold region. Above threshold the gate pulls the barrier top down toward the source Fermi level, and the current is limited instead by the channel charge and its velocity, as equation (1.14) describes. On the formula sheet as (1.13).

Two consequences follow. The off-state leakage is set by the height of the barrier the gate leaves in place, so anything that lowers that barrier — a hotter crystal, positive charge in the gate oxide, a drain reaching through a short channel — raises Ioff exponentially. And the on-state works by the same mechanism: an electron crosses the low barrier at the source end by thermal energy and then descends the steep drain-side slope, where nothing remains to cross. Quantum-mechanical tunnelling from source to drain through the barrier becomes significant only for channels well below 10 nm, and there it adds to the off-state leakage.

Two properties of this structure set everything in M02. The first is the current the device delivers when on.

ID,sat = µnCox2 · WL · (VGS − Vth)2      Cox = εoxtox
(1.14)
ID,sat saturated drain current (A) · µn channel electron mobility (cm²/V·s), lower than bulk because of surface scattering · Cox gate oxide capacitance per unit area (F/cm²) · εox permittivity of SiO₂ = 3.9 × 8.854 × 10−14 = 3.45 × 10−13 F/cm · tox oxide thickness (cm) · W, L channel width and gate length, entering only as their ratio · VGS gate–source voltage (V). Valid in saturation, VDS > VGS − Vth, and for long channels; short devices approach a linear rather than square-law dependence because carrier velocity saturates. On the formula sheet as (1.14).

Equation (1.14) explains the whole geometric history of the transistor. Current scales with W/L, so widening the channel or shortening the gate delivers more drive. It scales with Cox, so thinning the gate oxide does the same. And W is the perimeter of the channel, not a planar width — which is why folding the silicon into a fin, and then wrapping the gate all the way around it, increases the current a device of a given footprint can pass. That argument is the spine of M02 §3.

Deck 8 p. 30 shows what this produces at the terminals: the measured drain characteristics of a 2007 n-channel MOSFET with a 100 nm gate, drain current per micrometre of width against VDS for a series of gate voltages, reaching above 1,100 µA/µm at 1.2 V. At small VDS the channel acts as a resistor whose conductance the gate sets, and ID rises linearly with VDS: the linear region. At larger VDS the current levels off: the saturation region. The drain has pulled its end of the barrier far below the source end, and further drain voltage no longer changes how many electrons cross at the source. In the long-channel square law saturation begins at VDS,sat = VGS − Vth. In the 100 nm device the current saturates earlier, because carrier velocity approaches its limit near 107 cm/s, and keeps rising slowly beyond saturation, because the drain still lowers the barrier slightly.

The insets on Deck 8 p. 30 come from a different device

The two insets on Deck 8 p. 30 plot electron energy against position at VDS = 0.05 V and 1.0 V as the gate voltage steps up. Their position axis runs from −15 to +15 nm and their barrier is roughly 10–15 nm long: they are simulated conduction-band profiles of a transistor about a tenth the length of the 100 nm device whose measured curves they sit beside. They show the mechanism correctly — the gate lowering the barrier, the drain pulling down its end. They are not the band profile of the 100 nm transistor, whose barrier would extend over most of its 100 nm channel.

The second property is what happens below threshold. A mechanical switch carries no current when open. A MOSFET does. Below Vth the channel is not absent but merely sparse, and its carrier population falls exponentially rather than abruptly, because it is set by a thermal barrier. Equation (1.13) makes this quantitative. The gate moves the barrier through the surface potential, but only by a fraction of its own voltage change: the gate voltage divides between the oxide capacitance and the depletion capacitance Cd beneath it, which act in series, so ψs changes by ΔVG/(1 + Cd/Cox). One decade of current therefore costs (1 + Cd/Cox)(kT/q) ln 10 of gate voltage.

S = kTq · ln 10 · (1 + CdCox) ≥ 60 mV/decade at 300 K
(1.15)
S subthreshold swing — the gate-voltage change needed to alter drain current by one decade (mV/decade) · Cd depletion capacitance per unit area beneath the channel (F/cm²) · Cox from (1.10). The bracket is ≥ 1, so S cannot fall below (kT/q)·ln10 = 59.5 mV/decade at 300 K in any conventional MOSFET. That floor is set by thermal statistics, not by fabrication, and it is the reason supply voltages stopped falling. Note the explicit T: the floor drops to 19.8 mV/decade at 100 K and rises to 99.2 mV/decade at 500 K, before the body factor is applied. On the formula sheet as (1.15).

The off-state current Ioff is small but finite, and a modern chip contains 1010 to 1011 transistors, so the total static power it dissipates is not negligible. Lecture 2 identified this as one of the limits that ended frequency scaling, and the comparison it showed between a planar device and a tri-gate device at equal on-current was a comparison of exactly this quantity.

Panel (d) of the barrier figure shows a drain that leaves the source end of the barrier alone. In a short channel it does not: the drain's field reaches through the channel and lowers the top of the barrier as well, so the off-state current rises with drain voltage and the threshold voltage falls. This is drain-induced barrier lowering, DIBL, quantified as the threshold shift per volt of drain bias.

DIBL = −ΔVthΔVDS
(1.16)
DIBL drain-induced barrier lowering (mV/V) · ΔVth change in threshold voltage (mV) · ΔVDS change in drain–source voltage over which it is measured (V), typically from about 0.05 V to the supply voltage. Positive when the threshold falls as VDS rises. On the logarithmic transfer characteristic it appears as a shift of the subthreshold line toward lower gate voltage at high drain bias, which multiplies Ioff by 10DIBL·ΔVDS/S. Intel's 22 nm tri-gate transistors reported about 50 mV/V, with S near 70 mV/decade. On the formula sheet as (1.16).
Where equations (1.14) and (1.15) stop applying

Both are long-channel results. In a device with a gate length of tens of nanometres, the drain's field reaches through the channel and lowers the source barrier directly — the DIBL of equation (1.16) — so the gate loses some of its control: threshold voltage falls with gate length and with drain bias, and S rises above its thermal floor. These short-channel effects are the reason the planar transistor was abandoned for the fin and then the nanosheet, which surround the channel on three and four sides respectively and restore the gate's authority over it.

9 · What sets speed and power

A logic gate switches by charging or discharging the capacitance of the node it drives, using the current its transistors can supply. The time this takes follows directly.

td ≈ CLVDDID,sat      Pdyn = α CL VDD2 f
(1.17)
td gate delay (s) · CL load capacitance (F), the sum of gate, junction and interconnect capacitance · VDD supply voltage (V) · ID,sat from (1.14) (A) · Pdyn dynamic power (W) · α activity factor, the fraction of clock cycles on which the node actually switches, typically 0.1–0.2 · f clock frequency (Hz). Static power VDDIoff must be added separately and is not reduced by lowering f. On the formula sheet as (1.17).

The VDD2 in the power expression is why supply voltages were driven down for three decades, and the 60 mV/decade floor of equation (1.15) is why their fall stalled near 1 V in the mid-2000s (M02 §2) and has been slow since: below a few hundred millivolts of threshold, the threshold voltage cannot be reduced further without Ioff rising to meet Ion. With VDD fixed and f unable to rise without exceeding the power a package can remove, the remaining levers are capacitance — smaller cells, shorter wires — and parallelism. Lecture 2 traced exactly this sequence.

For space electronics the same expressions read differently. A part that runs at 100 MHz and 5 W rather than 5 GHz and 200 W is not primitive; it is a deliberate position on the curve described by equation (1.17), chosen because a spacecraft can generate a few watts and can remove heat only by radiating it. M03 takes that argument up.

A junction is reverse biased at 5 V and a passing particle deposits enough energy to create 106 electron–hole pairs inside its depletion region. Why does essentially all of that charge reach the terminals, when a similar number of pairs created deep in the neutral substrate largely does not?

The depletion region holds a field of order 105 V/cm across about a micrometre. Carriers created there are separated within picoseconds and swept to opposite terminals, far faster than they can recombine, so collection is essentially complete. In the neutral substrate there is no field: the pairs move only by diffusion, they are free to recombine while they do so, and only the fraction that reaches the depletion edge within a minority-carrier lifetime is collected. Collection from the neutral region is therefore partial, slower, and shows up as the diffusion tail on the current transient rather than the prompt spike.

An n-channel transistor sits in its off-state, VGS = 0 and VDS = 1 V, when a sheet of positive charge appears in its gate oxide at the silicon interface. Using the band diagram through the gate and the band diagram along the channel, explain why the off-state current rises, and why it rises exponentially rather than in proportion to the charge.

Through the gate, the positive sheet acts as an extra positive gate voltage: by equation (1.10) it shifts VFB, and with it Vth, by −Qox/Cox, so the surface bands under the gate bend further down at the same applied voltage. Along the channel, the same lowering of Ec at the surface lowers the top of the source-end barrier. The off-state current is the thermionic current of equation (1.13), exponential in that barrier height, so it rises by a factor of ten for every subthreshold swing S of threshold shift: a shift of 0.1 V at S = 80 mV/decade multiplies Ioff by about 18. The charge enters the barrier height linearly, and the barrier height enters the current exponentially.

Summary

  • Atoms brought into a crystal split their discrete levels into continuous bands separated by a forbidden gap, which opens at the zone boundary of any periodic structure: the perforated beam of Deck 8 passes 17.8 MHz and stops 25.5 MHz by the same splitting. Silicon's gap is 1.12 eV, rounded to 1.1 eV in this course; SiO₂'s is 8.9 eV.
  • Energy exceeding the gap promotes an electron and leaves a hole. Both are mobile and both carry current. Thermal energy, light and ionizing particles all do this by the same mechanism.
  • States are filled according to the Fermi–Dirac function; EF is the energy of half occupancy, and each band's population is set by the Boltzmann tail, n = ni exp[(EF − Ei)/kT]. ni ≈ 1.0 × 1010 cm−3 in silicon at 300 K and depends exponentially on Eg/kT.
  • Doping fixes the carrier population by design, at a cost of 45 meV per carrier rather than 1.12 eV. Phosphorus, arsenic and boron are used because they are shallow, dissolve to beyond 1020 cm−3 and stay in place. np = ni2 holds in equilibrium only, and the minority population is the sensitive one.
  • Doping controls the population only between freeze-out and the intrinsic onset: for 1015 cm−3 of phosphorus, 90 % ionization at 92 K and ni = Nd near 545 K, against near 390 K in germanium.
  • A positive voltage lowers electron energy, E = −qV. The field points from high to low potential, up the slope of the bands; electrons roll down Ec, holes rise along Ev.
  • A reverse-biased p–n junction holds a strong field across a carrier-free volume and collects any charge generated inside it. It is simultaneously the working principle of every photodetector and the vulnerability of every circuit node.
  • A MOS capacitor on p-type silicon passes from accumulation through depletion to inversion as the gate voltage rises; inversion begins at ψs = 2φF, and oxide charge shifts VFB and Vth by −Qox/Cox. The oxide's band offsets, 3.1 eV and about 4.4 eV, keep carriers out of it and trapped charge in it.
  • A MOSFET is a barrier-controlled device: the gate sets the height of the barrier at the source, the drain collects what crosses it, and the current over the barrier is thermionic. Hence off-state current is exponential in gate voltage and cannot fall faster than 59.5 mV/decade at 300 K, and anything that lowers the barrier — temperature, oxide charge, DIBL — raises it exponentially. Drive current scales with W/L and Cox.
  • Delay is CV/I and dynamic power is αCV2f. Those two, plus the leakage floor, define the design space that both commercial and space electronics sit in.
