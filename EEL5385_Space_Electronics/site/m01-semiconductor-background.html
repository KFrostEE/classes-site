Module 01 · background

Semiconductors and Devices: the Working Background

The solid-state physics and device behaviour that the rest of the course assumes: energy bands, carriers, doping, the p–n junction, and the MOSFET.

Prerequisites none Read time ~40 min Sources Decks 2, 5; prerequisite EEE 3350

Every effect this course studies is ultimately a change to one of a small number of quantities inside a semiconductor device: how many mobile charges there are, where they are, how fast they move, and how long they survive. Radiation creates charge where there should be none and defects where the lattice should be perfect. Temperature changes how many carriers exist and how easily they move. Packaging changes the mechanical and thermal boundary conditions those devices sit inside. None of that can be reasoned about without a working picture of the device itself.

This module supplies that picture. It is background rather than course content: the syllabus lists EEE 3350 Semiconductor Devices as a prerequisite, and the lectures assume bands, junctions and transistor operation from the second lecture onward while promising a refresher that has not yet been delivered. Read it if that material is not fresh; skip to M02 if it is. Two things here are load-bearing later and are treated in more detail than a first course would: the reverse-biased p–n junction, which is the object that collects radiation-generated charge in M08 and M15, and off-state leakage, which is the quantity that both radiation and temperature attack.

1 · From atomic levels to energy bands

An isolated atom holds its electrons in discrete energy levels. An electron may occupy one of those levels or another, and nothing between: absorbing exactly the energy difference moves it up, and falling back emits a photon of exactly that energy. This is why an isolated atom of hydrogen, helium or mercury emits a line spectrum — a set of sharp wavelengths characteristic of that element rather than a continuous band of colour.

Bringing two identical atoms close enough that their electron wavefunctions overlap changes this. The Pauli exclusion principle forbids two electrons in the same quantum state, so each shared level must split into two levels of slightly different energy. Four atoms split it into four. A crystal of silicon holds about 5.0 × 1022 atoms per cubic centimetre, so every atomic level splits into of order 1023 levels spaced so finely that they are indistinguishable from a continuous range of allowed energies. That range is an energy band.

Between the bands lies a range of energies with no allowed states, called the bandgap, Eg. The gap is a consequence of the crystal's periodicity. An electron in a periodic potential behaves as a wave, and a wave whose wavelength is twice the lattice spacing, λ = 2a, meets the Bragg condition and is reflected by every atomic plane at once. Incident and reflected waves superpose into two standing waves of the same wavevector: one piles electron density onto the ion cores, the other between them. Those two arrangements have different potential energies, and that difference is the gap.

Two bands matter. The valence band is the highest band that is full of electrons at absolute zero; its electrons are bound into chemical bonds and carry no net current, because for every electron moving one way there is another moving the opposite way. The conduction band is the lowest empty band; an electron placed in it has empty states immediately above it in energy, so an applied electric field can accelerate it and it carries current. The size of the gap between them separates the three classes of solid.

Silicon at 300 K

Diamond cubic lattice, constant 5.431 Å, 5.0 × 1022 atoms/cm³. Eg = 1.12 eV at 300 K, rising to 1.17 eV at 0 K: the gap narrows monotonically as temperature increases. Relative permittivity 11.7 — Sze & Ng use 11.9 and Pierret 11.8, which moves every depletion width below by under 1 %. The lectures round the gap to 1.1 eV and that value is used in the course's arithmetic.

2 · Electrons, holes, and how many there are

At any temperature above absolute zero, lattice vibrations supply energy in a statistical spread, and a small fraction of valence electrons receive more than Eg and are promoted into the conduction band. Each promotion leaves an empty state in the valence band. Because the valence band is otherwise full, a neighbouring electron can move into that empty state, leaving a new empty state behind it. The vacancy propagates through the crystal and responds to an electric field as though it were a particle of charge +q. That quasiparticle is a hole. Within the semiclassical band model it carries exactly the net current and crystal momentum of all the remaining valence electrons, which is what makes it useful; it describes that collective motion rather than any real particle, and silicon in fact has heavy-hole, light-hole and split-off bands whose differing effective masses the single symbol averages over.

Promotion therefore always produces a pair, and this is the single most important fact for everything that follows: ionizing radiation deposits energy in a semiconductor and the energy appears as electron–hole pairs. The mechanism is identical to thermal generation; only the energy source differs.

In a pure crystal the two populations are equal, and their common value is the intrinsic carrier concentration, ni. Its magnitude follows from the exponential tail of the thermal energy distribution.

ni = √NcNv · exp (−Eg2kT)
(1.1)
ni intrinsic carrier concentration (cm−3) · Nc effective density of states in the conduction band, 2.86 × 1019 cm−3 for silicon at 300 K · Nv the same for the valence band, 3.10 × 1019 cm−3 · Eg bandgap, 1.124 eV in the set these two belong to (eV) · k Boltzmann constant, use the eV form, 8.617 × 10−5 eV/K, so that kT = 0.02585 eV at 300 K · T absolute temperature (K). Take Nc, Nv and Eg from one self-consistent set: mixing tabulations is the commonest way to get a wrong answer from this expression. With the values above it returns 1.08 × 1010 cm−3. The accepted measured value at 300 K is 1.00 × 1010 cm−3, older textbooks carry 1.45 × 1010, and this course uses 1.0 × 1010 throughout. The distance between the model and the measurement is the effective-mass approximation, not an arithmetic slip. On the formula sheet as (1.1).

Two features of equation (1.1) do most of the work in this course. First, the prefactor is around 1019 cm−3 and the answer is 1010 cm−3, so the exponential suppresses the carrier population by nine orders of magnitude: only about one silicon atom in 5 × 1012 contributes a conduction electron. Second, that exponential contains Eg/kT, so ni roughly doubles for every 10 K rise near room temperature and climbs by orders of magnitude over the temperature range a spacecraft sees. This single dependence is why silicon fails at high temperature and why wide-bandgap materials replace it, which is the subject of M17.

3 · Doping

An intrinsic crystal is a poor conductor and, worse, its conductivity is set entirely by temperature. Devices need carrier populations that are fixed by design. That is achieved by doping — substituting a small fraction of the silicon atoms with atoms from a neighbouring column of the periodic table.

Silicon has four valence electrons and forms four covalent bonds. A phosphorus atom has five. Substituted onto a lattice site it satisfies the four bonds and has one electron left over, bound to the phosphorus ion so weakly that 45 meV releases it — less than twice kT at room temperature, so essentially every such atom is ionized at 300 K. In band terms, phosphorus introduces an allowed level 45 meV below the conduction band edge, and its electron is promoted from there rather than from across the full gap. Phosphorus, arsenic and antimony are donors, and the result is n-type silicon.

Boron has three valence electrons. Substituted onto a lattice site it leaves one bond unsatisfied, and an electron from a neighbouring bond fills it at a cost of 45 meV, leaving a mobile hole behind. Boron introduces a level just above the valence band edge and is an acceptor; the result is p-type silicon.

Typical doping densities run from 1015 cm−3 in a lightly doped substrate to 1020 cm−3 in a contact region. Even the highest of those replaces only one silicon atom in five hundred, yet it raises the electron population ten orders of magnitude above intrinsic. Doping is a controlled introduction of foreign atoms into an otherwise perfect lattice — the same class of perturbation that displacement damage produces, differing in that the dopant sits substitutionally on a lattice site and produces a shallow level, while a radiation defect generally does neither.

In doped material the two carrier populations are no longer equal, but their product is fixed at a given temperature.

n p = ni2     n ≈ Nd  (n-type)    p ≈ Na  (p-type)
(1.2)
n, p electron and hole concentrations (cm−3) · Nd, Na donor and acceptor concentrations (cm−3). Valid in thermal equilibrium only, with the dopants fully ionized and Nd or Na ≫ ni. It does not hold under illumination, under bias, or while radiation-generated carriers are present — those are exactly the non-equilibrium conditions the rest of the course studies. In n-type silicon doped 1016 cm−3, p = 1020/1016 = 104 cm−3: the minority population is twelve orders of magnitude below the majority, which is why a small injected charge changes it completely. On the formula sheet as (1.2).
Minority carriers are the sensitive quantity

A device is disturbed when a carrier population changes appreciably. Adding 1010 electrons per cubic centimetre to a region holding 1016 majority electrons changes nothing measurable. Adding the same 1010 holes to a region holding 104 of them changes the hole population by six orders of magnitude. Every radiation effect that appears as a current — junction leakage, single-event transients, solar-cell degradation — is a minority-carrier effect for this reason.

4 · How carriers move

A carrier in a crystal does not accelerate freely. It is scattered by lattice vibrations and by ionized dopant atoms, so under a steady electric field it reaches an average drift velocity proportional to the field. The constant of proportionality is the mobility, µ.

vd = µE      J = q(nµn + pµp)E
(1.3)
vd drift velocity (cm/s) · µ mobility (cm²/V·s) · E electric field (V/cm, not V/m — this course and the semiconductor literature work in centimetres throughout) · J current density (A/cm²) · q = 1.602 × 10−19 C. In lightly doped silicon at 300 K, µn ≈ 1,450 cm²/V·s and µp ≈ 505 cm²/V·s (Sze & Ng). Tabulations differ by up to 10 % — 1,350 to 1,500 and 450 to 505 — so name the source whenever a numerical answer turns on it. Both fall as doping rises, because ionized dopants scatter carriers, and both fall as temperature rises across the range this course uses, because lattice vibrations scatter them harder. In doped material the trend reverses below roughly 100 K, where ionized-impurity scattering takes over and mobility rises with temperature instead. Equation (1.3) fails at high field, where velocity saturates near 107 cm/s in silicon. On the formula sheet as (1.3).

Carriers also move down a concentration gradient by random thermal motion, which is diffusion. Both mechanisms operate at once, and both matter here: when a particle strike deposits a dense column of charge, the carriers within the junction's electric field are swept out by drift within picoseconds, while those outside it arrive later by diffusion. That two-stage response is visible in the current transient of M15 and was shown in Lecture 4 as a fast spike followed by a slow tail.

The electron mobility being nearly three times the hole mobility is why n-channel devices are faster than p-channel ones at the same size, and why silicon strained by embedded silicon–germanium is used in production: the strain distorts the band structure and raises the mobility, which Lecture 2 identified as one of the steps that kept scaling going.

5 · The p–n junction

Joining p-type and n-type silicon within a single crystal produces the structure on which almost every device is built, and the one that concerns this course most directly. Electrons diffuse from the n-side into the p-side and holes the other way. Each departure leaves behind an ionized dopant atom that cannot move — negative acceptors on the p-side, positive donors on the n-side. Those fixed charges build an electric field that opposes further diffusion, and the system settles where drift and diffusion cancel exactly.

The region stripped of mobile carriers is the depletion region, and the potential step across it is the built-in potential.

Vbi = kTq ln NaNdni2      W = √2εs(Vbi + VR)q (1Na + 1Nd)
(1.4)
Vbi built-in potential (V) · kT/q thermal voltage, 0.02585 V at 300 K · W depletion width (cm) · VR applied reverse bias (V), taken positive · εs permittivity of silicon = 11.7 × 8.854 × 10−14 = 1.036 × 10−12 F/cm · Na, Nd in cm−3. For Na = Nd = 1016 cm−3, Vbi = 0.71 V and W = 0.43 µm unbiased, widening to 1.22 µm at 5 V reverse. The width grows as the square root of (Vbi + VR), tending to √VR only once the reverse bias is large compared with the built-in potential, so a reverse-biased junction presents a larger sensitive volume to radiation than a forward-biased one — a fact used in M15. On the formula sheet as (1.4).

Applying forward bias — positive to the p-side — opposes the built-in field, narrows the depletion region and allows majority carriers to cross in large numbers, so current rises exponentially. Applying reverse bias does the opposite: it widens the region, deepens the potential step, and leaves only a small current carried by minority carriers that happen to be generated within a diffusion length of the depletion region and are then swept across it.

I = I0 [ exp (qVnkT) − 1 ]
(1.5)
I diode current (A), positive in forward bias · I0 ideal diffusion saturation current (A), 10−12 to 10−15 A for a small silicon junction — the reverse current actually measured is larger, because generation inside the depletion region dominates it in silicon at and below room temperature · V applied voltage (V), negative for reverse bias · n ideality factor, 1 for pure diffusion current and approaching 2 when generation inside the depletion region dominates · kT/q = 0.02585 V at 300 K. For V more negative than about −0.1 V the exponential vanishes and I → −I0: the current saturates and stops depending on voltage. I0 is proportional to ni2, so it carries twice the temperature sensitivity of equation (1.1) and doubles roughly every 5 K. The familiar rule that leakage doubles every 10 °C belongs to the depletion-region generation current, which is proportional to ni rather than its square and is the term that dominates measured silicon reverse current near 300 K. On the formula sheet as (1.5).

The reverse-biased junction is the structure to keep in mind for the rest of the course. It is designed to pass almost no current, it contains a strong field across a volume of order a cubic micrometre, and it collects any charge generated in that volume. A photodiode exploits this deliberately. A digital circuit does not: every node in a CMOS chip sits on a reverse-biased junction to the substrate, and every one of them is a charge collector whether or not that was intended. Lecture 5 made the consequence explicit — under sufficient carrier generation, a reverse-biased diode stops behaving as a diode and conducts like a resistor.

6 · The MOSFET

The metal–oxide–semiconductor field-effect transistor is the switch that digital electronics is built from, and it is what Lecture 2's scaling discussion is about. Its structure is four elements: a lightly doped substrate, two heavily doped regions called source and drain, a thin insulating layer of silicon dioxide over the substrate between them, and a conducting gate on top of that oxide.

Consider an n-channel device: an n-type source and drain in a p-type substrate. With the gate grounded, the path from source to drain crosses two p–n junctions back to back, one of which is always reverse biased, so no current flows. Raising the gate voltage places positive charge on the gate, which repels holes from the silicon surface beneath the oxide and then attracts electrons to it. Above a certain gate voltage, the threshold voltage Vth, the surface holds enough electrons to be locally n-type — it has been inverted — and a continuous conducting channel connects source to drain. The transistor is on.

Two properties of this structure set everything in M02. The first is the current the device delivers when on.

ID,sat = µnCox2 · WL · (VGS − Vth)2      Cox = εoxtox
(1.6)
ID,sat saturated drain current (A) · µn channel electron mobility (cm²/V·s), lower than bulk because of surface scattering · Cox gate oxide capacitance per unit area (F/cm²) · εox permittivity of SiO₂ = 3.9 × 8.854 × 10−14 = 3.45 × 10−13 F/cm · tox oxide thickness (cm) · W, L channel width and gate length, entering only as their ratio · VGS gate–source voltage (V). Valid in saturation, VDS > VGS − Vth, and for long channels; short devices approach a linear rather than square-law dependence because carrier velocity saturates. On the formula sheet as (1.6).

Equation (1.6) explains the whole geometric history of the transistor. Current scales with W/L, so widening the channel or shortening the gate delivers more drive. It scales with Cox, so thinning the gate oxide does the same. And W is the perimeter of the channel, not a planar width — which is why folding the silicon into a fin, and then wrapping the gate all the way around it, increases the current a device of a given footprint can pass. That argument is the spine of M02 §3.

The second property is what happens below threshold. A mechanical switch carries no current when open. A MOSFET does. Below Vth the channel is not absent but merely sparse, and its carrier population falls exponentially rather than abruptly, because it is set by a thermal barrier.

S = kTq · ln 10 · (1 + CdCox) ≥ 60 mV/decade at 300 K
(1.7)
S subthreshold swing — the gate-voltage change needed to alter drain current by one decade (mV/decade) · Cd depletion capacitance per unit area beneath the channel (F/cm²) · Cox from (1.6). The bracket is ≥ 1, so S cannot fall below (kT/q)·ln10 = 59.5 mV/decade at 300 K in any conventional MOSFET. That floor is set by thermal statistics, not by fabrication, and it is the reason supply voltages stopped falling. Note the explicit T: the floor drops to 19.8 mV/decade at 100 K and rises to 99.2 mV/decade at 500 K, before the body factor is applied. On the formula sheet as (1.7).

The off-state current Ioff is small but finite, and a modern chip contains 1010 to 1011 transistors, so the total static power it dissipates is not negligible. Lecture 2 identified this as one of the limits that ended frequency scaling, and the comparison it showed between a planar device and a tri-gate device at equal on-current was a comparison of exactly this quantity.

Where equations (1.6) and (1.7) stop applying

Both are long-channel results. In a device with a gate length of tens of nanometres, the drain's field reaches through the channel and lowers the source barrier directly, so the gate loses some of its control: threshold voltage falls with gate length and with drain bias, and S rises above its thermal floor. These short-channel effects are the reason the planar transistor was abandoned for the fin and then the nanosheet, which surround the channel on three and four sides respectively and restore the gate's authority over it.

7 · What sets speed and power

A logic gate switches by charging or discharging the capacitance of the node it drives, using the current its transistors can supply. The time this takes follows directly.

td ≈ CLVDDID,sat      Pdyn = α CL VDD2 f
(1.8)
td gate delay (s) · CL load capacitance (F), the sum of gate, junction and interconnect capacitance · VDD supply voltage (V) · ID,sat from (1.6) (A) · Pdyn dynamic power (W) · α activity factor, the fraction of clock cycles on which the node actually switches, typically 0.1–0.2 · f clock frequency (Hz). Static power VDDIoff must be added separately and is not reduced by lowering f. On the formula sheet as (1.8).

The VDD2 in the power expression is why supply voltages were driven down for three decades, and the 60 mV/decade floor of equation (1.7) is why they stopped near 0.7 V: below that, the threshold voltage cannot be reduced further without Ioff rising to meet Ion. With VDD fixed and f unable to rise without exceeding the power a package can remove, the remaining levers are capacitance — smaller cells, shorter wires — and parallelism. Lecture 2 traced exactly this sequence.

For space electronics the same expressions read differently. A part that runs at 100 MHz and 5 W rather than 5 GHz and 200 W is not primitive; it is a deliberate position on the curve described by equation (1.8), chosen because a spacecraft can generate a few watts and can remove heat only by radiating it. M03 takes that argument up.

A junction is reverse biased at 5 V and a passing particle deposits enough energy to create 106 electron–hole pairs inside its depletion region. Why does essentially all of that charge reach the terminals, when a similar number of pairs created deep in the neutral substrate largely does not?

The depletion region holds a field of order 105 V/cm across about a micrometre. Carriers created there are separated within picoseconds and swept to opposite terminals, far faster than they can recombine, so collection is essentially complete. In the neutral substrate there is no field: the pairs move only by diffusion, they are free to recombine while they do so, and only the fraction that reaches the depletion edge within a minority-carrier lifetime is collected. Collection from the neutral region is therefore partial, slower, and shows up as the diffusion tail on the current transient rather than the prompt spike.

Summary

  • Atoms brought into a crystal split their discrete levels into continuous bands separated by a forbidden gap. Silicon's gap is 1.12 eV, rounded to 1.1 eV in this course; SiO₂'s is 8.9 eV.
  • Energy exceeding the gap promotes an electron and leaves a hole. Both are mobile and both carry current. Thermal energy, light and ionizing particles all do this by the same mechanism.
  • ni ≈ 1.0 × 1010 cm−3 in silicon at 300 K and depends exponentially on Eg/kT. This dependence governs the high-temperature limit of silicon.
  • Doping fixes the carrier population by design, at a cost of 45 meV per carrier rather than 1.12 eV. np = ni2 holds in equilibrium only, and the minority population is the sensitive one.
  • A reverse-biased p–n junction holds a strong field across a carrier-free volume and collects any charge generated inside it. It is simultaneously the working principle of every photodetector and the vulnerability of every circuit node.
  • A MOSFET conducts through an inversion channel at the oxide interface. Drive current scales with W/L and Cox; off-state current is exponential in gate voltage and cannot fall faster than 60 mV/decade at 300 K.
  • Delay is CV/I and dynamic power is αCV2f. Those two, plus the leakage floor, define the design space that both commercial and space electronics sit in.
