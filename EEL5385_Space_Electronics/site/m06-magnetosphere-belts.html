Module 06

Earth's Magnetosphere and the Trapped Belts

How a magnetic field captures charged particles and holds them for years, the three motions a trapped particle performs, and why the resulting belts contain protons on the inside and electrons on the outside.

Prerequisites M05 Read time ~45 min Sources Decks 3–5, 7; Lectures 3–5, 7

The populations described in M05 stream through interplanetary space at densities of a few particles per cubic centimetre. Inside Earth's magnetic field there are regions where the particle density is higher by many orders of magnitude, sustained continuously, and they are the single largest radiation hazard for any spacecraft operating near Earth. Earth's field produces no particles of its own. What it does is capture a small fraction of those passing through, and then prevent them from leaving for years.

This module builds that mechanism from one equation. The Lorentz force acting on a moving charge in a non-uniform field produces three superimposed periodic motions on three widely separated timescales, and the combination confines a particle to a shell that wraps around the planet. The structure of the resulting belts, their composition, their variation with solar activity, and the one place where they reach low altitude, all follow. The module closes on the two shields — the field itself and the atmosphere beneath it — that a spacecraft leaves behind on launch, which is the transition to M07.

1 · The geomagnetic field

Earth's magnetic field is generated by convective motion of liquid iron in the outer core, and to a first approximation it is a dipole: a field with two poles whose strength falls as the inverse cube of distance from the centre. At the surface the flux density is about 50 µT — 0.5 gauss — varying by roughly a factor of two between the equator and the poles.

Two departures from an ideal dipole matter for radiation. The dipole axis is tilted about 11° from the rotation axis, and its centre is displaced from the centre of the Earth — by about 500 km in the figures the course uses, and by closer to 590 km at the present epoch, since the offset has been growing at roughly 2.5 km per year. The displacement means the field is weaker on one side of the planet than the other at a given altitude, and §9 shows what that does.

The field does not extend indefinitely. The solar wind carries its own magnetic field outward and cannot cross Earth's, so the two press against each other and the geomagnetic field is confined to a cavity: compressed to about ten Earth radii on the sunward side and drawn out into a tail hundreds of Earth radii long on the night side. That cavity is the magnetosphere, and its sunward boundary, the magnetopause, moves inward when the solar wind strengthens.

2 · The Lorentz force

A particle of charge q moving with velocity v through an electric field E and a magnetic flux density B experiences the Lorentz force. In the belts the electric field is small and the magnetic term dominates.

F = q(E + v × B)
(6.1)
F force on the particle (N) · q charge, signed (C; +1.6 × 10−19 C for a proton) · E electric field (V/m) · v particle velocity (m/s) · B magnetic flux density (T; Earth's surface field ≈ 5 × 10−5 T). The cross product makes the magnetic term perpendicular to both v and B, so it does no work and cannot change the particle's speed. Formula sheet §6

Two consequences of the cross product do all the work in this module. First, a magnetic field changes a charged particle's direction but never its speed or its kinetic energy, because a force perpendicular to the velocity does no work. A trapped particle therefore keeps the energy it arrived with indefinitely, which is why the belts are dangerous: they hold 100 MeV protons for years. Second, the force reverses sign with the charge, so a proton and an electron moving the same way through the same field curve in opposite directions. That single fact separates the two species spatially and produces the belt structure of §6.

3 · Gyration: the first motion

Resolve a particle's velocity into a component along the field line and a component perpendicular to it. The magnetic force acts only on the perpendicular component and is always at right angles to it, so the perpendicular motion is a circle. Combined with the unaffected parallel motion, the particle travels along a helix wound around the field line.

The radius of that helix, the gyroradius, follows from equating the magnetic force to the centripetal force required for circular motion.

rg = m v⊥q B = p⊥q B
(6.2)
rg gyroradius (m) · m particle mass (kg) · v⊥ velocity component perpendicular to B (m/s) · p⊥ perpendicular momentum (kg·m/s) — use this form for relativistic particles, where p = γmv · q charge magnitude (C) · B flux density (T). Formula sheet §6

The mass dependence is what separates species. In a 50 µT field a 1 MeV proton has a gyroradius of about 2.9 km, while a 1 MeV electron — with the same energy but far less momentum, because momentum at fixed kinetic energy scales with the square root of mass in the non-relativistic case and less steeply once relativistic — has a gyroradius of about 95 m. Both are small compared with the scale over which Earth's field varies, which is thousands of kilometres, and that separation of scales is the condition under which the trapping picture holds at all.

4 · Bounce: reflection at the poles

A dipole field line leaves the equatorial plane, curves toward a pole, and enters the Earth. The field strength along it is lowest at the equator and rises steeply toward the pole, where the field lines converge. A particle spiralling along that line therefore moves into a strengthening field, and what happens next is the mechanism that closes the trap.

As the particle moves into stronger field, the quantity mv⊥²/2B — the magnetic moment of its circular motion — is conserved. Because B rises, v⊥ must rise with it. The total speed cannot change, since the magnetic force does no work, so the increase in perpendicular velocity comes entirely at the expense of the parallel velocity. At some point along the line the parallel velocity reaches zero, the particle stops advancing, and the same force that slowed it accelerates it back the way it came. That location is the mirror point.

The particle then travels back along the field line, mirrors at the opposite hemisphere, and repeats. The period of this bounce is fractions of a second to a few seconds for the energies present in the belts. A trapped particle has been bouncing between the northern and southern mirror points continuously since it was captured, which may have been years earlier.

The mirror point sits where the field is strong enough to reverse the particle, and that location depends on the angle between the particle's velocity and the field line at the equator. A particle moving almost perpendicular to the line mirrors high above the atmosphere and stays trapped. A particle moving almost along the line mirrors only at very high field strength, which for a dipole means very low altitude — and if that altitude is below about 100 km the particle encounters the atmosphere and is absorbed before it can reflect. The range of angles for which this happens is the loss cone, and it is the only route out of the trap. Everything about the belts' lifetime, structure and low-altitude boundary follows from which particles fall into the loss cone.

5 · Drift: the third motion

The two motions so far confine a particle to one field line. A third motion moves it between them. Because the dipole field is stronger closer to Earth, the gyroradius of a particle is slightly smaller on the near side of its circular orbit than on the far side. The circle therefore does not close, and its centre creeps sideways — around the Earth, perpendicular to both the field and its gradient. The curvature of the field lines contributes a second drift in the same direction.

The drift direction depends on the sign of the charge: positive ions drift westward and electrons eastward. The two species therefore circle the planet in opposite senses, which constitutes a net westward electric current — the ring current, whose strength is the standard index of geomagnetic storm intensity, since the magnetic field it generates opposes and measurably weakens the field at Earth's surface. Drift periods are minutes to hours, so a trapped particle completes many thousands of bounces for each circuit of the planet.

The three motions together confine the particle to a closed surface — a shell of field lines, wrapped around the planet, on which the particle bounces and drifts indefinitely. That shell, not a region of space, is the object that the radiation belts are made of, and it is why belt populations are mapped against a field-line label rather than against altitude.

6 · The structure of the belts

The trapped population is not uniform. It divides into two concentrated regions separated by a comparatively empty gap, and the three zones differ in composition, stability and hazard.

The inner belt occupies roughly 1.2 to 2.5 Earth radii from the centre of the Earth — altitudes of about 1,270 to 9,600 km. It contains protons at energies up to several hundred MeV, the most penetrating trapped particles anywhere in the system, together with electrons and a heavy-ion component. Its population is stable on timescales of years, because at these altitudes there are few waves capable of scattering particles into the loss cone. The principal source of its very energetic protons is the decay of neutrons produced when galactic cosmic rays strike the upper atmosphere: a neutron is uncharged and so escapes the atmosphere freely, and if it decays while still inside the magnetosphere it leaves behind a proton already in a trapped orbit.

The slot region lies between about 2 and 3 Earth radii. It is depleted, not empty, and it is kept depleted by electromagnetic waves in the plasma that scatter electrons into the loss cone faster than they are resupplied. During large geomagnetic storms the slot fills with multi-MeV electrons within hours and then drains over weeks — the one part of the belt structure that changes on a timescale a spacecraft operator can observe.

The outer belt extends from about 3 to 7 Earth radii and is dominated by electrons, with energies up to roughly 10 MeV. Unlike the inner belt it is highly variable: its intensity can change by three orders of magnitude in a day in response to solar-wind conditions, and its inner and outer boundaries move. Geostationary orbit at 6.6 Earth radii sits at its outer edge, so geostationary spacecraft move in and out of the belt as it expands and contracts.

7 · Why protons inside and electrons outside

The separation follows from the source of each population and from what removes it, and the two belts have different answers to both questions.

The inner belt's most energetic protons are produced in place. Galactic cosmic rays striking the upper atmosphere generate neutrons, a fraction of which travel upward. Being uncharged, those neutrons are unaffected by the magnetic field and move in straight lines until they decay, with a half-life of about ten minutes. A neutron that decays at a few thousand kilometres altitude produces a proton at that location, already inside the trap, at an energy inherited from the cosmic ray that made it — hundreds of MeV. This mechanism deposits particles deep inside the field, which is exactly where the inner belt is, and it produces protons rather than electrons because the decay electron carries away only a small share of the energy.

The outer belt is populated instead from the solar wind and the magnetotail, where plasma is injected and then accelerated by wave–particle interactions. That plasma enters at large distances, so the resulting population sits at large distances, and the acceleration processes involved are far more efficient for electrons than for protons.

Stability then sorts the two by radius. Deep inside the field, at inner-belt altitudes, the plasma is cold and dense, wave activity is weak, and a trapped particle survives for years. Further out the field is weaker, more variable, and directly coupled to solar-wind disturbances, so particles are both injected and lost rapidly. The result is a stable, hard, proton-dominated inner region and a variable, electron-dominated outer region.

A third factor reinforces the separation. Equation (6.2) makes the gyroradius proportional to momentum, and a proton carries far more momentum than an electron of the same energy. For trapping to work, the gyroradius must stay small compared with the scale over which the field changes — so the more massive the particle and the higher its energy, the stronger the field must be to hold it. Strong field means close to Earth. Very energetic protons can only be trapped in the inner region; the outer region is too weak to confine them.

8 · How the belts respond to the solar cycle

The two belts respond to solar activity in opposite directions, and getting this the right way round matters for orbit selection.

Inner-belt protons are most intense at solar minimum. The mechanism is atmospheric. Solar extreme-ultraviolet output rises at solar maximum, heats the upper atmosphere, and causes it to expand; the neutral density at any given altitude increases as a result. Trapped protons passing through the denser gas near their mirror points lose energy to Coulomb collisions more rapidly and are removed from the belt sooner. The trapped proton population is therefore anticorrelated with solar activity — highest when the Sun is quiet and the atmosphere is contracted. The NASA orbit table used in this course records the same effect in its own note against low Earth orbit: trapped protons are higher at solar minimum. Mechanism after Qin et al., J. Geophys. Res. Space Physics 119, 9658 (2014), and Huston & Pfitzer's solar-modulation model.

Outer-belt electrons are most intense at solar maximum and during storms, since they are supplied and accelerated by solar-wind-driven processes. Their variability is much larger than the solar-cycle trend: a single geomagnetic storm changes the outer belt more than the cycle does.

A third, geometric effect operates on top of both. A stronger solar wind compresses the magnetopause inward on the day side and stretches the field on the night side, moving the outer boundary of the trapped region. A spacecraft in a fixed orbit therefore encounters a belt whose boundary has shifted, independently of how many particles the belt contains.

On the explanation given in class. Lecture 4 attributed the lower medium-Earth-orbit risk at solar maximum to compression of the magnetosphere opening a larger gap on the night side. That compression is real and does move the outer boundary, but it is not the mechanism behind the inner-belt proton anticorrelation that the orbit table is reporting. The dominant mechanism is atmospheric expansion increasing collisional loss, as above. Both effects act in the same direction for medium Earth orbit, so the conclusion in the table is unaffected.

9 · The South Atlantic Anomaly

Because the dipole is displaced from Earth's centre, the field is weaker on one side of the planet than the other at any given altitude. The weakest region is over the South Atlantic off the coast of Brazil. The offset dipole accounts for much of that weakening but not all of it: a patch of reversed magnetic flux at the core–mantle boundary beneath the South Atlantic deepens the minimum further and is what drives its observed growth and westward drift, which an eccentric dipole alone would not produce. A trapped particle drifting around the Earth on its shell reaches its lowest altitude there — the shell is anchored to field strength, and where the field is weak, a given field strength occurs closer to the ground.

The consequence is that the inner proton belt dips to altitudes as low as 200 km over the South Atlantic, intersecting orbits that are elsewhere completely below it. This region is the South Atlantic Anomaly, and for a spacecraft in low Earth orbit it is essentially the entire radiation environment: the International Space Station, the Hubble Space Telescope and every other low-orbit spacecraft accumulate the large majority of their trapped-particle dose during the few passes per day that cross it. Instruments are routinely commanded into a safe state for the duration of a crossing, and the elevated single-event upset rate inside it is a standard feature of low-orbit spacecraft operations.

10 · The two shields, and what launch removes

Earth provides two distinct shields against the populations of M05, and they work by different mechanisms.

The magnetic field deflects. A charged particle arriving from outside must cross field lines to reach Earth, and equation (6.1) opposes that motion. Below a threshold energy that depends on the arrival direction and the latitude, a particle simply cannot reach a given point — it is turned away. The threshold is highest at the equator, where field lines run horizontally and an incoming particle must cross them all, and falls to zero over the magnetic poles, where field lines run vertically and offer a direct path down. This is why polar orbits see solar energetic particles that equatorial orbits do not, and why the aurora is a polar phenomenon. The field deflects charged particles only: neutrons and photons pass through it unaffected.

The atmosphere absorbs. The column of air above sea level amounts to about 1,033 g/cm², equivalent to roughly ten metres of water. No particle in any of the populations discussed survives that thickness; what reaches the ground is a shower of secondary particles produced by the primaries high in the atmosphere, and it delivers the 20–25 mrem per year quantified in M04. The absorption is not uniform with altitude — half the atmospheric mass lies below 5.5 km — so the dose rate rises steeply with height long before orbit is reached.

One proton shows the scale. A 250 MeV proton, in the energy range where the galactic-cosmic-ray proton spectrum of M05 peaks, travels 42.9 g/cm² of dry air before ionization and excitation have taken all of its energy — its range, defined in M12 — which is 4 % of the column. Compressed to sea-level density that is 356 m of air; arriving vertically from space, where the air is thin, the proton comes to rest near 22 km altitude. Its range is also about half the nuclear interaction length of air, the mean path of 90 g/cm² that an energetic proton travels before an inelastic nuclear interaction, so a large fraction of such protons strike a nitrogen or oxygen nucleus before they stop, and those collisions start the secondary shower that reaches the ground. M13 reproduces the stopping calculation with SRIM, where the result depends on entering the density of the gas correctly.

A spacecraft in low Earth orbit has left the second shield entirely and retains most of the first. A spacecraft at geostationary altitude is beyond most of the first as well. Beyond the magnetosphere there is neither. That progression is the whole content of the orbit comparison in M07.

How the belts were found. Explorer 1, the first United States satellite, carried a Geiger counter built by James Van Allen's group at the University of Iowa and launched in January 1958. Above about 1,000 km the counter reported zero — not a low count, but nothing at all. The instrument had not failed; it was saturating, and its readout electronics could not distinguish a saturated detector from a silent one. The correct interpretation, that the count rate was far above anything expected, established the existence of the trapped radiation belts within months. The structure has been mapped continuously since, most recently by the two Van Allen Probes, which flew through both belts from 2012 to 2019 and found, among other things, a third transient belt that formed and persisted for four weeks.

Two particles are trapped on the same field line at the same equatorial altitude: a 1 MeV electron and a 1 MeV proton. Which mirrors closer to the pole, and which is lost from the belt sooner?

The mirror point does not depend on mass or energy. It is set entirely by the particle's pitch angle at the equator, because the conserved quantity mv⊥²/2B compares the particle with itself: mirroring occurs where B has risen by the factor 1/sin²α relative to its equatorial value, with α the equatorial pitch angle. Two particles launched at the same angle mirror at the same field strength and therefore at the same place, whatever their mass or energy. The mass enters only through the gyroradius, which is about 30 times larger for the proton, and through the bounce period, which is longer for the heavier particle at equal energy.

Loss is a different question with a different answer. Both particles lose energy by Coulomb collisions with the residual atmosphere near their mirror points, and the electron additionally radiates when deflected — the bremsstrahlung process of M09 — and is far more readily scattered into the loss cone by plasma waves, since the resonant wave modes in this region interact with electrons much more strongly than with protons. The electron is lost first, typically in days to weeks against years for the proton. This is the same asymmetry that leaves the stable inner belt proton-dominated.

Summary

  • Earth's field is approximately a dipole of about 50 µT at the surface, tilted 11° and offset ~500 km from the planet's centre (closer to 590 km at the present epoch), confined by the solar wind into a magnetosphere ~10 Earth radii on the day side with a long night-side tail.
  • F = q(E + v × B). The magnetic term does no work, so a trapped particle keeps its energy indefinitely, and it reverses with charge, so protons and electrons curve oppositely.
  • Three motions trap a particle: gyration around a field line at rg = p⊥/qB, bounce between mirror points where converging field lines reverse it, and drift around the planet — westward for ions, eastward for electrons, constituting the ring current.
  • Mirroring conserves mv⊥²/2B: rising B converts parallel velocity into perpendicular until the particle reverses. Particles whose mirror point falls below ~100 km are absorbed — the loss cone, the only exit from the trap.
  • The inner belt (1.2–2.5 RE, altitudes ~1,270–9,600 km) holds protons to several hundred MeV and is stable for years, sourced largely by decay of cosmic-ray-produced neutrons. The outer belt (3–7 RE) holds electrons to ~10 MeV and varies by three orders of magnitude in a day. The slot between them is kept depleted by wave scattering.
  • Inner-belt protons peak at solar minimum, because solar-maximum EUV heating expands the atmosphere and increases collisional loss. Outer-belt electrons peak at solar maximum and during storms.
  • The offset dipole, deepened by a reversed-flux patch at the core–mantle boundary, lowers the inner belt to ~200 km over the South Atlantic. The South Atlantic Anomaly supplies most of the trapped-particle dose and upsets for any low-Earth-orbit spacecraft.
  • The field deflects charged particles, with the cut-off energy highest at the equator and zero over the poles; the atmosphere absorbs, at 1,033 g/cm² ≈ 10 m water equivalent — a 250 MeV proton's range in air is 42.9 g/cm², 4 % of the column, and it stops near 22 km. Launch removes the second entirely and, with altitude, progressively the first.
