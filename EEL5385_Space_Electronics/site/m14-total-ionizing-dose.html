Module 14

Total Ionizing Dose

Total ionizing dose is the damage ionizing radiation accumulates in the oxides of a device over a mission: positive charge trapped in the silicon dioxide and electronic defects created at its interface with silicon. This module follows that charge from the first electron–hole pair to the threshold shift, leakage current and circuit failure it produces, and to the tests that qualify a part against it.

Prerequisites M01, M04, M08, M10 Read time ~45 min Sources Deck 8 pp. 31–42; Lecture 8; Oldham & McLean (2003); Schwank et al. (2008); Fleetwood et al. (1988)

M08 and M10 established how charged particles and photons give up energy by ionization, freeing electron–hole pairs, and M08 followed those pairs in silicon, where the device's own fields collect them within picoseconds and leave nothing behind. M11 followed the non-ionizing share of the energy into displaced atoms. This module follows ionization into the insulator. Silicon dioxide forms the gate dielectric and the isolation of every MOS device, and in it the holes are nearly immobile, a fraction of them are captured by defects for months, and hydrogen released along the way creates new electronic states at the silicon surface. The accumulation of that charge and those states with absorbed dose is the total ionizing dose effect, abbreviated TID.

TID sets the working lifetime of most electronics in orbit. Every trapped electron and proton of the belts in M06 contributes to it, a mission's requirement is stated as a dose behind a shield (M07 §9), and a commercial part can fail after a few krad(Si). The module follows the order of Deck 8: the classes of radiation effect, the symptoms of TID and a documented flash-memory failure, the mechanism on the band diagram of an MOS structure, the threshold-voltage shift and its two components, the role of oxide thickness, annealing, leakage through the isolation oxide, and testing.

The treatment stops at the mechanism and its device consequences. Defect chemistry, trapping kinetics and hardness-assurance statistics are developed in the Radiation Effects course, in EEE 5416 M07, M08, M09 and M15, and linked where they extend a point made here. The MOS background — the band diagram, the Fermi level, inversion, the threshold voltage and the subthreshold swing — is in M01.

1 · Three classes of radiation effect

Deck 8 p. 32 divides the effects of radiation on electronics into two groups, and the division is by how the damage accumulates. Long-term effects grow with the integrated exposure; every particle contributes a small share and no single one matters. Transient, or single-particle, effects are caused by one particle at one place and one instant, and can happen at the first particle of a mission as readily as at the last.

The long-term group has two members. Total ionizing dose is the damage done by the energy deposited through ionization, measured as absorbed dose (M04 §9) in rad or gray of a named material. Its damage resides in insulators, above all SiO₂, and it affects every MOS device. Displacement damage is the damage done by the non-ionizing share of the energy loss, which knocks atoms out of their lattice sites (M11). Its damage resides in the semiconductor crystal, it is measured by particle fluence at a stated energy, or as a displacement damage dose, the product of fluence and non-ionizing energy loss (M11 §4), not as ionizing dose, and it degrades devices that depend on minority-carrier lifetime: bipolar transistors, solar cells and optoelectronics.

The transient group is the single-event effects (SEE). A heavy ion by direct ionization, or a proton through the secondaries of a nuclear reaction (M12 §8), deposits a dense column of charge along its track, and a junction nearby collects it within nanoseconds (M08 §8). Whether that pulse matters depends on the linear energy transfer of the particle and on where it strikes, not on the dose accumulated so far. The consequence ranges from a flipped memory bit, which a rewrite corrects, to latchup or burnout, which destroy the part; these are the subject of M15. One particle species can produce all three effects: a trapped proton ionizes along its whole path, displaces atoms along it, most densely near the end, and occasionally triggers a nuclear reaction whose fragments cause a single event.

The overlap is the regime in which dose arrives so fast that it acts as a single event. The pulse of gamma rays from a nuclear detonation delivers from about 10⁸ to 10¹² rad(Si)/s. Silicon gains 4×10¹³ electron–hole pairs per cm³ for every rad(Si) (§3), so each nanosecond of the pulse adds 4×10¹³ cm⁻³ at 10⁹ rad(Si)/s and 4×10¹⁶ cm⁻³ at 10¹² rad(Si)/s. Toward the top of the range, within tens of nanoseconds, the excess carriers outnumber the dopants of lightly doped regions, and at any rate in the range every reverse-biased junction on the die collects a large photocurrent at the same moment. The results — upset of every storage node, latchup, burnout, collapse of the supply rail — are single-event consequences produced by a dose. MIL-STD-883 carries separate test methods for them: Method 1020 for dose-rate-induced latchup, 1021 for dose-rate upset of digital microcircuits and 1023 for the dose-rate response of linear microcircuits. These prompt effects arise only in the nuclear-weapon environment and are not of direct interest to civil and commercial space missions, where dose rates are many orders of magnitude lower.

Two different phenomena share the name dose-rate effect

Deck 8 p. 32 labels the whole overlap of its diagram "Dose-rate effects". That label fits the prompt effects above. The same name is also used for a different phenomenon: the dependence of ordinary total-dose damage on the rate at which a given dose accumulates, at the low rates found in orbit. That dependence belongs entirely inside the dose circle, because it concerns accumulated charge rather than a transient. §6 shows that MOS gate oxides normally have no true dependence of this kind — only a dependence on elapsed time — while bipolar linear circuits do have one, called ELDRS, and so can some thick isolation oxides. The Radiation Effects course draws the same three-way division in EEE 5416 M05.

2 · Total ionizing dose and what it does to a part

Deck 8 p. 33 defines TID as cumulative, long-term ionizing damage due to protons and electrons, lists four symptoms, and notes that shielding mitigates it only partly. This section makes each of those statements quantitative enough to use, and then examines the measured failure the deck uses to illustrate them: a flash memory that lost the ability to erase itself at 8 krad(Si).

The quantity is absorbed dose, the energy deposited per unit mass (M04 equation (4.5)), and it always belongs to a named material. Two materials matter here. Charge is trapped in SiO₂, so the physics of §3 is written in rad(SiO₂); test standards and mission requirements are written in rad(Si). For a photon field the two are related through the mass energy-absorption coefficients of M10 §7.

The two limits follow from the interaction that dominates. At 1.25 MeV essentially every interaction is a Compton scatter, whose mass coefficient is proportional to Z/A, and Z/A is 0.498 for silicon and 0.500 for oxygen. At 10 keV photoelectric absorption dominates. Per atom it rises as roughly Z⁴ to Z⁵ (M10), and per gram, since the mass number is about 2Z, as roughly Z³ to Z⁴; oxygen (Z = 8) makes up 53 % of the oxide's mass, so silicon absorbs 1.8 times more energy per gram than the oxide. The deck mixes units: p. 33 is in krad(Si), p. 39 in rad(SiO₂) and p. 41 in krad with no material stated. Throughout this module every dose names its material.

In orbit the dose comes from charged particles. Trapped electrons and protons in the belts (M06) and protons from solar particle events (M05) deliver almost all of it; galactic cosmic rays are too sparse to contribute appreciable dose, although they dominate single-event effects. Shielding helps only partly because the populations differ in range (M12). Low-energy protons are stopped within the first millimetre or so of aluminium and the belt electrons within about a centimetre, so the dose from them falls by orders of magnitude as the shield thickens. High-energy protons have ranges of many g/cm² and pass through any practical wall, and the electrons stopped in the shield radiate bremsstrahlung (M09), which aluminium barely attenuates (M10 §10). For a three-month geostationary-transfer orbit analysed by NASA, the electron dose at the centre of an aluminium sphere is cut off near 0.9 cm of wall, and behind thicker walls the dose is set by trapped and solar protons and a nearly flat bremsstrahlung floor. Beyond about a centimetre, added mass buys little.

The four symptoms on Deck 8 p. 33 are the device-level and circuit-level faces of the same trapped charge. Threshold shifts come from charge in the gate oxide and states at its interface (§4). Leakage current comes from the negative threshold shift of n-channel transistors and, far more strongly in modern parts, from charge in the thick isolation oxide (§7). Timing changes follow because a shifted threshold and a reduced carrier mobility both change the current a transistor delivers, and gate delay is inversely proportional to that current (M01). Functional failure is the point at which any of these carries a circuit outside its operating margin.

The flash-memory example

A flash memory cell is an n-channel MOSFET with two gates stacked above the channel. The lower one, the floating gate, is a layer of polysilicon enclosed entirely in SiO₂: a thin tunnel oxide separates it from the channel and an interpoly dielectric separates it from the control gate above. The floating gate has no electrical connection, so electrons placed on it stay there for years, held in by the energy barrier the oxide presents. Their negative charge partly screens the channel from the control gate, and the threshold voltage measured at the control gate rises. A cell with electrons stored is programmed: its threshold lies above the read voltage, it does not conduct when read, and it reads as 0. A cell with the electrons removed is erased: its threshold lies below the read voltage, it conducts, and it reads as 1. An erased byte therefore reads FF in hexadecimal.

Moving electrons through the oxide requires a high field. In a NAND-architecture cell both programming and erasure use Fowler–Nordheim tunnelling, in which the field thins the oxide barrier into a triangle narrow enough for electrons to tunnel through it, between the floating gate and the silicon body. NOR-architecture cells program instead by injecting hot electrons from the channel, and erase by Fowler–Nordheim tunnelling to the source. NAND cells need about 20 V for erasure and programming and NOR cells about 12 V, but the chips run from much lower supplies: 3.3 V for the Samsung NAND part of Figure 3, 5 V for the Intel NOR parts tested alongside it. An on-chip charge pump generates the difference: a chain of stages, each adding up to one supply voltage less a transistor threshold. In the Samsung pump each stage is built around a p-channel pass transistor in its own floating n-well.

Figure 3 plots a 128-Mb NAND flash from Samsung. The slide gives no source for the curve, but the part and its failure dose match the tests of Nguyen and co-workers at JPL, in which parts were irradiated with cobalt-60 in steps while powered and given a full erase, write and read test between steps. The part runs from a 3.3 V supply and needs about 20 V for erasure. The slide labels its vertical axis only as the voltage during the erase function and does not say at which node it is measured; at 12.6 V before irradiation it is not the roughly 20 V the cells need. The plotted voltage stays flat at about 12.6 V up to 6 krad(Si), falls slightly by 7 krad(Si), and collapses to about 6.7 V at 8 krad(Si), where the part fails to erase. In the JPL tests the same part left unbiased during irradiation did not fail erasure until 45 krad(Si), which is the first indication that the electric field inside the device controls the damage (§3).

Nguyen and co-workers attribute the failure to the charge pump rather than the memory cells, and the pump's own transfer function shows why TID attacks it. Each stage passes charge through a p-channel transistor and loses that transistor's threshold voltage in doing so.

The likely weak link is the high-voltage generator, not the cells

Total dose shifts the thresholds of the pump's p-channel pass transistors to larger magnitudes and raises leakage between stages; equation (14.2) turns each millivolt of threshold shift into N + 1 millivolts of lost output. Once the output falls below the tunnelling voltage the cells need, the operation that needs the highest voltage fails first. Nguyen and co-workers saw exactly that order in the 128-Mb part: erasure, then programming, then reading. They attribute the failure to the pump on that evidence and on the pump's transfer function; this part offers no way to bypass its pump, so the attribution could not be tested directly. In an older Intel NOR device that accepted its erase and write voltages from outside the chip, bypassing the internal pump raised the failure dose substantially, which identified the pump as the weak link there. The example therefore shows threshold shift and leakage — the first two symptoms — most likely producing a functional failure in peripheral circuitry while the storage array itself remains readable.

The JPL study irradiated at either 25 or 0.012 rad(Si)/s and does not say which rate applied to this part. At the higher rate 8 krad(Si) accumulates in 320 s, about five minutes; at the lower, in about eight days. A spacecraft accumulates the same dose over months or years, and §6 shows that this difference in time, rather than in rate, changes the outcome.

3 · From absorbed energy to trapped charge

Deck 8 pp. 34–37 set out the mechanism on the energy-band diagram of an MOS structure with a positive voltage on the gate. The band diagram (M01) plots electron energy against position through the gate, the oxide and the silicon. A positive gate lowers electron energy at the gate, so the oxide's bands tilt downward toward it: electrons in the oxide move toward the gate and holes toward the silicon. Six steps then carry the absorbed energy to two lasting populations — holes trapped in the oxide near the silicon, and new electronic states at the silicon surface. Each step discards part of the charge, and together the steps span more than twenty orders of magnitude in time, from femtoseconds to years.

① Pair generation

Incident radiation deposits its energy through a cascade of secondary electrons (M08), which excite electrons across the 8.9 eV gap of SiO₂. Deck 8 p. 36 places generation on a femtosecond time scale. The mean energy spent per pair is 17 eV in SiO₂ (Deck 8 p. 35; measured as 17 ± 1 eV by Benedetto and Boesch), against 3.6 eV in silicon; the pair energy exceeds the gap because part of each deposit goes into lattice vibration and into electrons left below the threshold for making further pairs. Dividing the energy a rad deposits in a cubic centimetre of oxide by the pair energy gives the number of pairs created.

g0 = ρSiO₂ · 6.24×1013Ep = 8.1×1012 cm−3 rad(SiO₂)−1
(14.3)
g0 generation constant, the number of electron–hole pairs created per cm³ of oxide per rad(SiO₂) (cm⁻³ rad(SiO₂)⁻¹) · ρSiO₂ density of thermal oxide, 2.2 g/cm³ · 6.24×1013 energy deposited by 1 rad, in eV/g (1 rad = 100 erg/g) · Ep mean pair-creation energy, 17 eV in SiO₂. The dose must be in rad(SiO₂); convert a rad(Si) figure with equation (14.1). The same construction with silicon's 2.33 g/cm³ and 3.6 eV gives 4×1013 pairs cm−3 rad(Si)−1. On the formula sheet as (14.3).

② Initial recombination and electron removal

A new electron and hole are close together and attract each other. Before the field separates them, some pairs recombine; this initial recombination is complete within about a picosecond. The fraction of holes that survives it is the fractional yield, fy (Deck 8 p. 36), and it depends on two quantities. The oxide field pulls each pair apart; the stronger the field, the fewer pairs recombine. The density of pairs along the track, set by the particle's linear energy transfer (M12), decides how many neighbours a carrier can recombine with. The fast secondary electrons of a cobalt-60 exposure leave isolated pairs, each of which can recombine only with its own partner, so most survive. An alpha particle or a low-energy proton leaves a dense column in which the carriers of neighbouring pairs overlap, and most recombine. At an oxide field of 1 MV/cm the yield spans more than an order of magnitude across particle types.

The electrons that escape recombination leave the oxide almost at once. Their mobility in SiO₂ is about 20 cm²/V·s, and under a positive gate they drift to the gate electrode in a picosecond or less. Deck 8 p. 36 notes that transport is by diffusion, and by drift wherever a field is present; in a biased gate oxide drift dominates. What remains is a sheet of holes spread through the oxide thickness.

Nh = fy(Eox) · g0 · D · tox
(14.4)

③ Hole transport

Holes in SiO₂ move many orders of magnitude more slowly than electrons. A hole in the oxide is a small polaron: its charge distorts the surrounding atomic network, and the distortion travels with it, so the hole moves by hopping from one localized state to the next — randomly placed sites about 1 nm apart — carrying the distortion along. Transport by this hopping is dispersive, spreading a single pulse of holes over many decades of time; it speeds up with field; it is strongly thermally activated above about 140 K and not activated below it, a transition that is the classic signature of small-polaron hopping; and its duration rises steeply with thickness, roughly as the fourth power. At room temperature it is normally over in much less than a second after a pulse, and at low temperature it is orders of magnitude slower. In a hardened oxide most holes that reach the interface pass into the silicon and recombine there, so the positive charge in the oxide and the threshold shift it causes fall as transport proceeds; in an unhardened oxide half or more of them are trapped (④).

④ Hole trapping near the interface

The holes that do not pass into the silicon are caught by defects in the last few nanometres of oxide. Oxidation is incomplete there: the transition layer between crystalline silicon and stoichiometric SiO₂ is oxygen-deficient and strained, and it contains a high density of oxygen vacancies. A vacancy leaves two silicon atoms, each still bonded to three oxygens, joined by a weak Si–Si bond where the oxygen should be. A captured hole breaks that bond. The structure relaxes asymmetrically: one silicon atom moves into the plane of its three oxygens and carries the positive charge, and the other keeps an unpaired electron in a dangling bond. This is the E′ centre, identified by electron spin resonance, which detects unpaired electrons through their absorption of microwaves in a magnetic field; its correlation with trapped holes was established by Lenahan and Dressendorfer in 1984. The same defect appears in the process context of EEE 6338 M05b §5.2. The trapped hole sits far from both band edges of the oxide — a deep trap — and at room temperature it persists for hours to years.

(14.5)

Holds before any annealing (§6). On the formula sheet as (14.5).

In radiation-hardened gate oxides the trapped charge lies within about 3–4 nm of the silicon interface, and in one study 90 % of it lay within 5 nm. In isolation oxides and some commercial oxides the traps are distributed through the bulk. Processing controls the vacancy density: a post-oxidation anneal in nitrogen above about 875 °C removes oxygen from the film, creates vacancies, and sharply increases the trapped charge.

⑤ Hydrogen release and ⑥ interface-trap formation

A silicon surface terminated by oxide leaves some silicon atoms with only three silicon neighbours and a fourth orbital, a dangling bond, pointing into the oxide. The oxidized surface carries of order 10¹³ cm⁻² such sites, and hydrogen introduced during processing passivates almost all of them as Si–H bonds (EEE 6338 M05b §4.1). Radiation undoes the passivation in two stages. Holes release hydrogen bound in the oxide as protons, H⁺, as they hop through it or as they are trapped near the silicon interface. The protons then hop — in steps of about 0.26 nm, the spacing of oxygen atoms — toward the silicon under a positive gate, and at the interface a proton reacts with an Si–H bond, leaves as H₂, and restores the dangling bond. The restored site, a silicon atom back-bonded to three silicon atoms, is the Pb centre, the radiation-induced interface trap.

An interface trap differs from a trapped hole in two ways that decide its effect. It is an electronic state with energy inside the silicon bandgap, located at the silicon surface, so it exchanges electrons with the silicon quickly and its charge follows the position of the Fermi level (§4). And it forms slowly, at the pace of proton transport: after a short pulse, buildup is under way at 1 s, takes thousands of seconds to approach saturation and in some oxides continues for months. Buildup is thermally activated, with an activation energy of 0.82 eV. Its areal density is ΔNit (cm⁻²), or, per unit energy in the gap, Dit (cm⁻² eV⁻¹). Interface traps need positive bias during the proton-transport stage; with the gate held negative through irradiation and anneal, little or none forms.

Deck 8 p. 35 states that an electric field is needed. The field acts twice: it raises the fractional yield, and it carries holes and protons to the silicon interface, where the hole traps and the Si–H bonds are. Without an applied bias some charge still builds, at lower yield, under the built-in field set by the gate and substrate work functions. Under negative bias the holes collect near the gate, where §4 shows they move the threshold far less. The field therefore controls how much charge survives and where it ends up, and the biased flash memory of §2, which failed at less than a fifth of the dose of the unbiased one, is an instance of it.

The deck's wording for the hole-trap precursors

Deck 8 p. 35 reads: close to the interface, a large number of "oxide vacancies" due to out-diffusion of oxygen in the oxide and "lattice mismatch". The review the slide follows (Schwank, Sandia 2007, published as Schwank et al. 2008) says oxygen vacancies, which is the defect described above. Thermal SiO₂ is amorphous and has no lattice; the mismatch is the strain of the sub-stoichiometric transition layer grown on crystalline silicon. The out-diffusion refers to oxygen lost from the film during high-temperature processing, which is how the vacancies form. The figures on pp. 34–36 also label the interface "Si/SO₂", for Si/SiO₂.

4 · The threshold-voltage shift

Deck 8 p. 38 writes the device consequence as one sum: the threshold-voltage shift is the shift due to oxide-trapped charge plus the shift due to interface traps. The threshold voltage is the gate voltage at which the silicon surface inverts (M01). Any charge added to the oxide or to the interface must be balanced by the gate before the surface reaches the same condition, so it moves the threshold. The two populations of §3 move it by different physics: oxide-trapped charge is fixed positive charge, while interface-trap charge depends on where the Fermi level sits at the surface, and therefore on the device type.

Oxide-trapped charge

A sheet of positive charge inside the oxide is balanced by negative image charge, partly on the gate and partly in the silicon. The share drawn from the silicon grows linearly with the sheet's distance from the gate: a sheet at the silicon interface draws its whole image from the silicon, and a sheet at the gate draws none. Only the share in the silicon changes the surface, so each element of charge shifts the threshold in proportion to its distance from the gate.

The shift is negative in both device types, because positive charge near the channel draws electrons to the silicon surface — what Deck 8 p. 37 means by "brings electrons to channel". An n-channel transistor moves toward depletion mode: its threshold falls, its off-state current rises, and with enough charge it conducts at zero gate voltage. A p-channel transistor, whose threshold is already negative, needs a more negative gate to turn on and delivers less current at a given gate voltage.

Interface traps

An interface trap holds or releases an electron according to whether its energy lies below or above the Fermi level at the surface. Two kinds of state behave differently when they change occupancy. An acceptor-like state is neutral when empty and negative when it holds an electron; a donor-like state is positive when empty and neutral when it holds an electron. Radiation-induced interface traps are predominantly acceptor-like in the upper half of the silicon gap and donor-like in the lower half, so their net charge is zero when the surface Fermi level sits at midgap. Such states, able to take either sign, are called amphoteric.

At threshold the surface of an n-channel device is inverted and its Fermi level lies above midgap. The acceptor-like traps between midgap and the Fermi level are filled, the interface carries net negative charge taken from the channel, and the gate needs a more positive voltage to invert the surface: ΔVit > 0. At threshold in a p-channel device the surface Fermi level lies below midgap. The donor-like traps between the Fermi level and midgap are empty, the interface carries net positive charge, and ΔVit < 0.

Both terms scale with 1/Cox, which is proportional to oxide thickness. For illustration, 10¹¹ cm⁻² charged interface traps shift the threshold by 0.46 V under a 100 nm oxide, where Cox = 3.45×10⁻⁸ F/cm², and by 0.046 V under a 10 nm oxide. Because the n-channel terms oppose each other and evolve on different time scales (§6), an n-channel transistor can show a small net shift that conceals large amounts of both kinds of charge.

What an interface trap does to the channel

Deck 8 p. 37 describes the interface component as "Interface Proton Trapping (it): Brings electrons to channel; Some electrons move into the proton", and p. 38 defines ΔVit as due to "Interface Electron Trapping". The proton is not trapped: at the interface it reacts with an Si–H bond, leaves as H₂, and creates the Pb dangling-bond state (Oldham & McLean 2003, §III.E; the review the slide cites, Schwank et al., IEEE TNS 55, 1833, 2008, labels the same end product Pb). That state then exchanges electrons with the silicon. In an n-channel device at threshold it takes electrons from the channel and becomes negative, which is why ΔVit is positive — the rule the deck itself gives on p. 38. In a p-channel device the traps are net positive, so interface-trap charge is not electron trapping in general; its sign follows the surface Fermi level. The p. 38 sign rules are correct and are the ones to use.

Mobility, drive current and subthreshold swing

Charged interface traps sit on the surface along which the channel carriers move, and scatter those carriers by their Coulomb field, so the channel mobility falls as they accumulate (Deck 8 p. 39). The dependence is usually written in one empirical form.

µ = µ01 + α ΔNit
(14.9)
µ channel mobility after irradiation (cm²/V·s) · µ0 mobility before irradiation (cm²/V·s) · ΔNit radiation-induced interface-trap density (cm⁻²) · α a fitted constant (cm²), specific to the technology. At short times after exposure, trapped holes close to the interface also scatter carriers and the form underestimates the loss. On the formula sheet as (14.9).

Drive current is proportional to µ(VGS − Vth)² (the saturation-current expression of M01), so both the mobility loss and the threshold shift change the current a transistor delivers, and with it the delay of every gate it drives: these are the timing changes of Deck 8 p. 33. In an n-channel transistor the negative ΔVot raises the drive at first, and the positive ΔVit and the mobility loss lower it as interface traps accumulate. In a p-channel transistor every term lowers it.

Interface traps also change the shape of the subthreshold characteristic. As the gate sweeps the surface through the subthreshold range, the Fermi level passes through the interface states, which fill or empty as it does; the gate must supply their charge in addition to the depletion charge. They act as a capacitance in parallel with the depletion capacitance, and the subthreshold swing of M01 acquires one more term.

5 · Oxide thickness

Deck 8 p. 35 states that thick oxides are particularly sensitive. Oxide thickness is the single strongest structural variable in total-dose response, and equations (14.4) to (14.6) already contain the reason. It governs which oxides in a modern part still matter, and it explains why the problem moved from the gate to the isolation (§7).

Thickness enters twice. The number of holes generated per unit area is proportional to the volume of oxide beneath that area, so Nh grows with tox. And the threshold shift produced by a given sheet of charge at the interface is inversely proportional to Cox, which also grows with tox. Substituting (14.4) and (14.5) into the interface limit of (14.6) gives the scaling law.

The measured thickness dependence of the shifts is slightly weaker than the ideal square law. Schwank and co-workers report both ΔVot and ΔVit falling as tox1.5 to tox1.8 for dry and wet oxides. "Roughly tox²", with measured exponents between about 1.5 and 2, is the working statement for threshold shifts. An exponent quoted for a trap density is a different quantity: because Cox ∝ 1/tox, the shift carries one more power of tox than the areal density that causes it. Oldham and McLean note that processing varies enough for a particular oxide to depart from the square law, although no other dependence has been shown to hold in general.

Where the square law stops: the thin-oxide limit

A hole trapped within a few nanometres of an electrode is neutralized by an electron that tunnels to it from the silicon or the gate (§6). The reachable distance is about 4 nm at 80 K; at room temperature estimates run from about 4 nm to at most 6 nm. For oxides thinner than about 12 nm, Saks, Ancona and Modolo found interface-trap buildup and trapped-hole shifts far below the power-law extrapolation from thicker films — for a 6 nm oxide, the rate of interface-trap buildup was a factor of 16 below it — and they cite a 5 nm tunnel oxide that showed no measurable increase in interface-trap density after 8 Mrad. When the oxide is thin enough, the tunnelling regions from the two electrodes meet in the middle and no net positive charge survives. Below about 10 nm a gate oxide is therefore nearly immune to total dose, and the gate oxides of current commercial processes, a few nanometres thick, trap almost no net charge. The thinning that scaling imposed for performance reasons hardened the gate oxide as a side effect.

The consequence reverses the historical picture. When gate oxides were about 100 nm thick, the gate oxide set a part's total-dose tolerance, and hardening meant growing a thin, low-trap oxide under tightly controlled conditions. With commercial gate oxides now below 10 nm, the gate-oxide problem is essentially solved, and what remains are the oxides that did not thin: the isolation between transistors, hundreds of nanometres thick, which by (14.12) develops hundreds to thousands of times the threshold shift of a thin gate oxide for the same dose (§7).

6 · Annealing and time dependence

The charge of §3 does not stay fixed once it forms. Deck 8 pp. 39–40 show that oxide-trapped charge is neutralized over time after irradiation, while interface traps do not anneal at room temperature and continue to form for thousands of seconds. The two components therefore evolve in opposite directions after an exposure, the net threshold shift of an n-channel transistor can change sign, and the outcome of a test depends on how long it lasts. This time dependence is what total-dose test methods are designed around (§8).

Neutralizing the trapped holes

Deck 8 p. 40 names two processes. In tunnel annealing, an electron from the silicon conduction band tunnels through the 3.1 eV barrier at the Si/SiO₂ interface into a trap holding a hole and neutralizes it. The tunnelling probability falls exponentially with distance, so traps close to the interface are emptied first and the boundary between neutralized and still-charged traps moves into the oxide as the logarithm of time. At room temperature only traps within about 4–6 nm of the interface are reachable this way (§5). The rate depends on how the traps are distributed in space and on bias, which changes the shape of the barrier.

Holds while the front is within the tunnelling range, about 4–6 nm at room temperature; it produces the recovery of ΔVot linear in log t shown in Figure 9. On the formula sheet as (14.13).

In thermal annealing, an electron is thermally emitted from the oxide valence band into the trap — equivalently, the trapped hole is emitted into the valence band. The rate depends on how far the trap level lies above the oxide valence band and rises exponentially with temperature, but does not depend on the trap's position. A thermal-emission front therefore sweeps upward through the distribution of trap energies, also as the logarithm of time. Near room temperature tunnelling dominates; at raised temperature thermal emission takes over. Both give a recovery linear in log t. Neutralization is not always removal: some trapped holes are compensated by an electron trapped next to them, and a later negative bias can return about half of the apparently annealed charge, as EEE 5416 M09 §6 develops.

Interface traps behave differently (Deck 8 p. 39). They do not anneal at room temperature; some annealing at 100 °C has been reported, and higher temperatures are normally needed to remove them. Meanwhile they continue to form as protons arrive at the interface. Their effect on mobility (14.9) persists for the same reason.

Figure 9 is the deck's evidence for the dose-rate statements on p. 39. The dose rates span eleven orders of magnitude, so the exposure times do too: the linear accelerator delivered 100 krad(SiO₂) in two pulses, while the slowest caesium-137 source needed 2×10⁶ s, about 23 days. Plotted against time since irradiation began, all five fall on one curve for each component. Fleetwood and co-workers report that, to within the ±10 % uncertainty of their dosimetry, these transistors show no true dose-rate effect and both trap densities are linear in dose. What looks like a dose-rate dependence in these gate oxides is a time dependence: a slow exposure is a long one, and during it oxide-trapped charge anneals while interface traps accumulate. That is the normal behaviour of MOS gate oxides, whose response does not normally depend on dose rate outside the prompt pulses of §1 (Oldham and McLean). It is not universal. The thick, low-field isolation oxides of §7 can show a true enhancement at low dose rate, as the bipolar oxides below do: Johnston, Swimm and Miyahira measured more off-state leakage from the shallow-trench isolation of 0.18 µm n-channel transistors at low dose rate than at high, and Esqueda and co-workers found a low-dose-rate enhancement in field-oxide transistors of a 90 nm process.

What "independent of dose rate" means, and what it does not

Deck 8 p. 39 describes interface-trap buildup as "independent of the dose-rate of the radiation source" and oxide-charge neutralization as following "a transient annealing curve which is independent of dose rate". Both statements hold when curves are compared at equal time since irradiation began, which is how Figure 9 is plotted. They do not mean that the damage a part shows at the end of an exposure is independent of how fast the dose was delivered. At the end of a fast laboratory exposure a part sits at the left of Figure 9, with large negative ΔVot and little ΔVit; at the end of a mission's slow exposure the same dose leaves it at the right, with most of the oxide charge annealed and the interface traps fully formed. For an n-channel transistor these two states can have opposite signs.

The opposite signs have a direct consequence called rebound. Oldham and McLean describe an n-channel transistor irradiated to 1 Mrad whose net threshold shift immediately afterward was below 1 V, because the positive oxide-trapped charge was compensated by negative interface-trap charge. As the trapped holes annealed, the interface traps remained, and the final shift was about +3.5 V — enough to fail the device — reached equally at 25 °C and at 125 °C; the heat only reached it sooner. A p-channel transistor cannot rebound, because both of its terms are negative. The same balance can make a circuit's measured tolerance depend on test rate in a non-monotonic way: one circuit tested across rates failed at a few krad at a high rate, from oxide charge, failed at a somewhat higher dose at a low rate, from interface traps, and survived to a far higher dose at one intermediate rate where the two terms balanced. A single high-rate test therefore cannot predict the end-of-mission state of an n-channel device.

ELDRS: a true dose-rate effect, in bipolar circuits

Bipolar linear circuits — operational amplifiers, comparators, voltage references — behave oppositely to MOS gate oxides. Their total-dose damage lies in the thick, low-field oxides that passivate the base region: interface traps there, joined in n-p-n transistors by the oxide-trapped charge, raise the base current through surface recombination and reduce the transistor's gain. In 1991 Enlow and co-workers found that bipolar transistors from advanced processes degraded more at low dose rates than at the moderate rates used for qualification, at equal dose, and in 1994 Johnston, Swift and Rax found the same enhancement in linear integrated circuits; in such circuits it appears at rates below about 1 rad(Si)/s. This is enhanced low-dose-rate sensitivity, ELDRS, and it is a true dose-rate effect, linked to space-charge effects of slowly moving charge in those low-field oxides. It makes a high-rate test non-conservative: the part looks harder in the laboratory than it is in orbit. The competing models are compared in EEE 5416 M08 §9, and the low-rate test flows in M15 §7–8.

An n-channel transistor with a 60 nm hardened gate oxide is irradiated under positive bias at a high dose rate and measured within minutes. Its threshold has barely moved and its subthreshold slope is slightly shallower. Is the transistor essentially undamaged, and what happens if it is then held under bias for a week at 100 °C?

It is not undamaged. A small net shift in an n-channel device is the sum of a negative ΔVot and a positive ΔVit (14.8), and the shallower subthreshold slope is the signature of interface traps (14.10): charge of both kinds is present and nearly cancels.

During the bake, thermal emission and tunnelling neutralize the trapped holes, so ΔVot moves toward zero, while the interface traps do not anneal at 100 °C to any comparable extent and may still be forming. The net shift therefore moves positive, possibly far beyond its initial value — rebound. The threshold rises, drive current falls with both the shift and the mobility loss (14.9), and timing fails even though the immediate post-irradiation measurement passed. That is the failure the elevated-temperature step of the test method in §8 is designed to expose.

7 · Leakage through the isolation oxide

Deck 8 p. 41 shows the total-dose failure that dominates commercial parts today: charge trapped in the isolation acts as a lateral parasitic leakage path. The gate oxide of §5 has thinned to near-immunity, but every transistor on a die is surrounded by a much thicker oxide that separates it from its neighbours, and by (14.12) that oxide develops threshold shifts of volts at doses that leave the gate oxide untouched.

Isolation oxides

LOCOS, local oxidation of silicon, grows a thick thermal oxide wherever a silicon-nitride mask leaves the surface exposed; oxidant diffusing sideways under the mask edge produces a tapered wedge called the bird's beak, where the thick oxide thins into the gate oxide. In LOCOS technology the thick oxide is called field oxide. Shallow-trench isolation (STI) etches a trench around each active area and fills it with deposited oxide; the industry moved from LOCOS to trenches below about 0.35 µm. The isolation oxide is 100 to 1000 nm thick. LOCOS oxide is grown and STI fill is deposited, but in either case its trapping properties are poorly controlled and can differ considerably from those of the gate oxide, and it is not thinned by scaling.

The gate electrode of each transistor runs across the active area and continues up over the isolation oxide at both edges of the channel. Where it does, the gate, the isolation oxide and the silicon beneath form a second MOS transistor at each channel edge, in parallel with the intended one and sharing its source and drain. Before irradiation this parasitic transistor has a thick oxide and a threshold far above the supply voltage, so it never turns on. Positive charge trapped in the isolation oxide, and especially in the bird's beak or the STI sidewall next to the channel edge, gives it a large negative threshold shift. Once the shift is large enough, the p-type surface along the edge inverts at zero gate voltage and conducts from source to drain while the intended transistor is off.

The measured family on Deck 8 p. 41 shows exactly this signature. The turn-on of the intended transistor near +0.5 V and its on-state current near 10⁻⁴ A do not move between 0 and 50 krad, because its gate oxide is thin. Below threshold a second characteristic appears and moves to more negative gate voltage with dose, and the off-state current at zero gate voltage rises from about 10⁻¹² A to about 10⁻⁶ A. The trapped charge has not changed the transistor; it has added a second one beside it.

Several features follow from the geometry. The problem is confined to n-channel transistors: positive charge over n-type silicon accumulates the surface rather than inverting it, and raises the threshold of the parasitic p-channel device instead of lowering it. A second path runs beneath the isolation from the n⁺ source or drain of one transistor to a neighbouring n-well, so leakage appears between devices as well as within one. The worst bias during irradiation is the on state, gate at the supply voltage with source, drain and substrate grounded, because it places the largest field across the isolation oxide; the thinning of the oxide at the top corners of an STI trench concentrates that field further. The circuit symptom is a rise in static supply current, because every n-channel transistor on the die leaks at once; functional failure follows when the leakage overwhelms the circuit's margins. A dose of about 10 krad(Si) in the isolation oxide is enough to cause IC failure in some commercial devices, and charge in the isolation oxide is the main cause of total-dose failure in advanced commercial technologies.

The remedies act on the geometry or on the oxide, and belong to hardening (M16). An enclosed-layout, or annular, transistor surrounds the drain with the gate so that no isolation edge connects source to drain. A p⁺ guard ring or channel-stop implant raises the doping of the p-type surface beneath the isolation so that far more charge is needed to invert it. A hardened isolation process lowers the trap density itself; such recipes are generally proprietary. The Radiation Effects course treats all three in EEE 5416 M08 §5–6.

A commercial microcontroller with a gate oxide a few nanometres thick fails a cobalt-60 test at about 10 krad(Si). Its supply current rose by several orders of magnitude before the first logic error. Which oxide failed, by what mechanism, and why did the thin gate oxide not protect the part?

The isolation oxide failed. The gate oxide is thin enough that by (14.12) its shift is negligible, and below about 10 nm tunnelling removes most of what little charge it traps. The shallow-trench isolation around each transistor is hundreds of nanometres thick: the ratio of squared thicknesses alone is 10³ to 10⁴, and a deposited oxide traps a larger fraction of its holes than a hardened gate oxide.

Positive charge in the trench sidewall inverts the p-type edge of every n-channel transistor, opening a parasitic source-to-drain path in parallel with the intended channel. Each transistor's off-state current rises, and since all n-channel transistors share the effect, the static supply current of the chip rises by orders of magnitude first; logic errors appear only when leakage collapses the margins of the weakest nodes or starves circuits such as charge pumps. The thin gate oxide is irrelevant, because the failing oxide is a different one. The fixes are layout — enclosed transistors, guard rings — or a hardened isolation process.

8 · Testing a part for total dose

A part's total-dose tolerance is measured, not calculated. The trapped fraction of (14.5) and the interface-trap yield depend on processing details that vary between manufacturers and between wafer lots of the same part, so no datasheet parameter predicts them. A total-dose test irradiates parts under bias with a calibrated source, measures their electrical parameters at a series of dose steps, and then follows the anneal. Each element of the procedure answers one of the physical effects of §3–§6, and the logic of the standard test methods follows from them.

Sources

The reference source is cobalt-60, whose 1.173 and 1.332 MeV gamma rays (M04 §8, M10 §9) interact mainly by Compton scattering, and almost exclusively so in the light elements of the die. The dose is therefore nearly uniform through the die and, by (14.1), equal in silicon and in its oxide to 0.3 %. Low-energy photons scattered from the surroundings would still enhance the dose next to layers of high atomic number, so the part sits inside an enclosure of at least 1.5 mm of lead lined with at least 0.7 mm of aluminium, which ESCC 22900 recommends: it filters those photons and establishes charged-particle equilibrium at the die. MIL-STD-883 Test Method 1019 has used cobalt-60 exclusively since its 1991 revision; the European ESCC Basic Specification 22900 also allows an electron beam of at least 1 MeV.

Laboratory X-ray testers operating near 10 keV irradiate bare dies and whole wafers cheaply and without a radioactive source, and they appear in Figure 9. Their dose needs three corrections before it can be compared with cobalt-60: photoelectric absorption makes 1 rad(SiO₂) equal to about 1.8 rad(Si) (14.1); the low-energy secondary electrons ionize densely and give a lower fractional yield (Figure 5); and next to layers of high atomic number, such as metallization, the dose in the oxide is enhanced (EEE 5416 M04 §5). The correlation factor depends on the oxide field and on the layer stack, and is established experimentally for each technology. Caesium-137 irradiators, with a single 662 keV gamma ray, provide the lowest rates in Figure 9.

The test sequence

A standard irradiation delivers the dose at a rate high enough to finish in hours: 50–300 rad(Si)/s under Test Method 1019 (revision 1019.9, current as of 2019), and 0.1–50 rad(Si)/s under ESCC 22900. Parts are biased in the condition that gives the largest shift — for isolation leakage, the on state of §7. By §6, a high rate places the part at the left of Figure 9: in a gate oxide it overstates the oxide-trapped charge that a slow mission exposure would leave and understates the interface traps. The two MOS supplements correct for each error. An isolation oxide with a true low-dose-rate enhancement (§6) is outside that reasoning, because in it a high rate can understate the charge as well.

Tolerance levels

Published tests place many commercial parts in the low tens of krad(Si) or below. The 128-Mb NAND flash of §2 failed to erase at 8 krad(Si) when biased, and a multilevel NOR flash from the same study stopped functioning at 12 krad(Si) under bias; some commercial devices fail from isolation leakage at about 10 krad(Si); and commercial analog parts have failed as low as 1 krad(Si). Against that, more than 90 % of NASA missions before 2004 required a device tolerance of 100 krad(Si) or less, stated behind a nominal shield such as 70 mils (1.78 mm) of aluminium and with a radiation design margin of at least 2, often more to cover ELDRS and uncertainty in the environment. A mission's dose follows from its orbit and shield as in M07 §9.

Parts sold as radiation-hardened carry a letter giving the total dose to which each lot has been qualified. Under the US specification MIL-PRF-38535 the levels in krad(Si) are M 3, D 10, P 30, L 50, R 100, F 300, G 500 and H 1000; ESCC uses a different letter scheme, in which F denotes 50 krad(Si). A hardened processor such as the RAD750 of M03 §7 reaches hundreds of krad(Si) to about a megarad, depending on variant. A designation certifies a lot, a bias and a test method, not a universal property, and it says nothing about single-event effects.

What the test sequence encodes

Each step of a total-dose test corresponds to a mechanism on this page. The source is chosen so that rad(Si) and rad(SiO₂) coincide (§2). The bias is chosen to maximize the field that sets both the fractional yield and the destination of the holes (§3). The anneal steps exist because the two components of (14.8) evolve in opposite directions after exposure (§6), and the low-rate test exists because bipolar oxides do not follow the time-equivalence of MOS gate oxides. How a designer then makes a part pass — through the process, the layout, the circuit, the system or the shield — is the subject of M16, and the statistics of lot acceptance are developed in EEE 5416 M15.

Summary

  • Radiation effects divide into long-term effects — total ionizing dose and displacement damage, which grow with dose or fluence — and single-event effects, caused by one particle. Prompt dose-rate effects, from weapon pulses of 10⁸–10¹² rad(Si)/s, lie in the overlap.
  • Dose always names a material. At cobalt-60 energies 1 rad(Si) = 1 rad(SiO₂) to 0.3 %; under 10 keV X-rays 1 rad(SiO₂) ≈ 1.8 rad(Si).
  • TID symptoms are threshold shifts, leakage, timing changes and functional failure. A 128-Mb NAND flash failed to erase at 8 krad(Si), most likely because its internal charge pump, built from p-channel stages, lost output as their thresholds shifted; reading survived to higher doses than erasing and writing.
  • Mechanism: pairs form at 17 eV each (g0 = 8.1×10¹² cm⁻³ rad(SiO₂)⁻¹); a fraction fy escapes initial recombination, about 0.9 for cobalt-60 at 1 MV/cm and below 0.1 for alphas; electrons leave in about a picosecond; holes hop to the silicon; a fraction is trapped at oxygen vacancies within a few nm of the interface; released protons create interface traps over thousands of seconds.
  • ΔVth = ΔVot + ΔVit. ΔVot = −qΔNot/Cox at the interface, negative for both types; ΔVit is positive for n-channel and negative for p-channel. The terms oppose in n-channel devices and add in p-channel ones.
  • Interface traps also lower mobility and stretch the subthreshold swing; a threshold shift ΔVth multiplies the off-state current by 10−ΔVth/S.
  • ΔVot scales roughly as tox², with measured exponents of 1.5 to 2: thick oxides are the sensitive ones. Below about 10 nm, tunnelling removes trapped holes and a gate oxide is nearly immune.
  • Oxide-trapped charge anneals linearly in log t by tunnelling (a front advancing 0.2–0.4 nm per decade) and thermal emission; interface traps do not anneal at room temperature. MOS gate oxides normally show no true dose-rate effect — none across eleven decades in the hardened oxide of Figure 9 — only a time dependence, which produces rebound in n-channel devices. Bipolar linear parts show a true one, ELDRS, and so can some thick isolation oxides.
  • Charge in isolation oxides 100–1000 nm thick inverts the p-type edges of n-channel transistors and opens parasitic leakage paths; this is the dominant TID failure of modern commercial parts.
  • Tests use cobalt-60 at 50–300 rad(Si)/s (TM 1019) with a 50 % overtest and a 168 h, 100 °C biased bake for MOS, and low-rate tests for bipolar linear parts.
