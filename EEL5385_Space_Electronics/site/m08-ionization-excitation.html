Module 08

Ionization and Excitation

How a charged particle transfers its energy to the electrons of the material it crosses, how many mobile carriers that produces, and why a single particle can deliver a power density larger than a directed-energy weapon.

Prerequisites M01, M04 Read time ~40 min Sources Decks 4–5; Lectures 4–5

Part II established what arrives at a spacecraft and in what quantity. Part III turns to the second link in the course's chain: what happens when one of those particles enters solid matter. Every radiation effect in electronics — the accumulated degradation of total ionising dose, the instantaneous disturbance of a single-event upset, the permanent lattice damage of displacement — begins with an energy transfer from an incident particle to the atoms of the target, and there are only a few ways that transfer can happen.

This module covers the two that dominate: ionization, in which an electron is removed from an atom entirely, and excitation, in which it is promoted to a higher bound state. Both act through the Coulomb force, both apply only to charged incident particles, and together they account for almost all of the energy a charged particle loses. The module then follows the consequence through to a device — from energy deposited, to carriers generated, to charge collected, to current, to a power density — which is the calculation the course returns to throughout and the origin of the single-event effects treated in M15.

1 · The four charged-particle interactions

A charged particle moving through matter interacts continuously with the electrons and nuclei of the atoms surrounding its path, and it loses energy by four distinct mechanisms.

MechanismWhat the incident particle doesApplies toWhere treated
IonizationRemoves an electron from an atom entirely, creating an ion pairalphas, protons, heavy ions, betas§3 — the dominant channel
ExcitationPromotes an electron to a higher bound state without removing italphas, protons, heavy ions, betas§6
BremsstrahlungRadiates a photon when its own trajectory is deflectedprimarily betasM09
Cherenkov radiationRadiates light because it exceeds the speed of light in that mediumprimarily betas§1, below

Ionization is almost always the primary mechanism of energy loss, and for heavy charged particles it and excitation account for essentially all of it. Bremsstrahlung requires the incident particle to be deflected sharply, which happens appreciably only to electrons, and is the subject of the next module. A fifth channel sits outside this list because it is negligible as an energy loss and decisive as a damage mechanism: a rare collision with a nucleus rather than with the electron cloud, which displaces the atom permanently. That is M11, and the four mechanisms here all leave the crystal structure intact.

Cherenkov radiation completes the list and is the only one of the four that does not appear again. The speed of light in a transparent medium of refractive index n is c/n, which is less than c, and a charged particle can exceed it while remaining below c. When it does, the polarisation it induces in the medium radiates coherently in a cone behind it, producing the blue glow visible in the water of a reactor pool. The threshold is v > c/n, which for electrons in water requires about 260 keV. The energy carried away is a small fraction of one percent of the particle's collision losses, so Cherenkov radiation is negligible as an energy-loss channel and valuable as a detection mechanism, which is why it is listed and then set aside.

2 · The Coulomb force at atomic range

All four mechanisms are electromagnetic, and the force involved is the Coulomb force between the incident particle's charge and the charges within the atom.

F = k q1q2r²
(8.1)
F force between the charges (N) · k Coulomb constant, 9 × 109 N·m²/C² · q1 charge on the incident particle (C) · q2 charge on the struck particle (C) · r separation (m). This equation is the one place in the course that requires metres; every other length here is in centimetres or micrometres. Attraction or repulsion follows from the signs. Formula sheet §6

The inverse-square dependence is what makes a charged particle interact continuously rather than on impact. The force falls with distance but never reaches zero, so a charged particle passing through a solid exerts a measurable force on the electrons of every atom within some distance of its path — not only on atoms it strikes. Since a solid contains on the order of 1022 to 1023 atoms per cubic centimetre, a particle crossing even one micrometre passes within interaction range of an enormous number of them, and loses energy to each in a small increment.

The alternative is instructive. An atom is about 10−10 m across and its nucleus about 10−15 m, so the nucleus occupies roughly 10−15 of the atomic volume — fifteen nines of emptiness. Decks 4 and 5 quote the figure as 99.9999999 % empty space, which is nine nines and therefore six orders of magnitude less extreme; the conclusion drawn from it is unaffected, since either figure makes a direct nuclear hit overwhelmingly improbable. A particle with no charge exerts no Coulomb force, has no interaction with the electron cloud, and can only interact by striking that vanishingly small nucleus. This is the quantitative statement behind M04's penetration ordering: a charged particle interacts with everything it passes, an uncharged particle with almost nothing.

3 · Ionization

Ionization is the complete removal of one or more electrons from an atom by the Coulomb force of a passing charged particle. For it to occur, the energy the incident particle imparts to the electron must exceed the electron's binding energy — the energy holding it to the atom. The result is an ion pair: a free electron, the negative member, and the positively charged atom it left behind.

Three properties of the process follow, and each is used later.

Ionization occurs close to the track. Equation (8.1) makes the force strongest at small separation, so the electrons that receive enough energy to be removed are those nearest the particle's path. The deposited charge is therefore concentrated in a narrow column along the trajectory rather than spread through the material, and the diameter of that column — on the order of tens to hundreds of nanometres — is comparable with the dimensions of a modern transistor.

Each ionization slows the incident particle. The energy given to the electron comes out of the particle's kinetic energy, so the particle decelerates continuously as it travels. A particle of finite energy therefore has a finite range, and both the range and the rate of energy loss along it are computable. That computation is the subject of M12, and SRIM, the simulation program this course is built around, performs it for any ion and target (M13).

The freed electron may itself ionise. If the energy transferred exceeds the binding energy by a sufficient margin, the liberated electron carries away enough kinetic energy to ionise further atoms on its own account. That is the subject of §4.

4 · Delta rays

A delta ray is a secondary electron — the negative member of an ion pair — that has sufficient kinetic energy to cause additional ionization. The name identifies a role, not a species: a delta ray is an ordinary electron, distinguished only by having been liberated by a passing particle rather than emitted by a nucleus. A beta particle of the same energy behaves identically once it is in flight, and the two are distinguished only by provenance.

Delta rays matter for two reasons. They broaden the track: the primary particle deposits energy in a narrow column, and the delta rays it produces carry some of that energy outward before depositing it, giving the track a halo whose radius is set by the range of the most energetic secondaries. And they are the majority carrier-producing mechanism in a dense material — most of the electron–hole pairs created along a heavy-ion track are produced not by the ion itself but by the cascade of secondary electrons it liberates.

5 · Counting carriers in silicon

Converting deposited energy into a number of mobile carriers is the step that connects radiation physics to circuit behaviour, and it is done by dividing the deposited energy by a characteristic energy per carrier. Which characteristic energy to use is the one place where this course's convention and the radiation-effects literature differ, so both are given here.

The course's arithmetic. Decks 4 and 5 take the electron binding energy in silicon as 99.42 eV and note the excitation energy as 1.1 eV, the bandgap. A particle depositing more than 99.42 eV can ionise, and dividing an incident energy of 1 MeV by that figure gives approximately 104 ionizations per incident particle. This is the estimate the lectures use and the one to reproduce for the course's problems.

Npairs = EdepEpair
(8.2)
Npairs number of electron–hole pairs generated (dimensionless) · Edep energy deposited in the material (eV) · Epair mean energy per pair (eV) — 99.42 eV in this course's silicon estimate; the measured value for silicon is 3.6 eV, and for SiO₂ it is 17 eV. Only the energy actually deposited counts; a particle that passes through and leaves deposits only a fraction of its kinetic energy. Formula sheet §6

Source discrepancy — what 99.42 eV is. 99.4 eV is the binding energy of the silicon 2p core level, the standard photoelectron-spectroscopy line for elemental silicon; it is not the binding energy of the outermost orbital, which the slide labels it. The outermost electrons in a silicon crystal are the valence electrons, bound by a few electron-volts.

The quantity that determines collected charge, and the one used throughout the radiation-effects literature, is the mean electron–hole pair creation energy: 3.6 eV in silicon and about 17 eV in SiO₂. It exceeds the 1.1 eV bandgap because each ionizing event also loses energy to lattice vibrations and to sub-threshold excitations. Using 3.6 eV, 1 MeV deposited in silicon produces about 2.8 × 105 pairs rather than 104 — a factor of 28 more.

Both numbers appear here because the course is graded on the first and the physical world runs on the second. Use 99.42 eV when reproducing the course's estimate, and 3.6 eV whenever the answer must match a measurement, a datasheet or a SRIM output.

The two conventions do not conflict in the place it matters most. The professor's worked single-event example in §8 begins from an assumed 106 carriers generated by one heavy ion, and that figure is independently correct: an ion with a linear energy transfer of 30 MeV·cm²/mg deposits about 7 MeV per micrometre of track in silicon, which at 3.6 eV per pair gives close to 2 × 106 pairs. The starting point of the calculation is sound whichever route reaches it.

Linear energy transfer, the energy an ion deposits locally per unit length of track divided by the density of the material, is defined in M12 §4, and its equation (12.5) turns it into charge for any ion: in silicon each MeV·cm²/mg liberates 10.4 fC per micrometre, so an ion of 30 MeV·cm²/mg liberates 0.31 pC/µm. SRIM reports the energy an ion gives to ionization at each depth of a target, and dividing that energy by the pair creation energy gives the carrier count along the track (M13).

6 · Excitation

Excitation is the alternative outcome when the energy transferred is insufficient to remove the electron. The Coulomb force promotes an electron to a higher available energy state within the atom, leaving it bound. The atom is left excited, and it de-excites shortly afterwards by emitting a low-energy photon, typically in the ultraviolet. Like ionization, each excitation event removes energy from the incident particle and slows it.

Excitation occurs on average further from the particle's trajectory than ionization does, because the force required is smaller and equation (8.1) makes a smaller force available at a larger distance. The two processes therefore occupy concentric regions around the track: a dense ionised core and a wider excited halo.

In an isolated atom, the available higher states are discrete, so the de-excitation photons appear at sharp wavelengths that identify the element — the line spectra that make emission spectroscopy possible. In a solid the picture changes. As atoms are brought together, their outer orbitals overlap and each discrete level broadens into a band of closely spaced states. The highest filled band is the valence band, the lowest empty one the conduction band, and the gap between them is the bandgap (M01). Excitation in a semiconductor means promoting an electron across that gap: 1.1 eV in silicon.

7 · What the generated carriers do to a junction

A reverse-biased p–n junction depends for its behaviour on the near-absence of mobile carriers in its depletion region. M01 established that the reverse leakage current of a silicon diode is small precisely because carriers must be thermally generated across a 1.1 eV gap, and the thermal energy available at room temperature is 0.026 eV.

Radiation removes that constraint. Carriers generated by ionization or by excitation inside the depletion region find themselves in a strong electric field — the built-in field plus the applied reverse bias, typically 105 V/cm — and are swept apart immediately: electrons toward the n side, holes toward the p side. Each pair contributes to the reverse current, and the reverse current rises by orders of magnitude while the irradiation continues.

The device consequence is that a reverse-biased junction stops behaving like a diode and begins behaving like a resistor. The off state is no longer off. In a MOSFET this appears as increased leakage between drain and source and between gate and channel, degrading the switching behaviour; in a memory cell it appears as loss of the stored charge that represents the data; and in every case it appears as power dissipated where the design assumed none, which heats the device and raises the leakage further.

8 · From charge to current: the single-event chain

The calculation below is the one the course returns to in both lectures that cover this material, and it converts a single particle into a quantity a circuit designer recognises. It proceeds in four steps and each is a single multiplication.

Step 1 — carriers to charge. Assume one heavy ion generates 106 electron–hole pairs in the semiconductor. The total charge liberated is that number times the elementary charge: Q = 106 × 1.6 × 10−19 C = 1.6 × 10−13 C.

Step 2 — charge to current. The junction field sweeps that charge out in the time it takes carriers to drift across the depletion region, on the order of τ = 10−12 s. Current is charge per unit time, I = Q/τ = 1.6 × 10−13 / 10−12 = 0.16 A, which the lectures round down to 0.1 A and carry through the remaining steps. A tenth of an ampere through a single junction is enormous — many orders of magnitude above the reverse leakage current the junction was designed for, and large enough to initiate avalanche multiplication, in which the carriers themselves acquire enough energy between collisions to ionise further and multiply the current again.

I = Qτ = q Npairsτ
(8.3)
I transient current (A) · Q collected charge (C) · q elementary charge, 1.6 × 10−19 C · Npairs carriers generated · τ collection time (s), ~10−12 s in this course's estimate. Treats the transient as a rectangular pulse; a real collected-charge transient has a fast drift peak followed by a slower diffusion tail, so this gives the order of magnitude of the peak. Formula sheet §6

Step 3 — current to power. With the junction reverse-biased at 0.5 V, the instantaneous power dissipated is P = IV = 0.1 × 0.5 = 0.05 W.

Step 4 — power to power density. The charge was generated within the track, whose cross-section is of order 100 nm × 100 nm — an area of 10−10 cm². Dividing gives 0.05 W / 10−10 cm² = 5 × 108 W/cm², or 500 MW per square centimetre.

The comparison the lectures draw is with a directed-energy weapon: the Lockheed Martin system quoted in Deck 5 delivers roughly 50 MW/cm². That figure comes from the slide and is not independently verifiable — published material on such systems gives total beam power rather than on-target power density — so treat it as the order-of-magnitude comparison the lecture intends rather than as a specification. A single heavy ion deposits an order of magnitude more power density than that, and it does so inside the silicon rather than on its surface. The scale is why single-event effects include destructive as well as recoverable outcomes, and why the photograph accompanying the slide shows a commercial 120 V DC–DC converter with its power transistor burned through.

The power density is large because the denominators are small, not because the energy is. The total energy in the event is 0.05 W for a picosecond, which is 5 × 10−14 J — less than the energy of the incident ion. Concentrating it into 10−12 s and 10−10 cm² is what produces the number. This is the general principle behind every single-particle effect in electronics: the total energy involved is trivial, and the concentration in space and time is not. It is also why the effect worsens as devices shrink — the same deposited charge arrives at a node holding progressively less stored charge.

9 · Why this matters more as devices scale

M02 described the reduction of transistor dimensions and supply voltage across technology generations. Both trends act against single-event tolerance, and the mechanism is visible directly in the chain above.

The charge that a circuit node must lose before its stored logic state is corrupted is the critical charge, and it is approximately the node capacitance multiplied by the supply voltage. Scaling reduces both: smaller devices have smaller capacitances, and supply voltages have fallen from 5 V to below 1 V. The charge deposited by an incident ion, meanwhile, is set by the physics of the track and does not scale at all. A deposited charge that was a small perturbation on a 250 nm node is a decisive disturbance on a 5 nm one.

Two effects work in the opposite direction. A smaller device presents a smaller target area, so the probability that any given particle strikes a sensitive volume falls; and the smaller collection volume intercepts a shorter length of track, so less of the deposited charge is collected. The net outcome differs by technology and by effect type, and it is one reason the radiation-hardened processors of M03 are built on mature nodes — the larger critical charge, not any total-dose advantage, is what the older process buys. The quantitative treatment of upset rate against deposited charge is M15.

A 1 MeV proton and a 1 MeV iron ion each enter a silicon device. Both deposit their full energy. Which is more likely to cause a single-event upset, and why is the answer not simply "they deposit the same energy"?

Both deposit 1 MeV, so both liberate the same total number of carriers — about 2.8 × 105 using the measured 3.6 eV per pair. What differs is the volume that energy is deposited into, and upset depends on the charge collected at one node rather than on the charge liberated in the die.

The rate of energy loss along the track scales as the square of the incident charge, so the iron ion with Z = 26 loses energy some 676 times faster than the proton at the same velocity. Its range is correspondingly shorter — a 1 MeV iron ion stops within a fraction of a micrometre, while a 1 MeV proton travels about 16.5 µm. The iron ion therefore deposits its entire 1 MeV inside a volume comparable with a single transistor, delivering the whole 2.8 × 105 pairs to one node. The proton spreads the same charge along a track crossing many devices, so no single node collects more than a small fraction of it.

The quantity that captures this is the linear energy transfer — energy deposited per unit path length — and it, rather than total energy, is what single-event rate prediction is built on. It is developed in M12.

Summary

  • A charged particle loses energy by four mechanisms: ionization, excitation, bremsstrahlung and Cherenkov radiation. Ionization dominates; Cherenkov is negligible as an energy loss and useful for detection.
  • F = kq1q2/r² with k = 9 × 109 N·m²/C² and r in metres. Its infinite range is why a charged particle interacts with every atom it passes; an uncharged particle must strike a nucleus occupying ~10−15 of the atomic volume.
  • Ionization removes an electron entirely, creating an ion pair, occurs close to the track, and slows the incident particle at every event. Excitation promotes an electron to a higher bound state, occurs further from the track, and de-excites by emitting an ultraviolet photon.
  • A delta ray is a liberated electron energetic enough to ionise further. Delta rays produce most of the pairs along a heavy-ion track and set the track's radial extent.
  • Npairs = Edep/Epair. The course uses Epair = 99.42 eV, giving 104 pairs per MeV. The measured electron–hole pair creation energy is 3.6 eV in Si and 17 eV in SiO₂, giving 2.8 × 105 pairs per MeV; 99.4 eV is the Si 2p core-level binding energy.
  • Generated carriers in a reverse-biased depletion region are swept out by the junction field and raise the reverse current by orders of magnitude — the off state stops being off.
  • The single-event chain: 106 pairs → Q = 1.6 × 10−13 C → I = Q/τ = 0.16 A at τ = 1 ps, rounded to 0.1 A in the lectures → P = IV = 0.05 W at 0.5 V → 500 MW/cm² over a 10−10 cm² track. Carrying 0.16 A through instead gives 0.08 W and 800 MW/cm². The slide-quoted laser weapon is ~50 MW/cm².
  • The energy involved is trivial — about 5 × 10−14 J, or 8 × 10−14 J unrounded. The concentration in space and time is what causes the damage.
  • Scaling lowers critical charge through both smaller node capacitance and lower supply voltage, while the deposited charge does not scale. A mature node's single-event margin, not its total-dose behaviour, is what makes it attractive for space.
