Module 12

Energy Loss, Range, and the Bragg Peak

The quantitative layer under every charged-particle mechanism the course has covered: how fast a particle loses energy, how far it travels before stopping, how sharply the loss concentrates at the end of its path, and what linear energy transfer measures. These are the quantities a transport simulation computes.

Prerequisites M08, M11, M04 Read time ~40 min Sources Deck 5; NIST PSTAR; Ziegler, Biersack & Littmark

M08 established that a charged particle ionises and excites the atoms it passes, and M11 established that it can also strike a nucleus and displace an atom. Both modules described what happens in a single event. This module counts the events: it gives the rate at which a particle loses energy along its path, the distance that rate implies before the particle stops, and the way the rate changes as the particle slows.

Nothing here is a new mechanism. Everything is bookkeeping over the mechanisms already established, and that bookkeeping is what turns qualitative statements into numbers. It answers whether a given proton reaches the active layer of a die or stops in the package, how much charge a heavy ion deposits in a sensitive volume, and whether adding shielding helps or hurts. It is also the layer the course's simulation tool computes: the entire output of SRIM consists of stopping powers, ranges and the damage distributions that follow from them, and M13 is not usable without the definitions collected here.

One convention before starting. All quantities in this module describe a particle that carries charge. Photons have no stopping power and no range, and are handled by the attenuation law of M10 instead.

1 · Stopping power

Stopping power is the rate at which a charged particle loses kinetic energy with distance travelled. It is a property of the particle and the material jointly, and it varies continuously along the path because the particle slows as it goes.

S = − dEdx,   Sρ = − 1ρ dEdx
(12.1)
S linear stopping power (MeV/cm) · E particle kinetic energy (MeV) · x distance along the track (cm) · ρ density (g/cm³) · S/ρ mass stopping power (MeV·cm²/g). The minus sign makes S positive, since energy falls as distance grows. As with the attenuation coefficient of M10, dividing by density gives a quantity that is a property of the element rather than of a particular sample, which is why tables list the mass form.

The total stopping power separates into two independent contributions, because the particle can lose energy to the electrons of the target or to its nuclei, and the two have completely different magnitudes, energy dependences and consequences.

Stotal = Selec + Snuc
(12.2)
Selec electronic stopping power, energy lost to ionization and excitation of target electrons (MeV·cm²/g) · Snuc nuclear stopping power, energy lost in elastic collisions with screened target nuclei (MeV·cm²/g). For light ions at MeV energies the electronic term dominates by three or four orders of magnitude; the nuclear term matters only near the end of the track. The names refer to what the energy is given to, not to the nuclear force. A third term, radiative stopping from bremsstrahlung, is omitted here because it is negligible for ions at these energies; it dominates only for electrons above a few MeV, which is the regime of M09.

The names are about the recipient, not the force

Nuclear stopping is an electrostatic process. The incident ion and the target nucleus repel each other through a Coulomb interaction screened by their electron clouds, and the collision is elastic. The word nuclear identifies the target as a nucleus rather than an electron; it does not mean the strong nuclear force is involved, and it does not mean a nuclear reaction occurs. Reactions of that kind do happen, and are treated separately as nuclear interactions.

2 · Electronic stopping and the 1/v² dependence

Electronic stopping is the sum of the ionization and excitation events of M08, counted per unit path length. A typical event removes tens of electronvolts, so a particle of a few MeV undergoes tens of thousands of them. The distribution has a long tail: the largest transfer a 10 MeV proton can make to a free electron is 21.9 keV, and it is those rare large transfers that produce the escaping delta rays of §4. Because the number is so large, the loss is smooth and well described by an average rate. The Bethe formula gives that rate.

Selecρ = K ZA z²β² [ ln (2mec²β²γ²I) − β² ]
(12.3)
K = 0.307 MeV·cm²/mol, a combination of fundamental constants · Z, A atomic number and atomic weight of the target (A in g/mol) · z charge number of the incident particle (1 for a proton, 2 for an alpha, 26 for iron) · β = v/c, particle speed as a fraction of the speed of light · γ = 1/√(1 − β²), the Lorentz factor · mec² electron rest energy, 0.511 MeV · I mean excitation energy of the target (eV), an empirical material constant equal to 173 eV for silicon. Valid where the particle is fast enough to be fully stripped of its own electrons; it fails at low velocity, treated below.

Three dependences in equation (12.3) carry all of the physics, and each can be read directly from the mechanism.

The loss grows as the square of the incident charge. The Coulomb force the particle exerts on a target electron is proportional to z, the momentum it imparts is therefore proportional to z, and the energy transferred goes as the square of that momentum. An iron ion with z = 26 would lose energy 26² = 676 times faster than a proton of the same velocity if it were fully stripped. It is fully stripped only at high velocity: at 1 MeV per nucleon its effective charge gives a factor nearer 160, climbing towards the bare value as the energy rises. Either way the ratio is enormous, which is why heavy ions dominate single-event effects even though they are a small fraction of the flux in space.

The loss grows as the inverse square of the velocity, through the 1/β² factor. A slow particle spends longer in the vicinity of each atom it passes, so the Coulomb impulse delivered to that atom's electrons is larger — proportional to 1/v — and the energy transferred, which goes as the square of that impulse, is proportional to 1/v². This single factor is the origin of the Bragg peak of §6: as the particle slows, its rate of loss rises, which slows it further.

The loss is proportional to the electron density of the target, through Z/A multiplied by the density. This is the same Z/A ratio that appeared for Compton scattering in M10, and for the same reason: the target that matters is an electron, and the number of electrons per gram barely varies between elements.

The turnover at low energy is a limit on the Bethe formula rather than a correction to it, and equation (12.3) cannot describe it. The formula holds only while the projectile moves fast compared with the orbital velocities of the target's electrons. As that condition fails, the logarithm shrinks and then changes sign: evaluated with silicon's I = 173 eV, the expression as printed reaches its own maximum near 216 keV and is meaningless below about 79 keV. The real turnover lies further down still, at 55 keV. What happens physically is that fewer and fewer target electrons can be excited at all as the projectile slows past them, and the stopping power becomes proportional to velocity rather than to its inverse square — a result derived by Lindhard and Scharff. The tabulated silicon values follow that proportionality exactly between 1 and 10 keV. Effective-charge reduction, in which the projectile captures electrons and its z falls, is a real effect and matters for heavy ions, which have many electrons to capture; it is not what governs a proton, which has only one. Stopping-power tables and simulation codes therefore use fitted forms in this region rather than a closed expression.

3 · Nuclear stopping

Nuclear stopping is the energy lost in elastic collisions with whole atoms rather than with their electrons. Because a silicon nucleus is some fifty thousand times heavier than an electron, each such collision transfers far more energy but occurs far more rarely. Every nuclear-stopping event is also a candidate displacement event, so this term is the direct link to the damage of M11: it is the part of the energy loss that goes into moving atoms rather than into freeing charge.

Nuclear stopping is negligible at high energy and grows in relative importance as the particle slows. For protons in silicon it never actually overtakes electronic stopping: at 10 MeV the two are 34.58 and 0.018 MeV·cm²/g, so nuclear stopping is 0.05 % of the total, and even at 1 keV it is only about 10 %. For heavy ions the crossover does occur, because the nuclear term rises with the mass and charge of the projectile faster than the electronic term does, and it happens within the last part of the track.

Where the damage is, and why shielding can make things worse

Nuclear stopping peaks near the end of the range, precisely where the particle is depositing energy fastest. A particle that passes through a die leaves a thin track of ionization and few displacements; a particle that stops inside the die delivers both its ionization maximum and its displacement maximum into that volume. Placing shielding in front of a part slows the incident spectrum, and a particle that would have passed straight through can be brought to rest inside the sensitive region. This is the reversal established in M11 §5, and §6 gives it a quantitative shape.

Below the nuclear-stopping peak the term falls again. At very low velocity the electron clouds of the two atoms screen their nuclear charges so effectively that the interaction weakens, and the collision becomes soft. The result is a maximum in the nuclear stopping curve rather than a monotonic rise, though it lies below the energies of Figure 1 for protons.

4 · Linear energy transfer

Linear energy transfer, universally abbreviated LET, is the quantity the radiation-effects literature uses in place of stopping power, and the two are related but not identical. Stopping power counts all energy the particle loses. LET counts only the energy deposited locally, near the track.

The two differ because some ionization events produce delta rays — the energetic secondary electrons of M08 §4 — that travel far from the track and deposit their energy elsewhere. Restricted LET removes them by imposing an energy cut-off Δ: transfers above Δ are assumed to leave the track and are subtracted. Radiative loss does not enter the comparison at all, because LET is a restricted electronic stopping power and bremsstrahlung is outside that quantity by definition.

LETΔ = (− dEdx)Δ
(12.4)
LETΔ restricted linear energy transfer (MeV·cm²/mg when divided by density, or keV/µm) · Δ the cut-off energy above which a secondary electron is considered to have escaped the track (eV or keV). With Δ = ∞ the restricted LET equals the electronic stopping power. In practice the radiation-effects literature quotes unrestricted LET and treats it as equal to electronic stopping power, which is accurate for heavy ions at the energies that matter for single-event effects.

The unit of LET in this field is MeV·cm²/mg, milligrams rather than grams, which makes the numerical values convenient: a heavy ion of interest for single-event testing has an LET of order 1 to 100 MeV·cm²/mg. Converting to a linear rate requires the density, and converting to collected charge requires the electron–hole pair creation energy of 3.6 eV established in M08.

Q′ = LET · ρ · qEehp = 10.4 fCµm per MeV·cm²mg
(12.5)
Q′ charge liberated per unit track length (C/cm, or fC/µm) · LET linear energy transfer (MeV·cm²/mg) · ρ density (mg/cm³; 2330 for silicon) · q elementary charge, 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ C · Eehp electron–hole pair creation energy, 3.6 eV in silicon. The numerical value 10.4 fC/µm is specific to silicon and follows from its density and pair energy; equivalently, an LET of 96.4 MeV·cm²/mg liberates 1 pC/µm. This is the bridge from a tabulated LET to the critical charge of a circuit node, used in M15.

5 · Range, projected range, and straggling

Because a charged particle loses energy continuously, it comes to rest after a definite distance. That distance follows from the stopping power by integration: the particle travels dE/S while giving up an increment dE, and summing those increments from the initial energy down to zero gives the path length.

RCSDA = ∫0E0 dEStotal(E)
(12.6)
RCSDA range in the continuous-slowing-down approximation (g/cm²; divide by ρ for cm) · E0 initial kinetic energy (MeV) · Stotal total mass stopping power at energy E (MeV·cm²/g). The approximation treats energy loss as perfectly smooth, ignoring the statistical spread of individual events. It gives the average path length travelled, measured along the trajectory.

Path length is not the same as penetration depth. Collisions with target electrons barely deflect a heavy ion, because it is thousands of times the electron's mass, but the comparatively rare screened-Coulomb collisions with nuclei described in §3 turn it measurably. Its path therefore wanders, and the straight-line distance from entry to stopping point is shorter than the distance travelled. That straight-line distance, measured along the original direction of motion, is the projected range, and it is the quantity an engineer needs when asking whether a particle reaches a buried layer.

fd = RprojRCSDA
(12.7)
fd detour factor (dimensionless, 0 to 1) · Rproj projected range (g/cm²) · RCSDA CSDA range (g/cm²). A value near 1 means the track is nearly straight. For protons in silicon fd = 0.995 at 10 MeV and 0.27 at 1 keV: a fast heavy particle travels almost in a straight line, a slow light one does not. For electrons the detour factor is far smaller at all energies, because an electron is light enough to be turned through large angles by elastic scattering from target nuclei, which is what dominates the wandering.

Neither range is a sharp cut-off. Energy loss is a statistical process, so two identical particles entering the same material stop at slightly different depths. The spread is called straggling, and it has a longitudinal component along the beam and a lateral component across it. Longitudinal straggling for MeV protons in silicon is of order one percent of the range, which is why an ion beam can be used to place a dose at a chosen depth with useful precision. For electrons straggling is so large that a range is barely a useful concept at all.

For protons in silicon, the tabulated ranges give a direct sense of scale. A 1 MeV proton travels 16.5 µm, a 10 MeV proton 715 µm, and a 100 MeV proton 4.2 cm. The first stops within the top layers of a die, the second crosses the 600 µm die thickness the course adopts but would not clear a 725 µm wafer, and the third passes through a spacecraft wall and everything behind it. Trapped-belt protons span this whole range, which is why the orbit tables of M07 matter so directly to part selection.

6 · The Bragg peak

The 1/β² factor of equation (12.3) means a particle loses energy faster the slower it goes. That creates a runaway near the end of the track: the particle slows, the stopping power rises, it slows faster still, and almost all of its remaining energy is given up in a short final distance. Plotting energy deposited per unit depth against depth therefore gives a curve that is nearly flat for most of the path and then rises to a sharp maximum just before the particle stops. That maximum is the Bragg peak.

The peak-to-entrance ratio depends on the particle and the energy, and it is largest for the lightest ions. For the 10 MeV proton of Figure 3 it is 15.9. Measured in silicon at a common 9.3 MeV per nucleon, it falls monotonically with ion mass: about 3.1 for nitrogen, 2.6 for neon, 1.9 for argon, 1.6 for iron and 1.2 for xenon. The reason is effective charge. A heavy ion begins capturing electrons at a higher velocity than a light one, so its charge is already falling well before the end of the track and the peak is blunted. That is why a heavy ion's LET can be treated as roughly constant across a sensitive volume in single-event work, while a proton's cannot. For electrons the peak is absent altogether, because their scattering is severe enough that a well-defined depth does not exist.

The 15.9 figure is also a single-particle value. Across a beam, straggling spreads the stopping depth by about one percent of the range, and convolving the curve of Figure 3 with that spread reduces the peak-to-entrance ratio to roughly 5. A single ion crossing one sensitive volume sees the sharp value; a therapy beam or a flux of identical particles sees the smeared one.

Two consequences follow, and they point in opposite directions. In proton therapy the Bragg peak is the entire purpose of the technique: the incident energy is chosen so that the peak lands on the tumour, sparing the tissue in front and leaving essentially nothing behind. In a spacecraft the same concentration is a hazard, because a shield that removes just enough energy to place the peak inside the die maximises the damage rather than minimising it. Effective shielding must either stop the particle well outside the sensitive volume or leave it energetic enough to pass through.

7 · Scaling with mass and charge

Equation (12.3) depends on the incident particle only through its charge number z and its velocity. That permits any ion's stopping power to be obtained from a proton table, provided the comparison is made at equal velocity rather than at equal energy. Equal velocity means equal kinetic energy per nucleon, E/M.

Sion(E) ≈ z² Sp(EM),   Rion(E) ≈ Mz² Rp(EM)
(12.8)
Sion, Rion stopping power and range of the ion · Sp, Rp the same quantities for a proton from a table · E ion kinetic energy (MeV) · M ion mass number (dimensionless) · z ion charge number. Valid only where both particles are fully stripped, so it degrades badly near the end of the range and for heavy ions at low energy, which is one of the reasons a dedicated ion code is used instead.

Applying this to an alpha particle, M = 4 and z = 2, gives Rα(E) ≈ Rp(E/4): an alpha has roughly the range of a proton with a quarter of its energy. A 5.5 MeV alpha from a typical decay therefore penetrates about as far as a 1.4 MeV proton, which the proton table puts at 26.6 µm in silicon. The tabulated alpha value is 27.8 µm; the scaling falls 4 % short because it ignores the low-energy end of the track, where neither particle is fully stripped, and a fuller form of equation (12.8) adds a constant to absorb exactly that. Either number explains why alpha contamination in package materials affects only the topmost layers of a die.

Range also scales strongly with energy. Over the MeV decade the tabulated proton ranges give 16.5 µm at 1 MeV and 715 µm at 10 MeV, a factor of 43 for a factor of 10 in energy, and 715 µm to 4.18 cm from 10 to 100 MeV, a factor of 58. The implied power law is R ∝ E1.6 to E1.8. Doubling the energy of an incident proton roughly triples its penetration.

A die is covered by 50 µm of silicon overlayer and passivation. Trapped protons arrive with a spectrum extending from 1 to 100 MeV. Which part of that spectrum can deposit its Bragg peak in the active silicon within the first few micrometres below the overlayer, and what does that imply for the damage such a shield produces?

A proton stops at its range. To stop just past 50 µm requires a range of roughly 50–55 µm, which from the scaling R ∝ E1.6 and the 16.5 µm figure at 1 MeV corresponds to about 2.1 MeV: the tabulated range at 2 MeV is 48 µm. Only a narrow slice of the spectrum, near 2 MeV, places its peak in that volume.

Everything below about 2 MeV is absorbed harmlessly in the overlayer. From about 4–5 MeV upward a proton crosses the active layer at close to its entrance stopping power and deposits comparatively little; between 2 and 4 MeV it is elevated but not at its peak. The overlayer therefore converts a narrow band of the incident spectrum into the worst possible case, and removes a broader band entirely. Whether it helps depends on how much flux sits in each band — which is exactly the calculation a transport simulation performs, and the reason shielding decisions are not made by inspection.

8 · What a transport simulation computes

Equations (12.1) to (12.8) describe an average particle. They do not give the distribution of stopping depths, the spatial pattern of vacancies, the trajectories of recoiling target atoms, or what happens in a layered target where each layer has a different density and composition. Those require following individual particles and accumulating statistics over many of them, which is what a Monte Carlo transport code does.

A binary-collision Monte Carlo treats the ion's history as a sequence of independent two-body encounters. At each step it samples an impact parameter at random, computes the deflection and the energy transferred using the screened Coulomb potential of §3, subtracts the electronic energy loss accumulated between collisions using a stopping formula, and repeats until the ion falls below a cut-off energy. Any target atom that receives more than the displacement threshold of M11 is followed in turn as a recoil, so a single incident ion generates a tree of trajectories. Running thousands of ions builds distributions rather than averages.

This is why the tool exists, and it is why the quantities in this module are the ones it reports: ion range and its longitudinal and lateral straggling, energy given to ionization against energy given to recoils, vacancy production against depth, and energy ending as lattice vibration. M13 covers operating the code and reading those outputs. Every one of them is a quantity defined in the sections above.

What the quantities in this module do not capture

Stopping power and range describe electromagnetic energy loss. They say nothing about nuclear reactions, in which an incident proton or neutron transforms the target nucleus and releases secondary particles with their own ranges and their own damage. Such reactions are rare compared with electronic stopping but they can deposit far more energy in one place. Down to about 90 nm they are the dominant cause of single-event upset from protons, whose direct ionization is too weak to exceed the critical charge of a node that size. At 65 nm and below the critical charge has fallen far enough that direct proton ionization is measurable and often dominant, so the classical picture no longer holds across the board. A binary-collision code models neither channel.

Summary

  • Stopping power S = −dE/dx is the rate of energy loss with distance; the mass form S/ρ in MeV·cm²/g is what tables list.
  • S splits into electronic, energy given to target electrons, and nuclear, energy given elastically to target atoms. Electronic dominates by three to four orders of magnitude for light ions at MeV energies; nuclear is what produces displacements.
  • The Bethe formula gives Selec ∝ z²/β² times the target electron density. Charge squared is why heavy ions matter; inverse velocity squared is why the Bragg peak exists.
  • The Bethe formula fails at low velocity, where the ion captures electrons and its effective charge falls. Proton stopping in silicon peaks at 549 MeV·cm²/g at 55 keV and falls below that on both sides.
  • LET is the locally deposited part of the energy loss, quoted in MeV·cm²/mg. In silicon, 1 MeV·cm²/mg liberates 10.4 fC/µm; 96.4 MeV·cm²/mg liberates 1 pC/µm.
  • CSDA range is path length; projected range is penetration depth; their ratio is the detour factor, 0.995 for a 10 MeV proton in silicon and far smaller for electrons. Neither is a sharp cut-off, because of straggling.
  • Protons in silicon: 16.5 µm at 1 MeV, 715 µm at 10 MeV, 4.2 cm at 100 MeV, with R ∝ E1.6 to E1.8.
  • The Bragg peak concentrates the rate of loss at the end of the track — a factor of 15.9 above entrance for a single 10 MeV proton in silicon, falling to about 5 once straggling is averaged over a beam, and falling further for heavier ions, to about 1.6 for iron. A shield that places the peak inside the die maximises damage instead of reducing it.
  • At equal velocity, S ∝ z² and R ∝ M/z², so any ion's behaviour follows approximately from a proton table evaluated at E/M.
