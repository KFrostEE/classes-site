Module 07

Orbits and the Environment They See

Where each class of orbit sits relative to the trapped belts, how orbital speed changes the exposure at a given altitude, and why the most heavily used navigation orbit is also the worst radiation environment near Earth.

Prerequisites M06 Read time ~40 min Sources Decks 4–5; Lectures 4–5

M06 established where the trapped populations are: an inner proton belt between roughly 1.2 and 2.5 Earth radii, a depleted slot, and a variable outer electron belt from 3 to 7. Those boundaries are fixed by the geometry of the geomagnetic field and by the physics of trapping. What an individual spacecraft experiences depends on where its orbit intersects them and on how fast it is moving when it does.

This module converts the belt map into a design input. It defines the orbit classes by altitude, derives the two relations that fix orbital speed and period, works through the intersection of each orbit class with the belts, and presents the radiation-risk comparison the course uses. It closes on the case that makes the subject concrete: the Global Positioning System operates in the heart of the outer electron belt, for reasons that have nothing to do with radiation and cannot be traded away.

1 · The orbit classes

Orbits near Earth are grouped by altitude, with the boundaries set by where the trapped belts and the geosynchronous radius fall rather than by anything intrinsic to orbital mechanics.

A geosynchronous orbit is one whose period matches Earth's rotation, so the spacecraft returns to the same point above the surface once per day. A geostationary orbit is the special case that is also circular and equatorial, so the spacecraft appears motionless from the ground. The distinction matters operationally — a ground antenna can be fixed only for the geostationary case — and not at all for radiation, since the altitude is the same.

2 · Speed, period, and why they are not free choices

Orbital altitude is not an independent design variable. Fixing it fixes both the period and the speed, through two relations that follow from balancing gravitational attraction against the centripetal acceleration of circular motion.

v = GMr     T = 2π r³GM
(7.1)
v orbital speed for a circular orbit (m/s) · T orbital period (s) · r orbital radius measured from Earth's centre (m) — add the Earth radius 6,371 km to any quoted altitude · GM standard gravitational parameter of Earth, 3.986 × 1014 m³/s². Circular orbits only; the elliptical generalisation is equation (7.2). Formula sheet §6

Speed falls as the inverse square root of radius, so a spacecraft in geostationary orbit moves at 3.07 km/s against 7.66 km/s for the International Space Station — less than half the speed, six times the radius. Period rises as the three-halves power, from 93 minutes to 24 hours over the same range. For a radiation analysis both matter: the period sets how often a repeating orbit re-enters a hazardous region, and the speed sets how long it stays each time.

For an elliptical orbit the speed is no longer constant, and the relation that replaces it is the vis-viva equation. It expresses the fact that the sum of kinetic and gravitational potential energy is fixed by the semi-major axis alone.

v² = GM ( 2r − 1a )
(7.2)
v instantaneous speed (m/s) · r current distance from Earth's centre (m) · a semi-major axis, half the sum of the perigee and apogee radii (m) · GM = 3.986 × 1014 m³/s². Reduces to equation (7.1) when r = a. The period depends on a only, so a circular and an elliptical orbit with the same semi-major axis share a period. Formula sheet §6

3 · Where each orbit meets the belts

Laying the orbit altitudes against the belt boundaries of M06 produces the whole comparison, and the four cases are qualitatively different.

Low Earth orbit lies below the inner belt. At 400–600 km the spacecraft is beneath the trapped proton population everywhere except over the South Atlantic, where the offset dipole brings the belt down to meet it. Trapped exposure is therefore concentrated into a few passes per day. Solar energetic particles are largely excluded at low inclination by the geomagnetic cut-off, but reach the spacecraft freely over the poles, so a polar low orbit sees a solar particle environment that an equatorial one does not.

Medium Earth orbit spans both belts. Its band, 2,000 to 35,786 km, holds two different environments. Below about 10,000 km an orbit passes through the inner belt, whose protons reach hundreds of MeV; this is the band the NASA table of §4 calls medium Earth orbit, "sometimes called high LEO", and rates as the highest proton exposure near Earth. The Global Positioning System flies far above it, at 20,200 km, where electrons set the environment.

Placing an orbit in the belts needs one more property of the dipole field. A trapped particle drifts around the Earth on a shell of field lines (M06 §5), and each shell is labelled by the distance, in Earth radii, at which its field lines cross the magnetic equator; the radii of the belts in M06 §6 are these labels. Every point of a dipole field line satisfies equation (7.3), which therefore maps each point of an orbit onto its shell.

L = rcos²λ
(7.3)
L shell label: the distance from Earth's centre at which the field line through the point crosses the magnetic equator (Earth radii) · r distance of the point from Earth's centre (Earth radii) · λ magnetic latitude of the point. For a centred dipole; the tilt and offset of M06 §1 change which geographic position lies on which shell. Particles on one shell drift around the whole Earth, so an orbit's exposure follows from the range of L it covers and from where along each field line it sits: away from the equator the field is stronger and only particles that mirror beyond that point reach it. Trapped-particle models are mapped in these two coordinates, the field strength and L. On the formula sheet as (7.3).

A GPS satellite moves at r = 4.17 Earth radii in a plane inclined 55° to the equator, so (7.3) gives L ≥ 4.17 everywhere on its orbit. It never enters the inner belt, whose protons above about 10 MeV are concentrated inside L ≈ 3 and fade out by L ≈ 4. Near the magnetic equator it crosses the heart of the outer electron belt. Beyond about 40° of magnetic latitude (7.3) gives L > 7, outside the belt, rising to L ≈ 13 at the highest point of the orbit. For a centred dipole aligned with the rotation axis the satellite is outside the belt for 43 % of the time. The real field's tilt, about 9°, changes the fraction from one orbit to the next, between about a third and a half, and lifts the highest shell on some orbits to L ≈ 22; Lecture 4 makes the same point, that the tilted medium orbits leave the belt for part of each revolution. The orbit's hazard is therefore the outer belt's electrons. The course's fluence plot in M05 puts the medium-orbit electron curve at the top, and dose measurements from the particle instruments on the GPS satellites find the protons' contribution minor (Chen et al. 2021).

Geostationary orbit sits at the outer edge of the outer belt. At 6.6 Earth radii it lies beyond the energetic trapped protons. Protons are trapped there too, but their spectrum is soft: at 1 MeV their flux is about a thousand times below that of the trapped electrons (Borovsky et al. 2016), and the thinnest shielding stops them, since a 1 MeV proton's range in aluminium is 15 µm. The NASA table therefore records no trapped protons at geostationary altitude, and the dose–depth curves of M14a §2 carry no trapped-proton component there. Its trapped-particle hazard is the outer belt's electrons, which dominate the dose behind thin shielding. Exposure to solar energetic particles is unobstructed, because the spacecraft is near the magnetopause boundary where the geomagnetic shielding is weak. Its proton risk is therefore low at solar minimum and moderate at solar maximum, set by solar activity rather than by trapped protons.

A highly elliptical orbit crosses everything. A Molniya orbit with perigee near 500 km and apogee near 40,000 km passes through the inner belt, the slot and the outer belt twice per revolution, and does so twice a day. It sees both trapped populations and the solar particle environment at apogee. What saves it is speed, which is the subject of §5.

Three features of the table are worth extracting, because each contradicts an assumption that altitude alone would suggest.

Higher is not worse. Geostationary orbit, at 35,786 km, carries a far lower proton risk than the table's medium orbit below 10,000 km, because it lies outside the energetic trapped protons rather than inside them. Radiation exposure is set by belt geometry, not by distance from the ground.

Medium Earth orbit and highly elliptical orbit swap their worst case. The table's medium orbit, the inner-belt band, is worst at solar minimum, highly elliptical orbit at solar maximum. That medium orbit is dominated by trapped protons, which peak at solar minimum for the atmospheric reason established in M06. A highly elliptical orbit spends much of its period at high altitude where solar particles reach it directly, so it follows solar activity instead. A mission's worst case is therefore at a different phase of the cycle depending on which orbit it flies.

Duration is part of the environment. The Shuttle row records a very low risk for an orbit that is otherwise identical to the low-orbit row above it, because a mission of two weeks accumulates two weeks of fluence. The environment is a rate; the design driver is the integral of that rate over the mission.

The ranking depends on the hazard. The table ranks one hazard, proton single events. The course's eleven-year fluence plot (Deck 3 p. 23, redrawn in M05 §9) ranks the species separately, and the two orders differ. In electrons, GPS and the Molniya orbit receive the largest fluence, geostationary orbit nearly as much, and low Earth orbit about a hundred times less. In protons above about 20 MeV, the energies that penetrate shielding, the order reverses: low Earth orbit, which crosses the inner belt in the South Atlantic Anomaly, receives the most, the Molniya orbit, whose perigee passes cross the inner belt, comes next, and GPS and geostationary orbit receive the least, more than ten times below low orbit's at 100 MeV. At 0.1 MeV the outer-belt orbits receive hundreds to thousands of times more protons than low orbit; the excess shrinks with energy, to a few times (geostationary orbit) and a few hundred times (GPS) at 1 MeV, and these soft protons are stopped by the thinnest shielding. Low Earth orbit is therefore the lowest-risk orbit for electron dose, the inner-belt band of medium orbit the highest-risk orbit for penetrating protons, and GPS high in electron risk and low in energetic-proton risk.

Which orbit the table's medium-orbit row describes

Lectures 4 and 5 attach the table's medium-orbit rating to the GPS orbit: medium Earth orbit, where the GPS satellites fly, passes through the belt and carries a very high radiation risk. GPS's radiation risk is indeed high, but it comes from the outer belt's electrons (§3). The guideline's own row reads "MEO (or sometimes called high LEO)" and sets the slot apart as a separate, moderate case, so its very high proton rating belongs to the inner-belt band below about 10,000 km. In the course's usage, medium Earth orbit, GPS included, is rated very high at solar minimum and high at solar maximum; physically, GPS's proton risk is low and its electron risk high.

5 · Why an elliptical orbit is cheaper than its altitude suggests

A highly elliptical orbit passes through the most intense part of the trapped belts twice per revolution and still carries a lower daily proton exposure than medium Earth orbit. The reason is in equation (7.2): on an eccentric orbit the speed varies with position, and it is highest exactly where the belts are.

Take a Molniya orbit with perigee at 600 km and apogee at 39,800 km. The semi-major axis is 26,571 km, giving a 12-hour period. Equation (7.2) returns 10.0 km/s at perigee and 1.5 km/s at apogee — a factor of 6.7. The spacecraft crosses the belts near perigee, where it is moving fastest, and spends the bulk of its 12 hours near apogee, far outside them. A circular medium orbit at the same mean radius moves at a uniform 3.87 km/s and never leaves the belts at all.

This is the same physical statement as Kepler's second law: the line from Earth to the spacecraft sweeps equal areas in equal times, so the spacecraft must move quickly when it is close. It is also why a highly elliptical orbit is used at all. Its purpose is to provide long dwell times over high latitudes, which a geostationary satellite cannot serve because it sits above the equator and appears too low on the horizon from polar regions. The slow apogee that makes the orbit useful for communications is the same slow apogee that keeps it out of the belts.

A Molniya orbit is inclined at 63.4°, and the value is not arbitrary. Earth's equatorial bulge perturbs an orbit so that the perigee point rotates within the orbital plane, which would move the slow apogee away from the intended hemisphere over a few years. At 63.4° the perturbation vanishes to first order, so the apogee stays over the northern hemisphere for the life of the mission. This is the critical inclination, and it is a rare case in which a geophysical constraint fixes an orbital parameter exactly.

6 · Why GPS accepts the outer electron belt

The Global Positioning System operates at 20,200 km altitude, where §3 placed it in the heart of the outer electron belt for much of each orbit, and where the course's fluence plot records, with the Molniya orbit, the largest electron fluence of the orbits it shows. That choice was made deliberately and cannot be revised, and the reasons are worth following because they are the clearest example in the course of radiation losing a design trade.

Navigation requires several satellites visible at once. A receiver determines its position by measuring the time each satellite's signal took to arrive and multiplying by the speed of light to get a distance. Three such distances intersect at a point, so three satellites would suffice if the receiver had a perfect clock. It does not: a receiver clock error of one nanosecond corresponds to 30 cm of range error, and no consumer device carries an atomic clock. A fourth measurement lets the receiver solve for its own clock offset as a fourth unknown alongside the three coordinates. Four satellites must therefore be simultaneously above the horizon, everywhere on Earth, at all times.

Altitude buys visibility. The higher a satellite, the larger the area of Earth from which it can be seen. Deck 4 records the consequence: a medium-orbit constellation can give stable coverage of almost the entire globe with as few as six satellites, and its sensors have far wider fields of view than those in low orbit. GPS flies more than that — twenty-four operational satellites in six planes — precisely because the requirement is four simultaneously visible rather than one, but the scaling is the point. Providing the same service from low Earth orbit would take hundreds of satellites, and each would be visible for only minutes at a time.

The orbit must last. GPS satellites are built for long service: the earlier blocks carried a 7.5-year design life and the current ones 12 to 15 years, and in practice many have operated well beyond twenty. At low altitude residual atmospheric drag removes energy from the orbit continuously, so a low-orbit satellite must carry propellant and expend it on reboost for its whole life, or accept re-entry. At 20,200 km there is no measurable atmosphere. Deck 4 lists this directly: medium orbit is less fuel-intensive to operate than low orbit and easier to maintain station in.

The result is that the orbit is fixed by coverage geometry, constellation economics and mission lifetime, and the radiation environment is a consequence to be engineered around rather than a parameter to be traded. Navigation satellites accordingly carry radiation-hardened parts, generous shielding on critical boxes, and a design dose in the hundreds of kilorad — the compensating expenditure that M03 described in the abstract, arising here from a concrete and unavoidable requirement.

7 · Inclination, and the polar exception

Altitude alone does not determine the environment; the angle between the orbital plane and the equator matters as well. M06 established that the geomagnetic cut-off — the minimum energy a charged particle needs to reach a given location from outside — is highest at the equator and falls to zero over the magnetic poles, where field lines run vertically and offer a direct path inward.

A low Earth orbit at low inclination therefore sees essentially no solar energetic particles, and the risk table records exactly that: solar particles "No" for low-inclination low orbit, "Yes" for polar low orbit. The two rows sit at the same altitude and differ only in the plane of the orbit. A polar orbit passes through the open field-line regions on every revolution and accumulates solar-particle exposure that an equatorial orbit at the same altitude never sees. Since sun-synchronous orbits — used by almost all Earth-observation missions because they cross the equator at a fixed local solar time — are necessarily near-polar, this exposure applies to a large fraction of operational low-orbit spacecraft.

8 · Beyond the magnetosphere

At the Lagrange points and in interplanetary space the trapped populations are simply absent: there is no magnetic field to trap anything. The table records no trapped protons for Lagrange-point missions, and the risk drops to low at solar minimum and moderate at maximum, entirely determined by solar particle events.

Interplanetary space is the one entry in the table with a range rather than a value, low to high at both phases of the cycle, and the range is genuine. During the cruise phase the environment is solar particles plus galactic cosmic rays, and the solar component falls with distance from the Sun as the inverse square, so an outer-planet mission has a quieter cruise than an inner-planet one. On arrival the environment is whatever the destination provides. Jupiter has a magnetic field roughly twenty thousand times Earth's magnetic moment and correspondingly severe trapped belts; the Juno spacecraft carries its electronics inside a titanium vault for this reason. Mars has no global field at all, and a spacecraft there is exposed to the unmodified interplanetary environment while gaining a little shielding from the thin atmosphere. The table's instruction to evaluate planetary exposure case by case is the only correct general statement.

9 · From orbit to a number

A radiation analysis converts the orbit into a single set of design numbers by integrating along the trajectory. The orbit is propagated for the mission duration; at each point the trapped-particle models are evaluated for the local field coordinates; the solar particle contribution is added using the 7-year-maximum, 4-year-minimum convention of M05; and the galactic cosmic-ray contribution is added at the appropriate modulation level. The result is a mission-integrated fluence spectrum for each species.

That spectrum is then transported through a model of the spacecraft structure to give two design quantities: a total ionising dose behind a stated shield thickness, quoted in krad(Si), which drives parts selection for cumulative degradation; and a linear energy transfer spectrum, which drives the single-event rate prediction. Both depend on how the intervening material redistributes the incident spectrum, and that redistribution is the subject of Part III, beginning with M08.

A constellation must provide continuous coverage of the Arctic. Compare a highly elliptical orbit against a geostationary orbit and against a polar low Earth orbit, on coverage and on radiation.

Geostationary orbit fails on coverage. A geostationary satellite sits above the equator at 35,786 km. Seen from the Arctic it is close to the horizon or below it, so the link is obstructed by terrain and passes through a long slant path of atmosphere. Its proton environment is the best of the three — outside the energetic trapped protons, low risk at solar minimum — though its electron dose behind thin shielding is high, and in any case the service cannot be delivered.

A polar low orbit covers the Arctic but not continuously. At 800 km each satellite is visible for minutes per pass, so continuous coverage requires a large constellation and handover between satellites. Radiation exposure is moderate: below the belts except over the South Atlantic, but with full solar-particle access through the polar cap, and with drag making the orbit expensive to maintain over a long mission.

A highly elliptical orbit is the conventional answer. Apogee is placed over the northern hemisphere, and equation (7.2) makes the spacecraft slow there, so it dwells high over the Arctic for most of its 12-hour period and two or three satellites give continuous coverage. The radiation cost is real — the orbit crosses both belts twice per revolution and the table rates it high to very high — but the perigee crossing is fast, so the daily accumulated exposure is below that of a circular medium orbit. The design response is to harden the spacecraft rather than to change the orbit, exactly as for GPS. This is why the Molniya orbit has been used for high-latitude communications since the 1960s.

Summary

  • Orbital speed and period are fixed by radius: v = √(GM/r), T = 2π√(r³/GM), with GM = 3.986 × 1014 m³/s² and r measured from Earth's centre. For eccentric orbits, v² = GM(2/r − 1/a).
  • LEO is below the belts except over the South Atlantic. MEO below about 10,000 km lies in the inner proton belt. GPS, at 20,200 km, has L = r/cos²λ ≥ 4.17 (7.3), so it never enters the energetic proton belt; it crosses the heart of the outer electron belt and is beyond L = 7 about 40 % of the time. GEO sits at the outer edge, beyond the energetic trapped protons; its own trapped protons are soft. HEO crosses both belts twice per revolution.
  • The course's risk table reports proton single-event risk: MEO (the inner-belt band) very high at solar minimum and high at maximum; HEO high then very high; GEO and Lagrange points low then moderate; low-inclination LEO moderate then low–moderate.
  • The ranking depends on the hazard. In electrons GPS, Molniya and GEO receive the most and LEO about a hundred times less; in protons above about 20 MeV LEO receives the most and GPS and GEO the least.
  • MEO and HEO have opposite worst cases because MEO is trapped-proton-dominated (peaking at solar minimum) while HEO spends most of its time high, where solar particles dominate (peaking at solar maximum).
  • An elliptical orbit crosses the belts at up to 10.0 km/s near perigee and dwells at 1.5 km/s near apogee outside them, so its daily exposure is below that of a circular orbit at the same mean radius. The 63.4° critical inclination keeps apogee over the intended hemisphere.
  • GPS accepts the outer belt's electron dose because navigation needs four satellites simultaneously visible, altitude buys that visibility with a small constellation, and there is no drag at 20,200 km to shorten a fifteen-year life.
  • Inclination is part of the environment: polar and sun-synchronous orbits receive solar energetic particles through the open polar field lines that low-inclination orbits at the same altitude never see.
  • Mission duration is part of the environment too — the Shuttle row is low-risk for the same orbit as the low-Earth row, because fluence is a time integral.
  • The analysis output is a mission-integrated fluence spectrum, converted behind a stated shield into a total ionising dose in krad(Si) and a linear energy transfer spectrum for single-event rate prediction.
