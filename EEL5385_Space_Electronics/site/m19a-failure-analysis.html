Module 19a

Failure Analysis

How a failed part is made to show where and why it failed: the loop from electrical signature to physical mechanism, the non-destructive techniques that locate a defect without disturbing it (X-ray computed tomography and thermal imaging), a NASA screening study that uses the same thermal physics to find resistor defects before they fail, and the focused ion beam that cuts a cross-section through the exact site.

Prerequisites M10 §3, §7, M12 §3, M18 §6–§8, M19 §1, §5 Read time ~55 min Sources Deck 19 pp. 22–30, 34, 36, 38–39; Deck 20; Lectures 20 and 21; NEPP resistor-screening report (2019)

Qualification (M19) produces failures on purpose: a vibration test cracks a joint, a thermal-cycling chamber opens a bump, a heavy-ion beam burns out a transistor. A failure is useful only once its cause is known, because only then can the design, the material or the process be changed and the part tested again. Failure analysis (FA) is the discipline that finds the cause. Deck 20 states its attitude in one line: to a failure analyst, a part is a collection of failure mechanisms waiting to happen. Lecture 20 gave the reason it matters more each year: integration in two and three dimensions (M18 §7–§8) multiplies the interconnects, the interconnects are the weak points, and so integrated parts need more failure analysis, starting with the simple methods, X-ray imaging and thermal imaging. Section 1 sets out the loop every analysis follows. Sections 2 and 3 treat the two non-destructive methods and the physics that gives each its contrast. Section 4 follows one NASA study from a resistor's construction to a fractured defect. Sections 5 to 7 treat the focused ion beam: the instrument, what it can and cannot do to a sample, and the cross-sections it produced of radiation damage in silicon carbide power transistors.

1 · The failure-analysis loop

Every analysis moves from what the part does wrong electrically to what is physically wrong inside it, and it moves from techniques that leave the part unchanged to techniques that destroy it. Deck 19 lays out its first case under six headings that name the stages (background, failure mode, interim findings, isolation, deprocessing, mechanism), and the same stages organise every case on this page.

  • Failure background: the circumstances of the failure, what was being done to the part and what was observed.
  • Failure mode: the electrical signature of the failure, measured at the terminals: an open, a short, a leakage, a drift. A failure mode says what is wrong.
  • Isolation: locating the site of the fault inside the part, first without opening or altering it (X-ray imaging, thermal imaging), then by removing material.
  • Failure mechanism: the physical process that produced the fault at that site, such as electrical overstress, fatigue cracking or a void in a joint. A failure mechanism says why.

The order matters because each destructive step can create the evidence it is looking for. Lecture 20 gave the argument for 3D-stacked parts: removing the dies of a stack one layer at a time may itself crack or short something, and then the analyst cannot tell whether the part failed from an internal defect or from the delayering. A technique that sees inside the intact part removes that doubt, so it comes first. Destructive physical analysis (DPA, the term NASA's parts programme uses) follows only once the site is known, and then removes as little as possible around it. Lecture 21 closed the loop at the other end: once the mechanism is known, the part or the process is improved and tested again, and the test is repeated until it passes (Figure 1).

The failure mode of Deck 19's first case. During power-up of a board assembly, an event drew a high current through one output of a demultiplexer, a circuit that routes one input to one of several outputs. At the terminals, the failure mode was a short of 3.8 Ω between one of the eight outputs and ground. The measurement that shows a short is the curve tracer, an instrument that sweeps the voltage between two pins and plots the current against it: a resistance draws a straight line through the origin whose slope is its conductance, so a short draws a line that is almost vertical. The deck's screen is scaled at 200 µA per vertical division and 500 mV per horizontal division, and its trace between output Y7 and VSS is vertical. The lid of the package was then removed, and the die showed no visible damage, so the site had to be found by other means (§3).

E1 · What the curve-tracer screen can and cannot measure

Given the screen of Deck 19 p. 34: 200 µA per division vertically (current), 500 mV per division horizontally (voltage); the reported short, 3.8 Ω. Find the resistance that draws a 45° line, the horizontal movement of the 3.8 Ω trace, and the smallest resistance whose trace a reader could tell from vertical.

Plan. On a curve tracer the trace of a resistance R satisfies V = IR, so its horizontal movement in divisions per vertical division is R times the current per division divided by the voltage per division. The hinge is that the screen's scales, not the part, decide what is resolvable.

The 45° resistance. A 45° line moves one horizontal division for each vertical division: R = (0.5 V)/(200×10⁻⁶ A) = 2,500 Ω. Any resistance far below 2.5 kΩ is drawn steeper than 45°.

The 3.8 Ω trace. Over one vertical division the current changes by 200 µA, so the voltage changes by (200×10⁻⁶ A)(3.8 Ω) = 0.76 mV, which is 0.76/500 = 0.00152 of a horizontal division, or 1/658. Over the screen's ten vertical divisions (2.0 mA) the trace moves 7.6 mV, 0.0152 of a division.

The smallest resistance visible as a tilt. Suppose a tilt of 0.1 division across the ten divisions of height is just visible. Then R = (0.1 × 0.5 V)/(10 × 200×10⁻⁶ A) = 25 Ω. A short of 3.8 Ω tilts the trace about 6.6 times less than that.

The 45° resistance is 2.5 kΩ; the 3.8 Ω trace moves 0.0152 division over the ten divisions, indistinguishable from vertical; anything below about 25 Ω looks the same. Check: the slide's trace is vertical, consistent with a short but not a measurement of it. The 3.8 Ω value must come from another measurement, a curve-tracer setting at a few millivolts per division or a resistance meter; the slide does not say which.

The same short is re-measured with the tracer set to 1 mA per division vertically and 50 mV per division horizontally. What resistance now draws a 45° line, and how far does the 3.8 Ω trace move horizontally per vertical division? Has the change made the short measurable?

The 45° resistance is the voltage per division over the current per division, (0.05 V)/(1×10⁻³ A) = 50 Ω. The 3.8 Ω trace moves (1×10⁻³ A)(3.8 Ω)/(0.05 V) = 0.076 division per vertical division, so over the ten divisions of the screen it moves 0.76 division: now a visible tilt, from which the resistance can be read to roughly one significant figure. Both scale changes help: a larger current per division raises the voltage across the short, and a smaller voltage per division magnifies it. A four-terminal resistance measurement would still be needed for a value as precise as 3.8 Ω.

2 · X-ray imaging and computed tomography

An X-ray image of a part records how much of a beam each path through it absorbs. Because absorption depends steeply on atomic number, the metal of a package (copper traces, solder, bond wires, bumps) stands out against the silicon, the glass fibre and the polymer around it, and a void, which absorbs nothing, shows as a gap in the metal. The part is not opened, so nothing is disturbed. This section explains where the contrast comes from, how computed tomography turns many projections into a three-dimensional map, what limits the smallest visible defect, and the two cases of Deck 19: a flight telecom board and the micro-bumps of a high-bandwidth memory stack.

Where the contrast comes from. M10 §7 derived the attenuation law: a beam of photons of one energy crossing a thickness x of a material keeps the fraction exp(−(µ/ρ)ρx), where µ/ρ is the mass attenuation coefficient and ρ the density. At the energies of a laboratory X-ray tube (M09 §5), photoelectric absorption dominates µ/ρ in heavy elements, and its cross-section grows as Z⁴ to Z⁵ and falls as E−3 (M10 §3). Copper (Z = 29) and tin (Z = 50) therefore absorb far more than silicon (Z = 14) per gram, and more again per centimetre because they are denser. Lecture 20 made the same point by recalling the course's homework on silicon against lead: heavy elements block X-rays most.

Equation (19a.1) shows the trade-off that sets the X-ray energy. A void shows as a brightening of the transmitted beam by the factor exp[(µ/ρ)ρd], which is large only if µ/ρ is large, that is at low energy. E2 · How visible is a void in a copper bump?

Given a copper path 20 µm long through a bump, and a parallel path through the same bump that crosses a 5 µm void; copper ρ = 8.960 g/cm³, µ/ρ = 10.92 cm²/g at 30 keV and 1.593 cm²/g at 60 keV; silicon ρ = 2.330 g/cm³, µ/ρ = 1.436 cm²/g at 30 keV and 0.3207 cm²/g at 60 keV (NIST). Find the two transmitted fractions and the brightening due to the void at each energy, and the transmission of 1 mm of silicon, the order of a die or interposer stack, at each.

Plan. Apply (10.8) to each path and form the ratio (19a.1). The hinge is that the contrast of the void depends on µ for copper, while the dose of photons that reaches the detector depends on everything else in the path.

Linear attenuation coefficients. µ = (µ/ρ)ρ. Copper at 30 keV: (10.92)(8.960) = 97.8 cm⁻¹; at 60 keV: (1.593)(8.960) = 14.3 cm⁻¹. Silicon at 30 keV: 3.35 cm⁻¹; at 60 keV: 0.747 cm⁻¹. Copper attenuates 29 times more strongly than silicon per centimetre at 30 keV and 19 times at 60 keV.

The solid path. 20 µm = 2.0×10⁻³ cm. At 30 keV, Tfull = exp(−97.8 × 0.0020) = 0.822. At 60 keV, exp(−14.3 × 0.0020) = 0.972.

The path through the void. 15 µm of copper remains. At 30 keV, Tvoid = exp(−97.8 × 0.0015) = 0.863; at 60 keV, 0.979.

The contrast. At 30 keV the void brightens the ray by 0.863/0.822 = 1.050, a 5.0 % change; (19a.1) gives the same, exp(97.8 × 5×10⁻⁴) = 1.050. At 60 keV the change is 0.72 %, seven times smaller.

The rest of the path. 1 mm of silicon transmits exp(−3.35 × 0.1) = 0.716 at 30 keV and 0.928 at 60 keV. Why this matters: the void's contrast is a fraction of the signal that arrives, and the noise of a photon-counting detector grows as the square root of the count, so absorption elsewhere in the path lengthens the scan needed to see the same contrast.

A 5 µm void in a 20 µm copper bump changes the transmitted beam by 5.0 % at 30 keV and by 0.72 % at 60 keV. Check: the logarithm of the contrast is µd, linear in both, so a 1 µm void at 30 keV gives 0.98 %, a fifth of the 5 µm value.

Tin brings one complication that Figure 3 shows: its K absorption edge at 29.2 keV. Just above the edge a photon can eject a K-shell electron, and µ/ρ jumps by a factor of 5.62, from 7.760 to 43.60 cm²/g. Through 20 µm of tin the transmission falls from 0.893 just below the edge to 0.529 just above it, so near 30 keV a tin-rich solder is far more opaque than copper. Absorption edges were introduced in M10 §3 with silicon's, at 1.839 keV.

From projections to tomography. A single radiograph superimposes everything along each ray, so a void above a trace and a void below it look the same, and dense parts hide what lies behind them. Computed tomography (CT) records many radiographs while the sample rotates through a full turn, with source and detector fixed. Each radiograph gives the line integral of µ along every ray at that angle; a reconstruction algorithm solves for the value of µ in each small cube of the volume, the voxel, that is consistent with all the line integrals at once. The result is a three-dimensional map of attenuation. Slices through it in any plane show the interior of an intact part, and a threshold on µ that removes the low-density materials leaves the metal alone, which is how Deck 19 p. 23 shows the wires and solder pads of a board with the lighter densities removed.

Magnification, voxel size and resolution. A laboratory CT uses a small source whose rays diverge in a cone (Figure 4). A sample at a source-to-object distance SOD projects onto a detector at a source-to-detector distance SDD with a geometric magnification M = SDD/SOD, by similar triangles. The voxel size is the detector pixel pitch divided by that magnification. A voxel size is a sampling interval, not a resolution: two features are told apart only if a lower value of µ lies between them, which needs at least one voxel between two others, so the smallest resolvable spacing is at least two voxels (the sampling theorem's limit). The focal spot of the source, the blur of the detector and the stability of the rotation make the real resolution coarser still.

Deck 19's first X-ray case applied CT to a whole flight-model telecom board (pp. 23–25). Rendering the volume with the light materials removed showed the wires and pads; a clipping plane through the volume exposed rows of pads inside a hybrid communications device mounted on the board; and slices showed the multilayer board, its subcomponents and the attachment of the hybrid. A slice through the upper contacts of the hybrid showed what the slide calls apparent solder bridging between pads, at several places: solder joining two pads that should be separate, which shorts them. The slide says apparent; Lecture 20 presented the bridging as the cause of the failure. A CT slice shows a continuous path of dense material, and an electrical check of the two pads, or a cross-section, confirms that it conducts.

Nano-CT of a memory stack. A high-bandwidth memory (HBM) stack is a set of DRAM dies joined by dense arrays of micro-bumps and through-silicon vias (M18 §7). Its bumps are too small and too deeply buried for a board-level CT. Deck 19 pp. 26–30 follow a 2025 study in Microelectronics Reliability that imaged one with an X-ray nano-CT, a CT whose source spot and magnification bring the voxel below a micrometre. The instrument on p. 26 has the source, the sample on a rotation stage, and a flat-panel detector drawn 48° off the axis of the source, receiving the rays that leave the source at that angle, in straight lines. The study also imaged by laminography, a tilted-geometry relative of CT, and the drawing may show that set-up rather than the cone-beam CT of Figure 4 (the paper was not available to settle it). The reconstructed slice on p. 27 shows three kinds of joint in one image, labelled 20 µm micro-bumps between the DRAM dies, 25 µm interposer bumps and 100 µm C4 copper bumps below, reconstructed with a voxel size of 600 nm.

Three statements to read with care

The detector geometry. Lecture 20 described the nano-CT detector as sitting deeper than the beam and the X-rays as deflected onto it. The figure on Deck 19 p. 26 shows neither: the beam travels in a straight line from source to sample, and the flat panel is mounted 48° off the source axis and receives the rays emitted in that direction. X-rays are not bent by any element of a CT; the image is a straight-line projection. Voxel and resolution. Deck 19 p. 27 gives a voxel size of 600 nm and p. 28 a resolution of 600 nm for the same scan. By (19a.2) the resolvable spacing is at least twice the voxel, 1.2 µm; read the 600 nm as the voxel. How many bumps have voids. Lecture 20 said that almost all the bumps had defects. The counts on p. 30 (below) do not show that by themselves: 1,680 voids in 1,275 bumps is an average of 1.32 per bump, consistent with voids in most bumps or with several voids in fewer bumps. The slide's horizontal slice beside the counts does show a dark interior spot in most of the bumps in that plane, which supports the statement qualitatively, for one plane.

Fast overview, then long scan. Deck 19 p. 28 compares two scans of the same stack: a 30 s scan that resolves the three bump levels and the stack's layout at coarse resolution, and a 75 min scan, 150 times longer, that resolves the bumps individually. Lecture 20 stressed the workflow this implies: a fast overview first, to find the region that matters, and a long high-resolution scan only of that region. The trade-off is set by the detector. For a detector of a fixed number of pixels, a smaller voxel means a smaller field of view (1,000 voxels of 600 nm cover 0.6 mm), and each smaller voxel receives fewer photons, so the count statistics that decide whether a 5 % contrast (E2) is visible require a longer exposure.

Defects, and their populations. In the long scan the slices show horizontal and vertical cracks, voids below a micrometre and larger voids, and the geometry of each bump and its alignment with its pad (p. 29). A crack that runs across a bump separates its upper and lower halves, and Lecture 20 called it a complete failure of the joint; a void reduces the conducting cross-section and concentrates current and strain around it. The study did not stop at example images. Segmenting the volume (p. 30), it identified 1,275 micro-bumps and 1,680 voids in the selected volume, found that 92.5 % of the voids were smaller than 50 µm³, and that 65 % had a sphericity above 0.8 (the slide states both as fractions of the voids, but its histograms are plotted as volume fraction, so they may be weighted by void volume; the paper was not available to settle it). Sphericity compares a shape's surface area with that of a sphere of the same volume and equals 1 for a sphere, so most of the voids are nearly round, while a crack, flat and wide, has a low sphericity. Counting populations is what turns imaging into a reliability measurement: a void fraction or a crack count can be compared before and after thermal cycling (M19 §5).

E3 · Which voids can a 600 nm voxel resolve?

Given the voxel size of Deck 19 p. 27, 600 nm, and the void volumes of p. 30: the 50 µm³ threshold below which 92.5 % of the voids lie, and two smaller volumes, 1 µm³ and 0.1 µm³. Find the diameter of a sphere of each volume, its size in voxels, and whether it is resolved.

Plan. Convert each volume to the diameter of a sphere of equal volume, d = (6V/π)1/3, divide by the voxel size, and compare with the two-voxel limit of (19a.2). The hinge is that detection and resolution differ: a void smaller than two voxels can still lower the mean µ of the voxel it occupies, so it may be detected without its shape being measured.

The 50 µm³ void. d = (6 × 50/π)1/3 = 4.57 µm = 7.62 voxels. It is resolved, and its shape can be measured: it occupies about 231 voxels, since one voxel is (0.600 µm)³ = 0.216 µm³.

The 1 µm³ void. d = 1.24 µm = 2.07 voxels, at the two-voxel limit: present, but its sphericity cannot be measured reliably.

The 0.1 µm³ void. d = 0.576 µm = 0.96 voxel: smaller than one voxel. Why it may still show: the reconstruction assigns the voxel the average µ of copper and void inside it, so it reads darker than its neighbours (the partial-volume effect), but the void's size and shape are lost.

Voids of 50 µm³ span about 7.6 voxels and are fully resolved; 1 µm³ voids sit at the two-voxel limit; 0.1 µm³ voids are below one voxel and can at most be detected. Check: halving the voxel to 300 nm doubles each count in voxels, so the 1 µm³ void would span 4.1.

A solder bump of tin, 25 µm along the ray, contains a 5 µm void, and the scan is run at 40 keV. Using µ/ρ(Sn) = 19.42 cm²/g, ρ = 7.310 g/cm³, and for copper µ/ρ = 4.862 cm²/g, ρ = 8.960 g/cm³, how much does the void brighten the ray in a tin bump and in a copper bump of the same size? Which bump shows the void more clearly, and why?

By (19a.1) the brightening is exp(µd) with d = 5×10⁻⁴ cm. Tin: µ = (19.42)(7.310) = 142 cm⁻¹, so exp(142 × 5×10⁻⁴) = 1.07, a 7 % brightening; the transmissions are 0.701 through the full 25 µm and 0.753 through the path with the void. Copper: µ = (4.862)(8.960) = 43.6 cm⁻¹, giving 1.02, a 2 % brightening (0.897 and 0.917). The tin bump shows the void 3.3 times more strongly in logarithmic contrast (142/43.6), because at 40 keV tin lies above its K edge (29.2 keV) and its µ is more than three times copper's; the void's contrast is proportional to the µ of the material it displaces.

An analyst reports that "every void in the stack is under 50 µm³ except about a hundred". From the counts on Deck 19 p. 30, how many voids are 50 µm³ or larger, and do the counts show what fraction of the bumps contain a void?

If the 7.5 % is a fraction by number, 7.5 % of 1,680 is 126 voids at or above 50 µm³, so "about a hundred" is close; if it is a fraction of void volume, as the slide's histogram axes suggest, the count cannot be found from the slide. Either way the counts do not fix how many bumps hold a void: 1.32 voids per bump on average is consistent with a void in nearly every bump and with several voids in each of fewer bumps. To state the fraction of bumps affected, the study would have to report voids per bump, which the slide does not. What supports "almost all the bumps" is the image, not the numbers: the slide's horizontal slice shows a dark interior spot in most of the bumps visible in that one plane.

3 · Thermal imaging and an electrical-overstress case

X-ray imaging finds a defect in the structure. Thermal imaging finds where the part dissipates power, which is where current flows that should not. A short between an output and ground, for example, carries its current through one small region, and the Joule heat released there raises the local temperature above its surroundings. A camera that images the infrared emission of the die therefore shows the site of the short as a hot spot, even when nothing is visible under an optical microscope. This section explains what the camera detects and why the image is taken as a difference of two frames, then completes Deck 19's demultiplexer case.

What the camera detects. Every surface emits thermal radiation with a spectrum close to that of a black body at its temperature, scaled by its emissivity, the fraction of black-body emission a surface achieves at each wavelength (between 0 and 1). Wien's displacement law places the peak of the black-body spectrum at λmax = b/T, with b = 2.898×10⁻³ m·K: 9.72 µm at 25 °C and 7.77 µm at 100 °C. The camera of the NASA study in §4, an indium antimonide (InSb) detector, works in the band from 3 to 5 µm, on the short-wavelength side of that peak. On that side the emission rises much faster with temperature than the total does, which makes the band sensitive to small temperature rises (E4). Thermal imaging of a die is complicated by emissivity: polished metal has a low emissivity and an oxide a high one, so an image of a die at uniform temperature still shows its layout as a pattern of bright and dark.

Why the image is a difference. Deck 19 p. 39 gives the procedure: the part is biased with an external supply, the thermal camera records the emission, and an unpowered frame is subtracted from the result. Both frames carry the same pattern of emissivity and the same background, so the difference removes them and leaves only the change in emission caused by the power the bias dissipates. A hot spot too faint to see against the emissivity pattern in a single frame stands out in the difference (Figure 6).

E4 · Why the 3–5 µm band suits a die near room temperature

Given Wien's constant b = 2.898×10⁻³ m·K; Planck's law for the spectral exitance of a black body; a die at 25 °C, and a hot spot at 35 °C. Find the peak wavelengths, the fraction of the total emission that falls in the 3–5 µm band, and how much the emission in that band rises between 25 and 35 °C compared with the total.

Plan. Wien's law gives the peaks; the in-band fraction is the integral of Planck's law from 3 to 5 µm divided by the Stefan–Boltzmann total σT⁴, computed numerically. The hinge is that the band lies on the steep short-wavelength flank of the spectrum.

Peaks. 25 °C = 298.15 K: λmax = 2.898×10⁻³/298.15 = 9.72 µm. At 50 °C, 8.97 µm; at 100 °C, 7.77 µm. Only at about 350 °C does the peak reach 4.65 µm, inside the band.

Fraction in band. The integral gives 1.22 % of the total emission between 3 and 5 µm at 25 °C, and 1.53 % at 35 °C.

Sensitivity. The total emission rises as T⁴: (308.15/298.15)⁴ = 1.141, a 14.1 % rise for 10 K. The in-band emission rises by 42.7 %, three times as much, because the 3–5 µm band lies where the exponential factor of Planck's law dominates.

The peak lies near 10 µm, so the band holds only about 1.2 % of the emission, but that emission changes three times faster with temperature than the total. Check: the band's fraction increases with temperature (1.22 % to 1.53 %), as it must when the peak moves toward shorter wavelengths.

The demultiplexer, completed. In Deck 19's first case (§1), delidding showed nothing. Thermal imaging, with the part biased by an external supply, showed a hot spot adjacent to the failing output's bond pad, and overlaying the optical image of the die placed it precisely (p. 39). The die was then deprocessed, its upper layers removed by the methods the slide lists, plasma etching, planar polishing and chemical etching, until the circuitry at the site was exposed (p. 38). Optical micrographs at increasing magnification followed the output's metal from the pad to its output driver, the transistors that drive the pin, and a scanning electron microscope image showed the damage at one point of the driver. The mechanism identified was electrical overstress (EOS) damage to the output driver circuitry.

Electrical overstress is an overstress mechanism in the sense of M19 §1: a voltage or current beyond the part's rating, applied once, here plausibly during the power-up event of the failure background. The excess current heats a conductor or junction locally, and the damage it leaves can join nodes that should be separate, which is a short. Lecture 20 described it as a sudden current that damaged the dielectric and left the chip dead. The sequence of this case is the loop of Figure 1 in full: background (a power-up event), mode (a 3.8 Ω short), isolation (thermal imaging, after optical inspection found nothing), deprocessing (plasma, polishing, chemical etching), mechanism (EOS at the driver).

Another way to find a current path

Thermal emission is one of several signals that mark where current flows. M17b §3 met a second in Lecture 15's case of a cracked dielectric between gate and source metal: optical beam-induced resistance change (OBIRCH), in which a scanned laser heats each point of the die in turn and the change of current through the part marks the points on the current path. The two are complementary: thermal imaging reads the heat the fault produces, OBIRCH perturbs the circuit with heat and reads the electrical response.

A part shows a bright region in a single powered infrared frame, at the position of a wide aluminium bus line. The difference with an unpowered frame shows nothing there. What explains the first image, and what does the second say about the bus?

The bright region in the single frame need not be hot. A powered frame contains the emissivity pattern of the die as well as any temperature change, and a region whose emissivity differs from its surroundings, here the bus against the dielectric around it, appears brighter or darker at uniform temperature (a polished metal can also reflect the emission of warmer surroundings into the camera). Subtracting the unpowered frame removes that pattern, because it is the same with and without bias. A null difference means the bias adds no detectable emission at the bus: the fault lies elsewhere, or dissipates too little at that bias to be seen.

4 · The resistor case: constriction defects, Joule hot spots and fatigue

Deck 19 ended by announcing that the next failure analysis would use a NASA report, and Lecture 20 added that it would begin with a resistor, the simplest of components, and its electrical stress. The report posted with the course as an optional reading on reliability and parts screening is a study of exactly that kind: J. Brusse (SSAI at NASA Goddard Space Flight Center) and L. Panashchenko (NASA Goddard), presented at the 2019 NASA Electronic Parts and Packaging (NEPP) Electronics Technology Workshop. It shows how a defect too small for the standard screens heats under current, how that heat fatigues and fractures it during operation, and how the same heating, imaged in the infrared, finds the defect before the part is used. Deck 20 p. 23 frames the question with the same component: a designer sees a resistor, a parts engineer sees a line in a bill of materials ("Resistor Film 1206 100 Ohm 1% 0.25W"), and a failure analyst sees its failure mechanisms.

What a foil resistor is. The parts in the study are surface-mount (SMT) foil resistors, chosen where a resistance must be precise and stable: the report lists a tolerance of ±0.01 % (±100 ppm), a temperature coefficient of resistance below ±1 ppm/°C from −55 to +125 °C, and a drift of at most ±0.03 % (±300 ppm) after a 2,000-hour life test at rated power and 70 °C. The resistive element is a foil of a nickel–chromium (NiCr) alloy, about 2–5 µm thick, bonded with an adhesive (about 5 µm) to an alumina substrate (the ceramic of M18 §2) and protected by a polymer coating (about 10 µm) and an epoxy coating (about 10–20 µm) (Figure 7). Photolithography and electrochemical etching cut the foil into a meander of gridlines, and the value is trimmed by laser or mechanical cutting of trim tabs, combinations of pattern segments connected in parallel.

(19a.3)
R resistance (Ω) · ρ resistivity of the foil (Ω·cm) · L length of the conducting path (cm) · w gridline width and t foil thickness (cm), so wt is the cross-sectional area that the report writes as A · j current density (A/cm²) · I current (A) · pv Joule power dissipated per unit volume (W/cm³). Uniform current in a straight gridline; at a constriction w is the local width, and the current spreads back to the full width within a few widths of it.

Equation (19a.3) is the report's own starting point: low values use wide, thick, short gridlines (about 10 µm or more wide, about 5 µm thick), high values narrow, thin, long ones (under about 10 µm wide, about 2 µm thick). Its second and third forms carry the failure mechanism. A constriction defect is a local narrowing of the current path, of three kinds the report shows in electron micrographs: a notch, a bite out of the edge of a gridline; a bridge, a thin strip of foil left by the etch between two gridlines that should be separate; and an embedded particle, such as an aluminium nitride grain in the foil, at which the gridline narrows. At a constriction the same current crosses a smaller area, so j rises as 1/w and the power dissipated per unit volume as 1/w².

The mechanism. The report states the chain in four links. Constrictions carry a higher current density and develop localized hot spots by Joule heating. The hot spots expand the NiCr foil locally more than the rest of the pattern. Constrictions are therefore at risk of thermomechanical fatigue fracture, especially during power cycling, because each on–off cycle strains and relaxes them: the mechanism of M19 §5, driven here by the part's own dissipation instead of the environment. When a constriction fractures, the resistance shifts upward, as far as an open circuit. The direction of the shift follows from the geometry. A fractured notch cuts a gridline that is in series, which opens the path. A bridge is a parallel path that bypassed part of the meander, and the report notes that it contributes directly to the final resistance value: it was present when the resistor was trimmed, so when it breaks, the bypassed section returns in series and the resistance rises (Figure 8).

E5 · Current density at a notch

Given one lot of the study: 0805 size, 5.1 kΩ, rated 100 mW, foil 2.3 µm thick, gridline 6.6 µm wide. The screening pulse is 6.25 times rated power. The visual screen rejects notches that remove more than 75 % of the line width. Find the current and current density at rated power, the same at 6.25× rated power, and the factor by which a notch at the visual reject limit raises the local current density and power density.

Plan. Rated power and resistance fix the current through P = I²R; the cross-section fixes j by (19a.3). The hinge is that power scales as current squared, so a 6.25-fold power is a 2.5-fold current, and a constriction multiplies power density by the square of its narrowing.

Cross-section. A = wt = (6.6 µm)(2.3 µm) = 15.2 µm² = 1.52×10⁻⁷ cm².

Current at rated power. I = √(P/R) = √(0.100 W / 5,100 Ω) = 4.43 mA, at V = IR = 22.6 V.

Current density. j = 4.43×10⁻³ A / 1.52×10⁻⁷ cm² = 2.92×10⁴ A/cm².

At 6.25× rated power. I scales as √P, so it rises by √6.25 = 2.5, to 11.1 mA, and j to 7.29×10⁴ A/cm².

At a notch leaving 25 % of the width. The same current crosses a quarter of the area: j rises 4-fold, to 2.92×10⁵ A/cm² under the screening pulse, and pv = ρj² 16-fold, since ρ is that of the same foil. A notch leaving half the width raises pv 4-fold.

At rated power 4.43 mA and 2.92×10⁴ A/cm²; under the screening pulse 11.1 mA and 7.29×10⁴ A/cm²; a notch at the 75 % reject limit multiplies the local power density by 16. Check: 6.25 = 2.5² matches the factor on the current, and the units A/cm² follow from amperes over square centimetres.

Why the standard screens miss them. The report lists the screens such a resistor normally passes (its p. 7): optical inspection at 30× to 60× before the coating is applied, rejecting notches that remove more than 75 % of the line width and bridges narrower than 10 % of the smallest line width; a short-time overload (STOL) of 6.25× rated power for 5 seconds, rejecting a shift above 0.02 %; and power conditioning at 1 to 1.5× rated power at maximum operating temperature for 100 hours, rejecting a shift above 0.03 % (the report's own p. 27 gives this conditioning instead as 1.5× rated power for 100 hours at 70 °C). Each screen leaves a gap. A notch that removes 70 % passes the visual limit and still raises the local power density 11-fold by (19a.3). A bridge wider than 10 % of the line passes by the criterion. And a constriction that survives 5 seconds of overload, or 100 hours of conditioning, has not yet accumulated the cycles that fracture it. The report adds that for foil resistors the overload is applied to a sample of 10 pieces of a high-reliability lot. Its conclusion: resistors with significant constriction defects are still occasionally received despite the screens.

The pulsed-power infrared screen. The new method applies the same 6.25× rated power as the overload, but in pulses of 50 ms at a 10 % duty cycle (p. 8; the demonstration on p. 9 ran 50 ms on and 150 ms off, a 25 % duty cycle), one or more of them, while a high-resolution infrared camera images the resistor. The report states that these conditions confine the heating to the localized constrictions, which then appear as hot spots; a resistor with a hot spot is rejected. The protective coatings are transparent from 3 to 5 µm, so the foil is imaged through them, and the screen is non-destructive: it can be applied by the manufacturer before coating or by the user after purchase. Resolution decides what it finds. The camera used (an InSb detector, 3–5 µm) images about 5 µm per pixel, close to the width of a gridline (4.1 to 6.6 µm in the study's lots); on a resistor with two constriction defects, a camera at about 25 µm per pixel left it in doubt whether even one hot spot was detected.

The test of the screen. The study screened 280 foil resistors, 40 from each of seven lots of three manufacturing types, and reported constriction defects in about 10 to 25 % of the resistors in each lot (its headline; the lot counts reach 11 of 40, 27.5 %, in one lot unless some resistors carry both defects): 30 with notch defects and 28 with bridge defects in all. Every resistor then ran a 10,000-hour life test at rated power and 70 °C, switched 1.5 hours on and 0.5 hours off, so each saw 5,000 power cycles. Three failed, each by an abrupt positive shift of resistance (Figure 9), and the failure analysis of each closed the loop of §1:

E6 · How large a section of meander did a bridge bypass?

Given the 2010-size, 50 kΩ resistor whose single bridge fractured with a shift of ≈ 4,200 ppm, and the 1206-size, 20 kΩ resistor whose shift was ≈ 19,400 ppm; the 300 ppm life-test limit. Find each shift as a percentage and as a multiple of the limit, the resistance added, and the share of the meander the bridge had bypassed, in a model in which the bridge's own resistance is negligible.

Plan. Let the full meander be R0 and the bypassed section Rs. With the bridge, R = R0 − Rs, the trimmed value; after fracture, R0. Then ΔR/R = Rs/(R0 − Rs), which rearranges to Rs/R0 = x/(1 + x) with x = ΔR/R.

Convert the shifts. 1 ppm = 10⁻⁶ = 10⁻⁴ %. So 4,200 ppm = 0.42 %, 14 times the 300 ppm limit; 19,400 ppm = 1.94 %, 65 times.

Resistance added. ΔR = xR: (4.2×10⁻³)(50 kΩ) = 210 Ω, and (1.94×10⁻²)(20 kΩ) = 388 Ω.

Share of the meander. Rs/R0 = 0.0042/1.0042 = 0.418 %, and 0.0194/1.0194 = 1.90 %. Why the model gives a lower bound: a real bridge has resistance of its own and carries only part of the current around the section, so the section it bypassed must be larger than this to produce the same shift.

0.42 % (14× the limit; 210 Ω; at least 0.42 % of the meander) and 1.94 % (65× the limit; 388 Ω; at least 1.9 %). Check: both shifts are positive, as the fracture of a parallel path requires, and for small shifts x ≈ Rs/R0.

A 50 kΩ foil resistor rated 300 mW has gridlines 4.8 µm wide in a 2.5 µm foil. What current density does a 6.25× rated-power pulse drive through a gridline, and by what factor does a notch that leaves 40 % of the width raise the local power density? Would the visual screen reject that notch?

Rated current √(0.300/50,000) = 2.45 mA; through (4.8 µm)(2.5 µm) = 12.0 µm² = 1.20×10⁻⁷ cm², that is 2.04×10⁴ A/cm². The pulse multiplies the current by √6.25 = 2.5, giving 5.10×10⁴ A/cm². A notch leaving 40 % of the width raises j by 1/0.4 = 2.5 and the power density by 2.5² = 6.25. Under the pulse the notch therefore dissipates per unit volume 6.25 × 6.25 = 39 times what the line does at rated power. It removes 60 % of the width, short of the 75 % visual reject limit, so the visual screen passes it; the infrared screen would show it as a hot spot.

5 · The focused ion beam

Once a site is located, it must be seen in section: a crack under a bump, a burned region under a gate, a void at a depth below the surface. The focused ion beam (FIB) cuts that section at a chosen point with sub-micrometre precision. It is a column, much like that of a scanning electron microscope (SEM), that focuses a beam of gallium ions instead of electrons; the ions are heavy enough to knock atoms out of the surface, so the beam mills where it is scanned. Deck 20 calls it a maskless etching process: the pattern is written by the beam itself, with no photomask. This section explains the ion source, why a FIB focuses with electric rather than magnetic fields, and how a FIB and a SEM are combined in one instrument.

The liquid-metal ion source. The source (Deck 20 p. 4) is a tungsten needle with a tip radius of 1 to 3 µm, mounted below a small reservoir of gallium and heated by a coil until the gallium melts and wets the needle. An extractor electrode facing the tip is held at 6 to 10 kV relative to it. The electric field at the tip pulls on the liquid film, and the surface tension of the liquid opposes it; where the electrostatic force, which grows as the square of the field, balances the surface tension, the liquid is drawn into a sharp cone, the Taylor cone, drawn with an angle of about 50° on the deck's figure. The field at the apex of the cone, a few nanometres across, exceeds 10⁸ V/cm, high enough to ionise gallium atoms at the surface and pull them off as Ga⁺ ions (field evaporation), at an emission current of 1 to 10 µA. The ions are then accelerated, typically to an energy between 1 and 50 keV, and focused onto the sample. The deck's slide calls the extracting field "electromagnetic"; it is an electrostatic field, set by the extractor voltage.

Why gallium. Deck 20 p. 6 lists the reasons. Gallium is a metal with a low melting point, 302.92 K (29.77 °C), so the source runs near room temperature and in high vacuum, and a very small volume in the reservoir gives the gun a long life; it gives a bright, stable emission; and it sits near the middle of the periodic table, so its momentum transfer to the atoms of most target metals is near optimum. Lecture 21 put the last point as a balance of mass: an ion that is too heavy loses resolution and control, one that is too light carries too little momentum to remove material. The physics of that transfer is the elastic-collision factor 4Mm/(M + m)² of M11 §3, the one the nuclear stopping of M12 §3 uses: the largest fraction of an ion's energy that can be handed to a target atom, it reaches 1 when the two masses are equal. For gallium (69.7 u) it is 1.00 on copper, 0.82 on silicon and 0.77 on gold, so the deck's "optimum for most metals" is a kinematic argument, and a fair one.

Why electrostatic lenses. One row of Deck 20 p. 8 compares the lenses of the two instruments: a FIB column is equipped with electrostatic lenses, a SEM column with electromagnetic ones. The reason is the mass of the ion. A magnetic field bends a charged particle on a radius r = p/(qB), so the field needed to focus it scales with its momentum per charge, the magnetic rigidity Br = p/q. At the same accelerating voltage the momentum grows as the square root of the mass, so a gallium ion needs about 350 times the field an electron does (E7), beyond what a compact lens can produce. An electrostatic lens acts through the potential alone: a particle of charge q accelerated through a voltage V follows a path that depends on the ratio of the lens potentials to V, not on its mass, so the same lens focuses any singly charged ion.

E7 · A gallium ion and an electron at 30 kV

Given an accelerating voltage of 30 kV; Ga atomic mass 69.723 u (1 u = 1.6605×10⁻²⁷ kg); electron mass 9.109×10⁻³¹ kg; e = 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ C; h = 6.626×10⁻³⁴ J·s. Find the momentum, magnetic rigidity and de Broglie wavelength of each, and what the ratios imply for the lenses and for resolution.

Plan. Use (19a.4), relativistic for the electron. The hinge is that momentum, and so rigidity, grows as √m at fixed energy, while wavelength falls as 1/√m.

Kinetic energy. Ek = eV = (1.602×10⁻¹⁹ C)(3.0×10⁴ V) = 4.81×10⁻¹⁵ J for both.

Electron momentum. Relativistic: p = 9.49×10⁻²³ kg·m/s (the non-relativistic form gives 9.36×10⁻²³, 1.46 % low; the electron moves at 0.328c).

Gallium momentum. m = 69.723 × 1.6605×10⁻²⁷ = 1.158×10⁻²⁵ kg; p = √(2mEk) = 3.34×10⁻²⁰ kg·m/s, at a speed of 2.88×10⁵ m/s.

Rigidity. Br = p/e: electron 5.93×10⁻⁴ T·m, gallium 0.208 T·m, a ratio of 351 (close to √(mGa/me) = 357; the difference is the electron's relativistic momentum). Why it matters: a magnetic lens's focal length scales as the square of the rigidity over the integral of B², so the same focal length would need about 1.2×10⁵ times the B² for gallium.

Wavelength. λ = h/p: electron 6.98 pm, gallium 0.0199 pm.

Rigidity 5.93×10⁻⁴ against 0.208 T·m (351×); wavelength 6.98 against 0.0199 pm. Check: a probe's diffraction disc is about 1.22λ/α, with α the probe's half-angle. At a milliradian (an illustrative value) the gallium disc is about 0.02 nm, far below the 2.5–6 nm smallest imaging beam size quoted for a modern FIB, so the ion spot is not diffraction-limited. The electron's disc at a few milliradians is 1–2 nm, which is one of the terms that set a SEM's probe size.

Two beams in one chamber. A FIB is usually built into a SEM (Deck 20 p. 7). The electron column stands vertically and the ion column is inclined to it, and the two beams are aimed at one point on the sample, so the same site can be imaged with electrons and milled with ions without moving it. Two attachments make the combination a workstation. A gas injection system (GIS) brings a fine needle close to the surface and releases a precursor gas, which the beam decomposes into a solid deposit where it is scanned (§6). A manipulator, a micro-positioned needle, lifts a cut piece out of the sample. Detectors of secondary and backscattered electrons form the images, and X-ray detectors identify elements by their characteristic lines (M09 §6), energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS).

An instrument maker proposes an ion column for helium ions at 30 kV with magnetic lenses, arguing that helium is light. By what factor does the magnetic rigidity of He⁺ (4.003 u) exceed that of a 30 kV electron, and how does that compare with gallium's? Is the argument sound?

For ions the non-relativistic momentum is exact enough, so the rigidity scales as √m: gallium's rigidity is 0.208 T·m, and helium's is 0.208 × √(4.003/69.723) = 0.208 × 0.240 = 0.0499 T·m. Against the electron's 5.93×10⁻⁴ T·m that is a factor of 84, against 351 for gallium. Helium is four times easier to bend than gallium but still 84 times harder than an electron, and for the same focal length the field must be about 84 times larger, its square (∫B²dz) about 7,000 times. The argument fails on the scaling: light for an ion is still heavy for a magnetic lens built for electrons. (Lecture 21 noted that helium and neon ion microscopes exist; they use a different source, not a liquid metal.)

6 · Milling, deposition and their limits

A FIB does two opposite things with one beam: it removes material by sputtering, and, with a precursor gas, it adds material by deposition. Lecture 21 called the result a microscopic CNC machine, subtractive and additive at once, and Deck 20's summary calls it a maskless, site-specific milling and deposition technique. Both processes leave traces in the sample, and those traces are the instrument's limitations. This section treats sputtering and how long a cut takes, deposition, the cross-section and lift-out procedures of failure analysis, and the damage and redeposition that limit them.

Sputtering. A gallium ion entering a solid loses its energy in collisions, by the nuclear stopping of M12 §3. Each recoiling target atom strikes others in turn, a collision cascade, and an atom near the surface that receives enough momentum directed outward leaves the solid. This is physical sputtering (Deck 20 p. 11). The number of atoms removed per incident ion, the sputter yield Y, depends on the ion, its energy and angle and the target; TRIM computes it with its surface-sputtering option (M13 §5). Because the beam is narrow and the yield is local, a FIB most often mills features of high aspect ratio, deep trenches with steep walls. The same conditions give different results in different materials (Deck 20 p. 10): four trenches cut under identical conditions in zinc, copper, silicon and aluminium differ in depth, wall profile and roughness, and copper's walls show heavy vertical striations, curtaining, the stripes that form when the milling rate varies across the face. Lecture 21 drew the conclusion that a milling recipe must be optimised for each material.

t = nVboxeIY
(19a.5)

Assumes every sputtered atom leaves the box for good; redeposition (below) lengthens the real time.
E8 · How long a cross-section box takes to mill

Given a box 10 µm × 10 µm × 5 µm deep to be removed from silicon, at beam currents of 1 nA and 10 nA (a modern FIB delivers up to tens of nanoamperes); silicon ρ = 2.330 g/cm³, M = 28.0855 g/mol; NA = 6.022×10²³ mol⁻¹. Find the number of atoms to remove, the ion arrival rate, and the milling time as a function of the yield Y.

Plan. Count atoms, count ions per second, and divide by (19a.5). The hinge is that the time scales as 1/(IY): the yield, which this page does not tabulate, enters only as a divisor, so the answer is written as tY.

Atom density. n = ρNA/M = (2.330)(6.022×10²³)/28.0855 = 4.99×10²² cm⁻³.

Atoms in the box. Vbox = (10×10⁻⁴)(10×10⁻⁴)(5×10⁻⁴) cm³ = 5.0×10⁻¹⁰ cm³ = 500 µm³; N = nV = 2.50×10¹³ atoms.

Ions per second. I/e = 1×10⁻⁹/1.602×10⁻¹⁹ = 6.24×10⁹ ions/s at 1 nA, 6.24×10¹⁰ at 10 nA.

Time. tY = N/(I/e) = 4.00×10³ s, 66.7 min, at 1 nA, and 400 s, 6.67 min, at 10 nA. For a yield of a few atoms per ion the cut takes tens of minutes at 1 nA.

t = 66.7/Y min at 1 nA and 6.67/Y min at 10 nA. Check: ten times the current gives one tenth of the time; the charge delivered, Ne/Y = 4.0×10³/Y nC, is the same at both currents. This is why a cross-section is roughed out at high current and finished at low current: the high current is fast but its wide spot leaves a rough face.

Deposition. With the gas injection system open, precursor molecules adsorb on the surface, and the ion beam decomposes them where it is scanned, leaving a solid (Deck 20 p. 12). Precursors that yield metals, tungsten, platinum, gold, aluminium and copper, are the most common; tungsten hexacarbonyl, W(CO)₆, is one. Insulators can be deposited as well. Every deposited film contains impurities, from incompletely decomposed precursor and from the gallium of the beam, so a FIB-deposited metal conducts worse than the bulk metal. Two uses matter for failure analysis. A protective strap of platinum deposited over a site before it is cut shields the surface from the beam's tails (Deck 20 p. 24). A circuit edit joins two lines with a deposited conductor, or isolates one by cutting it and filling the gap with insulator, so a fault can be confirmed by repairing it (Deck 20 p. 13); Lecture 21 described repairing an open line with a short platinum strap.

The cross-section and the lamella. A failure-analysis cut follows a fixed recipe. A platinum strap is laid over the site; a stepped trench is milled beside it at high current; the face of the trench nearest the site is polished at decreasing currents until it passes through the defect; and the face is imaged with the electron beam, tilted to look into the trench. For transmission electron microscopy (TEM), which needs a slice thin enough for electrons to pass through, the site is cut free as a lamella: trenches are milled on both sides of the strap to leave a thin wall; the manipulator needle is welded to the wall with deposited platinum (Lecture 21: "platinum is glue"); the last anchor is cut; the lamella is lifted out, welded to a holder, released, and thinned further (Deck 20 p. 9, the lift-out video of Lecture 21). Lecture 21 stressed two practical points: polish by stepping the milling rate down gradually, and expect a thin lamella to break, so that a successful lift-out at the first attempt is luck.

What the beam does to the sample. Deck 20 pp. 16 and 18 list the limitations, and Lecture 21 named redeposition as the key one. In a plasma etch the products of the reaction are volatile and are pumped away; in a wet etch they dissolve in the etchant. The atoms a FIB sputters do neither: they leave as solid atoms, and many land on nearby surfaces, including the walls of the trench being cut, which contaminates the region and partly refills the cut. Back-sputtered gallium ions deposit near the site as well. The beam also damages what it leaves behind: gallium is implanted and the surface made amorphous, to a depth of about 22 nm at 30 keV, falling to about 4 nm at 5 keV and 1 nm at 2 keV in one systematic study, and as a physically aggressive, brute-force etch it can induce stress. In the professor's work on silicon carbide films on silicon (pp. 17–18), the difference in thermal and mechanical stress between the two materials opened micro-cracks along their interface after FIB processing (p. 18), and Lecture 21 explained that a later wet etch of the silicon then entered the crack, removed the silicon under the structure, and left no disk at all. For failure analysis these effects set one rule: the artefacts a FIB makes, curtaining, redeposited material, an amorphous skin, cracks it opened itself, must be told apart from the defect it was sent to find.

A trench of 20 µm × 5 µm × 10 µm deep must be cut in silicon to expose a buried void, at 3 nA. What is the milling time for a yield Y, and why will the real time be longer than this estimate for a narrow, deep trench in particular?

Volume (20×10⁻⁴)(5×10⁻⁴)(10×10⁻⁴) = 1.0×10⁻⁹ cm³; atoms (4.99×10²²)(1.0×10⁻⁹) = 4.99×10¹³; ions per second 3×10⁻⁹/1.602×10⁻¹⁹ = 1.87×10¹⁰; so tY = 2.67×10³ s, 44.5/Y min. The estimate assumes every sputtered atom leaves. In a narrow, deep trench many sputtered atoms strike the opposite wall and stick, so they must be sputtered again: redeposition lowers the net removal per ion, and the deeper and narrower the trench, the smaller the fraction of sputtered atoms that escapes through its mouth. The beam also meets the sloping walls at grazing angles, which changes the local yield.

7 · Cross-sections of radiation damage

A heavy ion that destroys a power transistor often leaves nothing to see at the surface, or a mark that does not say how deep the damage goes. Deck 20 ends with case studies in which the FIB reveals the mechanism, and they connect failure analysis back to the radiation half of the course: single-event effects (M08 §8) are tested by irradiating parts (EEE 5416 M16 §6), and a part that fails in the beam is brought to the FIB. Lecture 21 gave the procedure: a group of devices on one test board is irradiated, the device that stops working is examined first under an optical microscope, then in a SEM, and then sectioned with the FIB at the suspect point.

The three silicon carbide (SiC) power MOSFET cases show three different signatures, each from a published study the deck cites:

  • A burned crater (Deck 20 p. 25; "Heavy-ion induced single event effects and latent damages in SiC power MOSFETs", Microelectronics Reliability 2021). Optical inspection shows the failure area and the SEM a crater; the FIB cross-section shows a deep burned region with a void, which Lecture 21 distinguished from curtaining, a milling artefact, by its shape and texture. This is single-event burnout: the heavy ion's charge track triggers a current that the drain bias sustains until the device melts locally.
  • A damaged gate oxide (p. 26; "SiC power MOSFETs after heavy ion irradiation", Electronics 2023). The packages are opened (decapsulated) to expose the wire-bonded chips. The slide marks a small burned-out point on the die (Lecture 21 described this case as having no visible burn); a FIB cut through it shows the gate oxide damaged between the polysilicon gate and the SiC, which the slide links to a large gate leakage current. The gate has lost control because current flows through its dielectric.
  • A burnout crater and its depth (p. 27; "Radiation-induced single event burnout in SiC-based power MOSFETs", IET Power Electronics, after irradiation with ¹⁸¹Ta ions). With the top metal stripped, the optical and SEM views locate a crater about 5.7 by 7.2 µm; a FIB section beneath it shows damage reaching about 24 µm into the die, and a second cut through the trench-gate structure shows the melted region. As in the first case, the energy that melts the material comes from the drain supply through the sustained current; the ion's track only decides where it starts. Lecture 21 added a general case: a void can lie below a surface that looks normal, and is then found only by cutting layer by layer and imaging after each cut.

The physics of these mechanisms, single-event burnout and gate rupture in power devices, is the subject of the single-event modules: M15 of this course, which is not yet built, and, in depth, EEE 5416 M16. What the FIB adds is the discrimination. A burnout, a damaged gate oxide and a void under a normal-looking surface can all end in the same electrical failure mode, a part that no longer switches, and Lecture 21 closed on the point that only a cross-section tells them apart, because radiation damage is small and often invisible at the surface.

Two further cases extend the method beyond radiation. A PIN diode limiter, a microwave protection diode, damaged by microwave pulses of about 5 GHz, 100 ns and 2 kW peak power (p. 28), shows voids at the edge of the diode in section. Lecture 21 attributed the location to field crowding: the electric field is concentrated at an edge, as at the tip of a needle, so damage starts there; knowing where it starts says which feature to redesign. And the solder joint of a capacitor on a printed circuit board (p. 29), sectioned to evaluate the bonding between layers, measure the thickness and uniformity of the intermetallic compound that forms between solder and copper, and find voids, cracks and interface defects, brings the method back to the joints of M19 §5. For a joint that size Lecture 21 advised X-ray imaging first and a FIB section only where time and cost allow, since the volume is large for a FIB.

Two SiC power MOSFETs fail in a heavy-ion test. Device A shows a dark spot at the surface and its drain–source path is shorted; device B looks undamaged but its gate leaks. In what order would you apply the techniques of this page to each, and what would you expect the FIB section to show?

Both begin at the terminals (curve tracer: A's drain–source short, B's gate leakage) and with optical inspection and SEM of the decapsulated die. For A the surface already locates the site, so the FIB section goes straight through the dark spot; a burnout would show a burned, melted region extending down from the surface, possibly with a void, distinct from curtaining. For B nothing locates the site at the surface. Thermal imaging with the gate biased would show where the leakage current dissipates, and the FIB section through that point would be expected to show the gate oxide damaged between gate and SiC. If no hot spot is found, cutting successive slices under the gate and imaging each is the remaining way to find damage that lies below the surface.

Summary

  • Failure analysis moves from failure background and failure mode (the terminals) through isolation of the site to the failure mechanism, and from non-destructive to destructive steps, so that the analysis cannot create what it finds; the loop closes with an improvement and a retest.
  • A curve tracer shows a short as a vertical trace; at 200 µA and 500 mV per division anything below about 25 Ω looks the same, so the value of a short needs a finer scale.
  • X-ray contrast comes from attenuation, steep in Z: a void brightens a ray by exp[(µ/ρ)ρd] (19a.1), 5 % for 5 µm in copper at 30 keV and 0.7 % at 60 keV. CT reconstructs µ in voxels; the voxel is p/M, and resolution is at least two voxels (19a.2).
  • Nano-CT of an HBM stack resolved micro-bumps with 600 nm voxels after a fast overview (30 s) and a long scan (75 min), and counted 1,680 voids in 1,275 bumps: a population, not an example.
  • Thermal imaging locates dissipation; subtracting an unpowered frame removes emissivity contrast. In the demultiplexer case it found a hot spot by the failing output, and deprocessing found electrical overstress at the output driver.
  • In foil resistors, notches, bridges and particles concentrate Joule power as 1/w² (19a.3), fatigue under power cycling and fracture with a positive resistance shift. Pulsed-power infrared imaging found them as hot spots, and each of the three life-test failures among 280 parts was a bridge it had flagged.
  • A FIB focuses Ga⁺ from a liquid-metal source with electrostatic lenses, because the ion's magnetic rigidity is about 350 times an electron's at the same voltage (19a.4); combined with a SEM, a GIS and a manipulator, it mills, deposits, cross-sections and lifts out lamellae. Milling time scales as 1/(IY) (19a.5).
  • Redeposition, gallium implantation, an amorphous surface and stress cracks are the FIB's own artefacts; a cross-section is still the only way to tell burnout, gate-oxide damage and a buried void apart.
