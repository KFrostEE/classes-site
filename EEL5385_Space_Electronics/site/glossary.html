Glossary and quick reference

Short identifications for every term the modules use, for when one appears in passing and the definition has slipped.

How to read this

Entries are deliberately short: the mechanism lives in the module, and this page carries only the identification and the numbers worth remembering. It covers the modules built so far — M01 to M12 — and grows as more are written. Every number here matches the module it came from; where a module later corrects a value, this page is corrected with it.

Semiconductor physics

TermWhat it isKey factsWhy it matters
Energy bandA continuous range of allowed electron energies in a crystal, formed when the discrete levels of many atoms split and mergeA silicon crystal holds 5 × 1022 atoms/cm³, so the splitting is unresolvably fineEverything about conduction follows from which bands are full and which are empty
Bandgap, EgThe forbidden energy range between the valence and conduction bandsSi 1.12 eV (the lectures round to 1.1); SiO₂ 8.9 eV; SiC 3.26 eV; diamond 5.47 eVSets carrier population, temperature limit and radiation response together
Valence bandThe highest band full of electrons at absolute zeroIts electrons are in chemical bonds and carry no net current when the band is fullVacancies in it are holes
Conduction bandThe lowest empty bandAn electron in it has empty states above it and so responds to a fieldConduction by electrons happens here
HoleA vacant state in the valence band, treated as a particle of charge +qMobility ~505 cm²/V·s in silicon, about a third of the electron valueHalf of every electron–hole pair that radiation creates
Electron–hole pairAn electron promoted to the conduction band plus the hole it leavesCreated identically by heat, light and ionizing particles. Mean creation energy 3.6 eV in Si, ~17 eV in SiO₂The unit of everything radiation does electrically
Intrinsic carrier concentration, niThe equal electron and hole population in an undoped crystal1.0 × 1010 cm−3 in Si at 300 K; exponential in Eg/2kT; doubles roughly every 10 KThe high-temperature limit of every silicon device traces back to it
DopingSubstituting a small fraction of lattice atoms with donors or acceptorsTypically 1015–1020 cm−3; P, As, Sb donate, B accepts; all shallow at 45–54 meVFixes the carrier population by design rather than by temperature
Majority / minority carrierThe abundant and the scarce carrier type in doped materialnp = ni2 in equilibrium: 1016 doping leaves 104 cm−3 of the minority typeRadiation effects that appear as current are minority-carrier effects
Mobility, µDrift velocity per unit electric fieldSi at 300 K: 1,450 (electrons), 505 (holes) cm²/V·s. Falls with doping and with temperature above ~100 KSets drive current, and is what strain engineering improves
Drift / diffusionCarrier motion driven by a field, and by a concentration gradientDrift acts within picoseconds inside a depletion region; diffusion is slower and less completeThe prompt spike and the slow tail of a particle-strike transient
Depletion regionThe carrier-free zone either side of a junction, holding fixed ionized dopants and a strong field0.43 µm at 1016/1016 doping unbiased, 1.22 µm at 5 V reverse; width ∝ √(Vbi+VR)The volume in which radiation-generated charge is collected
Built-in potential, VbiThe equilibrium potential step across a junction0.71 V for 1016/1016 siliconPresent with no bias applied; sets the unbiased depletion width
Reverse saturation current, I0The small minority-carrier current of a reverse-biased junction10−12–10−15 A typical; ∝ ni2, so doubles every ~5 K; the generation current, ∝ ni, is the one that doubles every ~10 K and dominates in siliconThe baseline that radiation and heat both raise

Transistors and manufacturing

TermWhat it isKey factsWhy it matters
MOSFETMetal–oxide–semiconductor field-effect transistor: source, drain, gate oxide and gate over a substrateConducts through an inversion channel a few nanometres thick at the oxide interfaceThe switch all digital electronics is built from
Threshold voltage, VthThe gate voltage at which the surface inverts and a channel formsShifted by charge trapped in the gate oxide, which is the central total-dose effectWhere "on" begins
Subthreshold swing, SGate voltage needed to change drain current by one decade below thresholdFloored at 59.5 mV/decade at 300 K; ∝ T, so 19.8 mV/dec at 100 K and 99.2 at 500 KThe floor is why supply voltages stopped falling, and why Dennard scaling ended
Off-state current, IoffThe current a transistor passes when nominally offNever zero. Multiplied by ~1010 devices it is a significant power termThe quantity that both temperature and radiation attack
Short-channel effectLoss of gate control when the drain's field reaches through a short channelThreshold falls with gate length and drain bias; swing rises above its floorEnded the planar transistor — not lithography
FinFET / tri-gateA transistor whose channel is a thin vertical fin with the gate on three sidesProduction from Intel's 22 nm node, 2011. Weff = 2Hfin + Wfin; width quantised in whole finsRestores gate control and raises drive per unit area at once
Gate-all-around / nanosheetStacked horizontal channel sheets with gate material around all four facesSamsung calls it MBCFET, Intel RibbonFET. Sheets are electrically in parallel: three sheets, one transistorThe current generation; width is continuously adjustable again
Strained siliconChannel silicon placed under mechanical strain by embedded SiGeIntroduced at 90 nm, 2003. Raises mobility with no dimensional changeThe template for modern scaling: change a term in the equation when the dimension will not move
Technology nodeThe name of a process generationCeased to be a physical dimension around 130 nm; physical gate length has flattened near 18 nmNot comparable between vendors — use transistor density instead
Standard cellA fixed-height layout block implementing one logic functionArea = cell height in metal tracks × width in gate pitches. Modern node gains come mostly from cell area, not device sizeThe real unit of density
Dennard scalingThe 1974 result that dividing all dimensions and the supply voltage by κ keeps power density constantHeld ~30 years; ended when VDD could no longer fallWhat actually delivered the benefits attributed to Moore's law
Moore's lawTransistor count per chip doubles about every two yearsEmpirical and economic. Describes count only — never speed, power or costStill partly holding while Dennard scaling does not
PhotolithographyProjecting a mask pattern onto photosensitive polymer to define chip features2w0 = (4λ/π)(F/D), equivalently CD = k1λ/NA with k1 floored at 0.25 for one exposure. Electron-beam reaches ~2 nm, EUV ~8 nmSets the smallest feature any process can make
DUV / immersionDeep-ultraviolet exposure at 248 nm (KrF) or 193 nm (ArF), the latter often through waterWater's index 1.44 at 193 nm raises NA from ~0.93 to 1.35Carried the industry for two decades while no shorter wavelength existed
Multiple patterningPrinting one layer with several interleaved exposuresEach exposure adds a mask, a process sequence and an alignment error budgetHow sub-resolution pitches were reached without EUV, and why doing so became expensive
EUVExtreme-ultraviolet lithography at 13.5 nmAll-reflective, in vacuum, Mo/Si multilayer mirrors at ~70 % each; source is a CO₂ laser striking tin droplets with a pre-pulse and a main pulse. Equipment cost quoted in the course as $880 MOne supplier worldwide, which makes access a strategic constraint

Radiation species, sources and decay

TermWhat it isKey factsWhy it matters
Alpha particle, αA helium-4 nucleus — two protons and two neutrons, no electronsCharge +2e; 3–7 MeV in this course, 4–9 MeV across all emitters; monoenergetic per isotope; ~4 % of cStops in tens of micrometres of solid. The densest ioniser per unit path of the common species
Beta particle, β−An electron created in a nucleus when a neutron converts to a protonContinuous energy spectrum up to an endpoint, because the decay energy is shared with an antineutrino; mean energy about a third of the endpointThe only common species that produces significant bremsstrahlung, so its shield must be chosen for low Z
ProtonA hydrogen nucleus938.3 MeV/c²; keV to hundreds of MeV in spaceThe dominant trapped species and the dominant solar particle; sets dose behind any practical shield
Heavy ionA nucleus heavier than helium, stripped of its electronsCharge +Ze; energy loss scales as Z² at equal velocity, so iron deposits ~676× as densely as a protonRare but disproportionately destructive — the species behind destructive single-event effects
NeutronAn uncharged nuclear constituent of nearly proton massDoes not ionise directly; free half-life ~10 min; energy transfer per collision is 4Mm/(M+m)², maximised against hydrogenModerated by water and paraffin, not by lead. All of its observable effects come from secondaries
X-ray / gamma rayPhotons, named by origin rather than by energy: X-rays from electron processes, gammas from nuclear transitionsGammas are discrete lines; X-ray tubes give a continuum with an endpoint at the tube voltageThe same photon energy behaves identically whichever name it carries
Delta rayA secondary electron liberated with enough kinetic energy to ionise further atomsA role, not a species — identical to a beta particle of the same energyProduces most of the pairs along a heavy-ion track, and sets the track's radial extent
Caesium-137Fission product used as the reference gamma source30.1 y; one line at 662 keV, in 85.1 % of decays; output falls ~2 %/yearStable calibration over years. The standard dosimetry source
Cobalt-60Activation product used as the workhorse total-dose source5.27 y; two lines at 1.17 and 1.33 MeV, mean 1.25 MeV; activity falls ~12 %/yearHigher mean energy penetrates thick test articles uniformly; the price is replacement every decade or so
Half-life, t1/2Time for half a population of nuclei to decayλ = ln2/t1/2; N = N0e−λtWhole numbers of half-lives are halvings; no exponential arithmetic needed
Activity, ADecays per second in a sourceA = λN, in becquerel; 1 Ci = 3.7 × 1010 BqCompute N from the mass of the isotope, not of the element — the usual error
Quality factor, QWeighting that converts absorbed dose to equivalent dose for biological riskCourse table: photons and betas 1, thermal neutrons 5, fast neutrons 10, alphas 20Has no meaning for a device; component hardness is always absorbed dose

The space radiation environment

TermWhat it isKey factsWhy it matters
Proton–proton chainThe fusion sequence that powers the Sun4p → ⁴He + 2e⁺ + 2ν, releasing 26.7 MeV; the first branch supplies ~85 % of solar energyFixes the 5,772 K surface temperature and therefore the photon spectrum and thermal environment
Solar windContinuous outflow of coronal plasmaProtons and electrons at 300–800 km/s, a few cm−3, 0.5–3 keV per protonCannot penetrate a wall. Causes surface charging, sputtering, and shapes the magnetosphere
Solar flareSudden release of magnetic energy as electromagnetic radiationArrives in 8 min 20 s; classified A–X by peak soft X-ray flux, each letter a decadeAffects the sunlit atmosphere and exposed surfaces; deposits no charge inside a device
Coronal mass ejectionExpulsion of ~1012 kg of coronal plasma with its magnetic field250–3,000 km/s; arrives in 15 hours to 3 daysThe basis of operational space-weather warning, since it can be seen leaving the Sun
Solar energetic particlesProtons and heavier ions accelerated by a CME shock or flare reconnectionMeV to ~1 GeV; raise the proton flux 10³–10⁵× for hours to days; peak at solar maximumDominate the radiation budget outside the trapped belts, and outrun any warning
Galactic cosmic raysFully ionised nuclei from outside the solar system87 % p, 12 % He, 1 % heavier; ~4 cm−2s−1; peak at hundreds of MeV/nucleon; worst at solar minimumPractically unshieldable; dominate single-event rates beyond the belts
Solar cycleThe ~11-year rise and fall of solar magnetic activity9–13 years; modelled as 7 years maximum plus 4 years minimumSolar particles peak at maximum; GCR and inner-belt protons peak at minimum — opposite worst cases
MagnetosphereThe cavity in the solar wind occupied by Earth's magnetic field~10 RE sunward, a tail hundreds of RE long; surface field ~50 µTDeflects charged particles; the cut-off energy is highest at the equator and zero over the poles
Van Allen beltsRegions where the geomagnetic field holds energetic charged particles for yearsInner belt 1.2–2.5 RE, protons to several hundred MeV; outer belt 3–7 RE, electrons to ~10 MeVThe largest radiation hazard near Earth. Belt geometry, not altitude, sets orbital risk
Slot regionThe depleted gap between the belts, 2–3 REKept empty by wave scattering; fills in hours during a storm and drains over weeksThe one part of the belt structure that changes on an operator's timescale
Gyration, bounce, driftThe three motions that trap a particleHelix around a field line (µs–ms); mirroring between poles (fractions of a second); circling the planet (minutes–hours)Together they confine a particle to a shell of field lines, which is why belts are mapped against field coordinates
Mirror pointWhere a converging field reverses a particle's motion along a lineConserves mv⊥²/2B; position depends only on equatorial pitch angle, not on mass or energyParticles mirroring below ~100 km hit the atmosphere — the loss cone, the only exit from the trap
Ring currentNet westward current from ions and electrons drifting in opposite sensesIts field opposes and measurably weakens the surface fieldThe standard index of geomagnetic storm intensity
South Atlantic AnomalyRegion where the inner belt reaches its lowest altitude, ~200 kmCaused by the offset dipole, deepened by a reversed-flux patch at the core–mantle boundarySupplies most of the trapped-particle dose and upsets for any low-orbit spacecraft
Lagrange pointLocation where two bodies' gravity supplies exactly the centripetal force for a matched orbital periodL1 and L2 lie 1.5 million km sunward and anti-sunward; SOHO at L1, JWST and Roman at L2No trapped particles at all — the environment is solar particles plus GCR
Fluence, ΦFlux integrated over time, in cm−2The course's plots use 11-year integral fluence above a threshold energyDesign uses fluence, not flux: damage is a time integral, which is why mission duration is part of the environment

Interaction mechanisms and effects

TermWhat it isKey factsWhy it matters
IonizationComplete removal of an electron from an atom by a passing charged particleRequires more than the binding energy; occurs close to the track; the dominant energy-loss channelCreates the mobile charge behind total dose and every single-event effect
ExcitationPromotion of an electron to a higher bound state without removing itOccurs farther from the track than ionization; de-excites by emitting an ultraviolet photon; 1.1 eV across the silicon gapIn a semiconductor it already produces a mobile electron and hole, so it raises leakage like ionization does
Pair creation energy, EpairMean energy spent per electron–hole pair generated3.6 eV in Si, ~17 eV in SiO₂. The course instead divides by the 99.42 eV Si 2p core-level binding energySets collected charge. The two conventions differ by a factor near 28
BremsstrahlungPhoton emitted when a charged particle's trajectory is bent by a nucleusRadiated power ∝ 1/m² and ∝ Z²; yield f = 0.0007ZE(MeV)Converts a stoppable electron flux into penetrating X-rays — use low-Z shields first
Cherenkov radiationLight emitted when a charged particle exceeds c/n in a mediumThreshold ~260 keV for electrons in water; carries well under 1 % of collision lossesNegligible as energy loss; the basis of several particle detectors
Characteristic X-rayPhoton from an electron filling an inner-shell vacancyFixed by the element: W Kα₁ 59.3 keV and Kβ₁ 67.2 keV; Cu 8.05 and 8.90 keVIdentifies an X-ray tube's anode material from its spectrum
Duane–Hunt limitThe highest photon energy an X-ray tube can produceEmax = eVtube; the meaning of “kVp”The tube voltage is the single control over the beam's maximum energy
Beam hardeningRise in a beam's mean energy caused by filtrationPhotoelectric absorption falls as ~1/E³, so filters remove low-energy photons preferentiallyMakes dose uniform through a sample's depth, at the cost of dose rate
Half-value layerThickness of a reference material that halves a beam's intensityX-RAD 320: 3 Gy/min at ~1 mm Cu HVL, 1 Gy/min at ~4 mmHow a polyenergetic beam's penetrating power is specified
Photoelectric absorptionComplete absorption of a photon by a bound atomic electron, which is ejectedσ ∝ Z⁴–Z⁵ and E−3; the photon is destroyed; Te = Eph − Eb; a free electron cannot absorb a photon at allDominant below 56.9 keV in silicon and 556 keV in lead; still a fifth of lead's attenuation at Co-60 energies, which is why high-Z materials shield
Compton scatteringPartial energy transfer from a photon to a quasi-free electron; the photon survives with longer wavelengthΔλ = 2.426 pm × (1 − cos θ); σ per atom ∝ Z, so per gram it follows Z/A ≈ 0.5 and barely variesThe only interaction that matters at Co-60 energies in silicon; it degrades a beam rather than removing it, which is why shields need buildup factors
Pair productionConversion of a photon into an electron and a positron near a nucleusThreshold 2mec² = 1.022 MeV; σ ∝ Z²; the nucleus absorbs the momentum; the positron annihilates into two 0.511 MeV photonsDominant only above 14.3 MeV in silicon; just 0.06 % of silicon's cross-section at 1.25 MeV. Being above threshold does not make it significant
Absorption edgePhoton energy at which a new electron shell becomes accessible to photoelectric absorptionSi K edge 1.839 keV, where µ/ρ steps from 309 to 3192 cm²/g; Si L edge ~99.8 eVWhy a NIST table lists the same energy twice — use the lower value below the edge, the upper above
Mass attenuation coefficient, µ/ρProbability per unit areal mass that a photon interactscm²/g; µ = (µ/ρ)ρ; independent of physical form. Si at 1 MeV: 6.361 × 10⁻²The tabulated quantity every photon shielding calculation starts from
Mass energy-absorption coefficient, µen/ρThe part of the removed energy that stays behind as secondary-electron kinetic energySi at 1 MeV: 2.778 × 10⁻² cm²/g, less than half of µ/ρAttenuation problems use µ/ρ; dose problems use µen/ρ
Buildup factor, BMultiplier accounting for scattered photons that still reach the far side of a shield≥ 1; equals 1 only for a narrow collimated beam; grows with shield thickness in mean free pathsThe plain exponential law is a lower bound on what penetrates a real shield
Dose enhancementLocal dose increase near an interface between materials of different atomic numberHigh-Z material makes more secondary electrons per gram, and they cross into the low-Z side; strongest in the photoelectric regionWhy a part tested in a low-energy X-ray cabinet can respond differently from the same part at Co-60 energies
Stopping power, SRate at which a charged particle loses kinetic energy with distance−dE/dx; mass form in MeV·cm²/g; splits into electronic and nuclear; ∝ z²/β²The quantity SRIM computes, and the origin of both the Bragg peak and LET
Nuclear stoppingEnergy lost in elastic, screened-Coulomb collisions with whole target atomsNot the strong force and not a nuclear reaction; 0.05 % of the total for 10 MeV protons in Si; peaks near end of rangeThe part of the energy loss that makes displacements rather than charge
Linear energy transfer, LETThe locally deposited part of a particle's energy lossMeV·cm²/mg; excludes delta rays that escape the track; in Si, 1 MeV·cm²/mg liberates 10.4 fC/µmThe axis of every single-event cross-section curve, and the bridge to critical charge
CSDA rangeAverage path length a charged particle travels before stopping∫dE/S; in g/cm²; protons in Si 16.5 µm at 1 MeV, 715 µm at 10 MeVWhether a particle reaches the active layer or stops in the package
Projected range and detour factorPenetration depth along the original direction, and its ratio to path lengthfd = 0.995 for a 10 MeV proton in Si, 0.55 at 10 keV, far smaller for electronsPenetration depth, not path length, is what an engineer needs
StragglingStatistical spread in where identical particles stopLongitudinal and lateral components; of order 1 % of range for MeV protons in SiA range is a distribution, never a sharp cut-off
Bragg peakSharp maximum in energy deposition just before a charged particle stopsCaused by the 1/β² term; peak-to-entrance ratio 15.9 for one 10 MeV proton in Si, ~5 averaged over a beam, and only ~1.6 for iron — heavier ions have blunter peaksThe basis of proton therapy, and the reason a shield that stops a particle inside the die maximises damage
Displacement damagePermanent removal of an atom from its lattice site by a nuclear collisionThreshold Ed ~21 eV in Si; volumetric and cumulativeDegrades minority-carrier lifetime — the governing effect for solar cells, imagers and optocouplers
Frenkel pairA vacancy and an interstitial, created together by one displacementMost recombine within picoseconds; ~30 % of a dense cascade survivesThe elementary unit of displacement damage; survivors form stable complexes with impurities
Primary knock-on atomThe first atom displaced, carrying away most of the transferred energyUp to 133 keV from a 1 MeV neutron on silicon; then starts a branching cascadeOne incident particle produces hundreds to thousands of displacements in a region tens of nm across
NIELNon-ionizing energy loss — energy going to atomic displacement rather than ionizationMeV·cm²/g; × fluence gives displacement damage dose in MeV/gDegradation scales with it largely independently of species, which is what makes single-species testing valid
Carrier removalLoss of free carriers when radiation-induced donor and acceptor levels compensate the dopingRaises resistivity; at high fluence can invert the material's typeOne of the four device consequences of a defect level in the gap
AnnealingPost-irradiation evolution of the defect populationVacancies and interstitials are mobile at room temperature; part of the damage recovers in minutes to hoursTest standards specify the interval between exposure and measurement because the answer moves
Critical charge, QcritCharge a circuit node must lose before its stored state is corrupted≈ node capacitance × supply voltage; both fall with scaling while deposited charge does notWhy mature process nodes are attractive for space — the margin, not any total-dose advantage

The space environment and mission engineering

TermWhat it isKey factsWhy it matters
Solar irradiance, SSolar power per unit area at a given distance1,361 W/m² at 1 AU; falls as 1/r²Sets both the thermal environment and the available electrical power
Radiative equilibrium temperatureThe temperature at which a body's radiated power equals its absorbed power278 K for a sphere at 1 AU with αs = εSpace is not a coolant; only radiation removes heat, and it is weak
Solar absorptance / emittance, αs, εFraction of incident solar energy absorbed, and infrared emission efficiencyOnly the ratio matters; both are surface propertiesThermal control is done with coatings and insulation, not bulk material
OutgassingRelease of volatiles from polymers and adhesives in vacuumLimited by total mass loss and collected volatile condensable materialReleased material condenses on cold optics and radiators
QualificationDemonstrating on the ground that hardware survives the flight environmentThermal cycling, thermal vacuum, random vibration, acoustic, shock, total dose, single-event testingThere is no repair after launch
Technology readiness levelA nine-point scale from observed principle to flight-proven systemDefined in the NASA Systems Engineering Handbook. TRL 7 and 8 are reachable on the ground or in space; only TRL 9 requires flightCreates a circular barrier to any part that has not flown
Flight heritageThe record of how often and how successfully a part has flownRemoves qualification cost and schedule riskProduces lock-in: manufacturers report being unable to retire long-flown designs
Rad-hard by designRadiation tolerance obtained from circuit and layout choices on a standard processRedundant cells, error correction, triple redundancy, latch-up guard structuresAvoids needing a custom fabrication process, at a cost in area, power and speed
UpscreeningTesting commercial parts against mission requirements and flying those that passRequires single-lot traceability, because a process change invalidates the characterisationNow standard for short-duration and constellation missions
RAD750Radiation-hardened PowerPC-derived processor. BAE Systems designed it at Manassas under licence to the IBM/Motorola PowerPC 750 core, and manufactures it; introduced 2001, first flew 2005~10.4 M transistors, 250/150 nm, 110–200 MHz, ~5 W, −55 to +125 °C, hundreds of krad(Si), of order $10⁵ per flight unit — the widely quoted $200,000 (2002) is the predecessor RAD6000's list priceThe standard example of trading speed for power, temperature range and dose tolerance
Mass, power, volumeThe three budgets every spacecraft subsystem is allocated againstCoupled: shielding costs mass, mass costs launch energy, power costs radiator areaWhy no space design decision is made in isolation
