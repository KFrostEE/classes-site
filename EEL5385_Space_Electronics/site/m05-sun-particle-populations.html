Module 05

The Sun and the Particle Populations of Deep Space

Where the radiation in the solar system is produced: the fusion reaction that powers the Sun, the four distinct things the Sun emits, the eleven-year cycle that modulates all of them, and the galactic component that arrives from outside.

Prerequisites M04 Read time ~40 min Sources Decks 3–4; Lectures 3–4

M04 catalogued the species of radiation without saying where any of them comes from. In the space environment there are three suppliers, and they differ in almost every respect that matters: the Sun, which produces a continuous low-energy outflow punctuated by violent events; the galaxy beyond the solar system, which produces a steady trickle of extremely energetic nuclei; and Earth's own magnetic field, which does not produce particles at all but concentrates them into trapped populations far denser than the surrounding space. This module covers the first two. The third is M06.

The organising fact is that the Sun emits four physically distinct things, on four different timescales, and a spacecraft designer must treat them separately. Photons arrive continuously and set the thermal environment. The solar wind flows outward at all times and carries particles of too little energy to penetrate any spacecraft wall. Flares release electromagnetic radiation in minutes. Coronal mass ejections and the energetic particles associated with them arrive hours to days later and are capable of destroying electronics. Confusing any of these with another produces predictions that are wrong by many orders of magnitude, so the module separates them explicitly before combining them into the fluence a mission actually accumulates.

1 · The reaction that powers the Sun

The Sun is a self-gravitating sphere of hydrogen and helium whose core is compressed to about 1.5 × 107 K and 150 g/cm³. At that temperature protons have enough kinetic energy that a small fraction of collisions bring two of them close enough for the attractive nuclear force to overcome their Coulomb repulsion. The resulting fusion sequence, the proton–proton chain, is the source of all the energy the Sun radiates, and it proceeds in three steps.

In the first step, two protons combine and one of them converts into a neutron, producing a deuterium nucleus — one proton and one neutron — together with a positron and a neutrino. This step is the slow one: it requires a weak-interaction conversion of a proton into a neutron at the moment of contact, and its improbability is what makes the Sun burn for billions of years rather than exploding. In the second step, the deuterium nucleus captures another proton to form helium-3, releasing a gamma ray. In the third, two helium-3 nuclei combine to form helium-4 and release two protons back into the plasma.

The net reaction converts four protons into one helium-4 nucleus and releases about 26.7 MeV, which is 0.7 % of the rest mass of the four protons. The three steps above are the first and dominant branch of the chain, supplying roughly 85 % of the Sun's energy; two further branches convert helium-3 by way of beryllium-7 instead of by meeting a second helium-3, and account for most of the remainder. They matter for neutrino astronomy and not for the radiation environment, so the first branch is the one carried forward here. Almost all of that energy appears as gamma rays and as the kinetic energy of the products, and it is thermalised in the dense core; the photons that eventually leave the surface bear no resemblance to the gamma rays that were created, having been absorbed and re-emitted over a passage that takes on the order of a hundred thousand years. The neutrinos alone leave immediately and unaltered, because they interact so weakly that the whole Sun is transparent to them.

The relevance of the core reaction to a radiation-effects course is indirect but real. It fixes the Sun's surface temperature at about 5,772 K, which fixes the photon spectrum in §2 and therefore the thermal environment of every mission. It also supplies the free protons that the Sun's magnetic activity later accelerates: the particles that damage electronics are ordinary solar-atmosphere protons that have been given energy by processes in the corona, not fusion products.

2 · The photon output

The Sun radiates as an approximate blackbody at 5,772 K, giving a spectrum that peaks in the visible near 500 nm and falls away on both sides. Integrated over all wavelengths, the flux at Earth's orbital distance is the solar constant, about 1,361 W/m², and it is this figure that sets spacecraft thermal design (M03). Almost all of the energy lies between 300 nm and 3 µm, in the near-ultraviolet, visible and near-infrared.

The part of the spectrum that matters for radiation effects is the far tail on the short-wavelength side, which contains a negligible fraction of the total energy and is nonetheless the whole story. Extreme ultraviolet and X-ray photons from the solar corona are ionising; visible and infrared photons are not. Because the corona is hotter than the photosphere below it — millions of kelvin against thousands — the short-wavelength output does not follow the 5,772 K blackbody at all, and it varies by orders of magnitude with solar activity while the total irradiance varies by about 0.1 %.

Earth's upper atmosphere absorbs every solar photon below 200 nm before it reaches the ground, which is why the ionising part of the solar spectrum plays no part in terrestrial life and a large part in spacecraft design. The absorption is what creates the ionosphere: photons energetic enough to strip electrons from atmospheric molecules leave behind a partially ionised layer between roughly 60 and 1,000 km altitude, and the free-electron density there varies with solar activity strongly enough to disturb radio propagation and to change the drag on satellites in low orbit.

3 · The solar wind

The solar corona is hot enough — one to two million kelvin — that its outer layers are not gravitationally bound, and plasma escapes continuously in all directions. This outflow is the solar wind: mostly protons and electrons in equal numbers, with a few percent of helium nuclei, streaming past Earth at 300–800 km/s with a density of a few particles per cubic centimetre.

Across that speed range an individual proton carries between about 0.5 and 3 keV of kinetic energy, close to 1 keV at typical wind speeds. That is far too little to penetrate anything: a 1 keV proton is stopped by less than a micrometre of aluminium, so the solar wind never reaches an internal component. Its importance is entirely at the surface. It erodes external coatings by sputtering, degrades solar-cell cover glass and thermal-control paint, and, most consequentially, deposits charge on spacecraft surfaces. Differential charging between surfaces of different conductivity can build potentials of thousands of volts, and the resulting discharge is a well-documented cause of spacecraft anomalies even though the particles causing it are individually harmless.

The solar wind's second role is structural. It carries the Sun's magnetic field outward with it and, on reaching Earth, compresses the geomagnetic field on the sunward side and draws it into a long tail on the night side. That deformed cavity is the magnetosphere, and its shape — which the solar wind sets and changes — determines where trapped particles can exist. M06 takes this up.

4 · Solar flares

A solar flare is a sudden release of magnetic energy in the solar atmosphere, occurring where twisted field lines reconnect into a lower-energy configuration. The energy appears as a burst of electromagnetic radiation across the whole spectrum, with the largest relative increase in X-rays and extreme ultraviolet, and the largest flares release on the order of 1025 joules in minutes.

Two properties follow from the fact that a flare's primary output is electromagnetic. It travels at the speed of light, so it arrives 8 minutes and 20 seconds after it occurs, and there is no possibility of warning: the light announcing the flare and the radiation from it arrive together. And it affects only what the photons can reach — the sunlit side of Earth's atmosphere, where the sudden ionisation disrupts high-frequency radio, and the exposed surfaces of spacecraft. Flares are classified by their peak soft X-ray flux measured at Earth, in a logarithmic sequence of letters A, B, C, M and X, each step a factor of ten, with the X class open-ended above 10−4 W/m².

A flare and a coronal mass ejection are different events with different physics, different arrival times and different consequences, although large examples of each usually accompany the other. The flare is electromagnetic radiation and arrives in 8 minutes. The mass ejection is matter and arrives in one to three days. A flare cannot deposit charge inside a device; the particles associated with an ejection can destroy one. Keeping them separate is the difference between a useful space-weather forecast and a useless one.

5 · Coronal mass ejections and solar energetic particles

A coronal mass ejection is the expulsion of a large volume of coronal plasma and its embedded magnetic field into interplanetary space — on the order of 1012 kg of material, travelling at anything from 250 to 3,000 km/s. Unlike the flare, this is matter in bulk, and its transit to Earth's orbit takes between about 15 hours for the fastest events and several days for slow ones. That delay is the entire basis of operational space-weather warning: a coronagraph that observes an ejection leaving the Sun provides hours of notice before the plasma arrives.

The damaging radiation associated with these events is the population of solar energetic particles: protons, and a smaller number of heavier nuclei, accelerated to energies from a few MeV to occasionally above 1 GeV by the shock front the ejection drives ahead of itself and by the reconnection region of the associated flare. A solar particle event raises the proton flux at Earth by three to five orders of magnitude for hours to days. These are the particles that produce the single-event failures and the accumulated dose that dominate a mission's radiation budget outside the trapped belts.

Energy determines arrival time. The most energetic protons travel at a large fraction of the speed of light and arrive within tens of minutes of the eruption, ahead of any possible warning; the bulk of the event follows over hours as slower particles arrive; the plasma cloud itself arrives a day or more later. Spacecraft operators therefore have essentially no warning of the most penetrating particles and a day of warning of the geomagnetic disturbance.

6 · The solar cycle

Solar activity is not steady. The number of sunspots — regions of intense, concentrated magnetic field on the photosphere — rises and falls in a cycle whose length varies between about 9 and 13 years, conventionally taken as 11. Flares, coronal mass ejections and solar particle events all track the sunspot number, because all of them draw on the same stored magnetic energy. The cycle is the single largest source of variability in the space radiation environment, and every mission radiation analysis must state where in the cycle it assumes the mission flies.

For radiation-environment modelling the cycle is conventionally divided into 7 years of solar maximum and 4 years of solar minimum within each 11-year period. This partition is not a physical boundary but a modelling convention that makes integrated fluences computable: solar particle fluences are accumulated using the solar-maximum portion, since events during solar minimum contribute negligibly.

The cycle acts in opposite directions on the two non-solar populations, and this is the reason a mission can face its worst environment at either phase depending on orbit. Solar particle events follow solar activity, so they are worst at solar maximum. Galactic cosmic rays are excluded from the inner solar system by the outflowing solar wind and its magnetic field, so they are worst at solar minimum, when that exclusion is weakest. Trapped protons in the inner belt also peak at solar minimum, for a reason developed in M06.

7 · Galactic cosmic rays

Galactic cosmic rays are fully ionised atomic nuclei arriving from outside the solar system, accelerated over millions of years by supernova shock fronts and other galactic processes. By number they are about 87 % protons, 12 % helium nuclei and 1 % heavier nuclei covering the whole periodic table up to iron and beyond. Their energy spectrum peaks near a few hundred MeV per nucleon and falls steeply above it, with the galactic population understood to extend to roughly 1018–1019 eV. Individual cosmic rays above 1020 eV have been observed, but no known galactic accelerator can reach those energies and they are attributed to extragalactic sources; they are far too rare to affect a spacecraft.

The flux is small — roughly 4 particles per square centimetre per second in interplanetary space, nearly isotropic — and it is essentially constant on human timescales, varying only through the factor of two or so of solar modulation over the cycle. That combination of very low flux and very high energy makes galactic cosmic rays qualitatively different from every other population in this course.

Galactic cosmic rays cannot be shielded against in any practical sense. An iron nucleus at 1 GeV per nucleon passes through several centimetres of aluminium, and the material it does traverse generates a shower of secondary particles. Added aluminium keeps helping only to roughly 20–30 g/cm²; beyond that the secondary neutrons and light fragments produced by target fragmentation offset the reduction in primary flux, and further mass buys diminishing or reversed returns at a cost in launch mass no mission will pay. Their contribution to total dose is small because the flux is small. Their contribution to single-event upset is dominant in orbits outside the trapped belts, because the iron component deposits energy densely enough to disturb a circuit node with a single particle regardless of shielding. This is why interplanetary missions are designed to tolerate upsets rather than to prevent them.

8 · Observing the environment

Space weather is monitored operationally, and the data are public. Two vantage points do most of the work.

The Lagrange point L1 lies about 1.5 million kilometres from Earth directly toward the Sun. A Lagrange point is a location where the combined gravitational attraction of two massive bodies supplies exactly the centripetal force needed to hold a small third body in an orbit with the same period as the second — so a spacecraft placed there keeps station between Earth and the Sun without continuous thrust. There are five such points for any two-body system. L1 is the natural place to watch the Sun: it has an uninterrupted view, and the solar wind — at the 300–800 km/s of §3 — covers the remaining 1.5 million kilometres in roughly 30 to 80 minutes, which is exactly the warning interval operators need. The Solar and Heliospheric Observatory has occupied L1 since 1996 and its coronagraphs are the primary detector of coronal mass ejections.

Geostationary orbit carries the particle detectors. The GOES satellites measure proton flux, electron flux and soft X-ray flux continuously, and their feeds are the operational definition of a radiation storm. The current values are published by the NOAA Space Weather Prediction Center as the proton flux, electron flux, X-ray flux and space-weather overview products cited in Deck 3.

Source discrepancy — Lagrange point assignments. Lecture 4 states that the James Webb Space Telescope and the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope occupy L4, and attributes their low radiation risk to their sitting in Earth's shadow. Both telescopes are in fact at L2, 1.5 million kilometres from Earth directly away from the Sun, and neither is shadowed: Earth's umbra ends about 1.4 million kilometres out, and both spacecraft fly halo orbits around L2 chosen specifically to avoid eclipse so that their solar arrays stay illuminated. Their low trapped-particle risk comes from being far outside the magnetosphere, where no trapped population exists; what remains is solar energetic particles and galactic cosmic rays, and the NASA orbit table on Deck 4 p. 12 records exactly that — no trapped protons, solar particles present, low risk at solar minimum and moderate at maximum. SOHO, at L1, is correctly placed on Deck 3 p. 21.

9 · What a mission actually accumulates

Instantaneous flux is the wrong quantity for design. A spacecraft accumulates damage over years, through quiet periods and storms, so the quantity that determines both total dose and the expected number of single-event failures is fluence: flux integrated over time, in particles per square centimetre. The course's plots use the integral fluence accumulated over one full 11-year solar cycle, which is the natural unit because it averages over a complete activity cycle.

Integral fluence is quoted as a function of a threshold energy: the value at 10 MeV is the fluence of all particles with energy above 10 MeV. Plotted that way, the curve falls monotonically, and its shape is more informative than its magnitude. The steepness tells you how quickly the population runs out of energy, and therefore how much shielding buys.

Three readings of that figure carry into the rest of the course. The trapped electron spectrum ends abruptly, so electron dose is a shielding problem with a solution. The proton spectrum does not end, so proton dose is a shielding problem without one, and the design response is to accept a dose and qualify parts to it. And the ordering between orbits — medium Earth orbit and highly elliptical orbit worst, low Earth orbit lowest — is set entirely by how much time the orbit spends inside the trapped belts, which is the subject of the next two modules.

A mission is being designed for a five-year operational life and must choose between launching at solar minimum and at solar maximum. Which phase gives the larger accumulated single-event upset count in interplanetary space, and which gives the larger total dose?

The two questions have opposite answers, which is why the trade is not obvious.

Total dose is larger for a mission flown through solar maximum. Dose is accumulated from large numbers of moderately energetic particles, and solar energetic particle events — which occur almost exclusively near solar maximum and raise the proton flux by three to five orders of magnitude for days at a time — supply far more of them than any other source in interplanetary space.

Single-event upset count from galactic cosmic rays is larger at solar minimum. Upsets from cosmic rays are caused by individual heavy nuclei depositing energy densely, and the galactic flux is modulated inversely by solar activity: the outflowing solar wind and its embedded magnetic field exclude low-energy galactic particles from the inner solar system, and that exclusion is weakest when the Sun is quiet. The cosmic-ray flux therefore roughly doubles at solar minimum. Missions are conventionally analysed for both cases: the worst-case dose is computed at solar maximum and the worst-case upset rate at solar minimum.

Summary

  • The proton–proton chain fuses four protons into helium-4 and releases 26.7 MeV, through a rate-limiting first step in which a proton converts to a neutron. It sets the Sun's 5,772 K surface temperature and supplies the protons that solar activity later accelerates.
  • The Sun emits four distinct things on four timescales: photons continuously, the solar wind continuously, flare radiation in 8 minutes, and coronal mass ejections with their energetic particles over hours to days.
  • The solar wind is ~1 keV protons and electrons at 300–800 km/s. It cannot penetrate a spacecraft wall; it damages surfaces and deposits charge, and it shapes the magnetosphere.
  • A flare is electromagnetic and arrives at c, classified A–X by peak soft X-ray flux with each letter a decade. A coronal mass ejection is ~1012 kg of plasma arriving 15 hours to 3 days later.
  • Solar energetic particles — MeV to ~1 GeV protons accelerated by the associated shock — raise the proton flux by 10³–10⁵ for hours to days and dominate the radiation budget outside the belts.
  • The solar cycle runs 9–13 years, conventionally 11, and is modelled as 7 years of maximum plus 4 of minimum. Solar particle events peak at maximum; galactic cosmic rays and inner-belt protons peak at minimum.
  • Galactic cosmic rays are 87 % protons, 12 % helium and 1 % heavier nuclei at ~4 cm−2s−1, energies centred at hundreds of MeV/nucleon. They cannot be shielded against practically and dominate the upset rate outside the belts.
  • L1, 1.5 million km sunward, gives 30–60 minutes of solar-wind warning and hosts SOHO; GOES in geostationary orbit supplies the operational proton, electron and X-ray flux feeds.
  • Design uses fluence, not flux — 11-year integral fluence above a threshold energy. Trapped electron fluence cuts off near 7 MeV and can be shielded away; proton fluence extends past 300 MeV and cannot.
