Module 21

Photodetectors for Space

How a semiconductor junction turns light into current, how much current a watt of light gives, what sets the smallest signal a detector can see, why every infrared detector must be cold, which materials reach which wavelengths, how a detector becomes an imaging array, what a superconducting nanowire adds, and what radiation does to all of them — with the detectors of the James Webb Space Telescope as the worked case.

Prerequisites M01 §1–§3, §6 (bands, doping, the p–n junction), M10 §1–§2 (photon energy, band-regime absorption), M17a §2, §5 (ni(T), the junction against temperature), M11 §6–§7, M18 §6–§7 (bumps, 3D integration) Read time ~55 min Sources Lecture 24 deck (Space Photodetectors); Lecture 24 (18 Nov); Lecture 23 deck (JWST) pp. 23–28; JWST User Documentation; Rieke et al. 2015; Hansen et al. 1982; Natarajan et al. 2012; Goiffon 2021 short course

A photodetector converts optical power into an electrical signal, and in a space instrument it is the element that decides what the instrument can see. Most detectors in this module rest on one rule from M10 §2: a photon is absorbed in a semiconductor only if its energy exceeds the bandgap, and the absorption lifts an electron into the conduction band, leaving a hole. §6 adds the extrinsic route, in which a photon lifts an electron from a dopant level just below the conduction band, and §8 a superconducting detector that uses neither. The rest is engineering of the absorption event. A reverse-biased junction separates the pair and delivers it as current (§1–§2); the ratio of current to optical power is the responsivity (§3); the smallest optical power the detector can distinguish is set by its noise, of which the dark current is the professor's measure (§4). A gap small enough to absorb infrared photons is also small enough for heat to excite carriers across it, so infrared detectors are operated tens of kelvin or a few kelvin above absolute zero (§5). The materials that reach the infrared — narrow-gap compounds, mercury cadmium telluride with its composition-tuned gap, and arsenic-doped silicon — are §6, and the hybrid arrays that turn millions of these junctions into a camera are §7. The James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) carries both of these technologies: HgCdTe arrays at about 37 K in its near-infrared instruments and Si:As arrays below 6.7 K in its mid-infrared instrument. §8 covers the superconducting nanowire detector that may succeed them, and §9 what radiation does to each. The telescope that carries these detectors — its mirror, sunshield, orbit and operations — is M21a.

1 · Absorption and the cutoff wavelength

Which wavelengths a detector can see is fixed before any circuit is attached: it is set by the bandgap of the absorbing material. This section states that rule, turns it into the cutoff wavelength, and applies it to the materials the lecture compared.

A photon of wavelength λ carries energy Eph = hc/λ (M10 §1). In the band regime — photon energies of a few electronvolts and below, where the wavelength is far larger than the atomic spacing — the photon interacts with the solid's band structure as a whole. It can be absorbed only by promoting an electron from a filled state to an empty one. In an intrinsic semiconductor the nearest empty states lie across the gap, so absorption requires Eph ≥ Eg. A photon with less energy than the gap finds no allowed transition and passes through: below its gap a semiconductor is transparent. A photon with more energy than the gap is absorbed and generates one electron–hole pair; the excess energy above Eg passes to lattice vibrations as the electron and hole relax to the band edges.

2 · The photodiode and its current–voltage characteristic

An absorbed photon creates a pair, but a pair that recombines where it was made produces no signal. The photodiode is a p–n junction arranged so that the pair is separated and collected before it recombines. This section describes the structure, then reads its current–voltage curve, which shows in one figure the photodetector, the solar cell and the avalanche photodiode as three operating regions of the same junction.

Assumes: the p–n junction in reverse bias (M01 §6)

Where p-type and n-type silicon meet, electrons diffuse into the p side and holes into the n side, leaving the ionized dopants uncovered in a depletion region that carries an electric field from the n side to the p side. Reverse bias (n side positive) widens the depletion region and strengthens the field. A small reverse current still flows. One part is the diffusion saturation current I0 of the diode law, carried by minority carriers that reach the depletion region by diffusion; the other is a generation current from pairs thermally generated inside the depletion region, which in silicon near room temperature is usually the larger (M01 §6). Together they are the photodiode's dark current, and both grow steeply with temperature (M17a §5).

The structure the lecture drew is the p–i–n photodiode: a thin p⁺ layer, a wide undoped (intrinsic) layer, and an n⁺ layer, with electrodes on top and bottom and a window on top carrying an anti-reflection coating so that the light enters the semiconductor instead of reflecting from its surface. Under reverse bias the intrinsic layer is fully depleted, so the junction field extends across the whole of it. A photon absorbed there generates a pair inside the field, and the field drives the electron toward the n⁺ side and the hole toward the p⁺ side (Figure 2). The pair is separated by drift, which is fast, rather than by diffusion, which is slow; pairs generated in the thin doped layers within a diffusion length of the depletion edge can still reach it by diffusion and add to the current. The wide intrinsic layer is there to make the absorbing volume thick: the thicker the depleted layer, the larger the fraction of incident photons absorbed inside the field.

The separated carriers flow out through the external circuit as the photocurrent Iph. Its direction is the direction of the reverse current — electrons leave through the n contact — so the photocurrent adds to the reverse saturation current rather than opposing it. Even with no light at all, the reverse saturation current flows; in a photodetector it is called the dark current, because it is the current measured in the dark. The professor made both points the ones to remember: a photodiode is operated in reverse bias, and the photon energy must exceed the bandgap.

The current–voltage curve (Figure 3) collects this. In the dark the junction follows the diode law: a small, nearly constant reverse current at negative voltage and an exponential rise in forward bias. Illumination adds the photocurrent, which is to first order independent of the bias across the junction, so light shifts the whole curve downward by Iph, and more light shifts it further.

The sign of the product VI identifies three operating regions of the same device.

  • Photodetector, V < 0 and I < 0. Reverse bias. The current is −(I0 + Iph), linear in the light, and the product VI is positive: the external supply does work on the device. The background against which the photocurrent is read is I0, which is small, so a small photocurrent is a large fractional change.
  • Solar cell, V > 0 and I < 0. The fourth quadrant. Here VI < 0: the device delivers power to the load, and the light is the source of that power. This is the region the solar array of M20 §3 operates in, at the maximum power point between the short-circuit current and the open-circuit voltage.
  • Avalanche photodiode, near breakdown. At a reverse bias close to breakdown, carriers crossing the high-field region gain enough energy to create further pairs by impact ionization (the multiplication of M01 §6), so each photogenerated pair yields many. The avalanche photodiode gains signal this way, but the multiplication process is random and multiplies the dark current too, so its noise is higher. The professor's summary was "pros and cons": gain, at the cost of noise and of operating a junction at the edge of breakdown.

Forward bias with large positive current, the remaining region, is useless for detection: the forward current is exponentially large, so a photocurrent of picoamperes is a negligible change on top of it.

Why is a photodiode operated in reverse bias rather than at zero bias or in forward bias? Give one reason for each alternative.

Against forward bias: the forward current grows as exp(qV/kT), so at a few hundred millivolts it is many orders of magnitude larger than I0. The photocurrent adds the same absolute amount in any region (21.2), so against a large forward current it is a vanishing fraction and cannot be resolved. In reverse bias the background is only I0.

Against zero bias: at V = 0 the device still collects photocurrent, and it carries no bias-driven current at all, but the depletion region is at its built-in width only. Reverse bias widens it, which in a p–i–n device depletes the whole intrinsic layer, so more of the absorbed photons are generated inside the field and are collected by drift rather than lost to recombination. The stronger field also sweeps carriers out faster, which shortens the response.

3 · Responsivity and quantum efficiency

A detector is characterized first by how much output it gives for a given input. For a photodiode the input is optical power and the output is photocurrent, and their ratio is the responsivity. This section defines it, works the lecture's example, and connects it to the quantum efficiency, which says what fraction of the photons produce a collected electron.

The responsivity ℜ is the output signal produced per unit of input (Lecture 24 deck p. 14). For a photodiode it is the photocurrent per watt of incident optical power, in A/W. A photodiode's response is linear over a wide range of power, so ℜ is a constant at a given wavelength and the photocurrent is simply ℜ times the power. The reason ℜ depends on wavelength follows from counting photons. Light of wavelength λ carries λ/(hc) photons per joule, so one watt delivers λ/(hc) photons per second; at longer wavelength the same power carries more photons, each of lower energy. If each absorbed photon yields one collected electron, the current per watt rises in proportion to λ — up to the cutoff, beyond which no photon is absorbed and ℜ falls to zero. The fraction of incident photons that produce a collected electron is the quantum efficiency η; it is below 1 because of reflection at the surface, photons that pass through the absorbing layer unabsorbed, and pairs that recombine before collection.

A detector has ℜ = 0.85 A/W at 1550 nm and receives 20 µW. What is the photocurrent and the quantum efficiency? Could a silicon photodiode replace it?

A silicon photodiode cannot replace it: 1550 nm is beyond silicon's cutoff of about 1.1 µm (E1), so a 1550 nm photon has 0.800 eV, less than silicon's gap, and is not absorbed. The responsivity of silicon at 1550 nm is zero, however high its responsivity in the visible. Germanium (λc = 1.85 µm) absorbs it.

4 · Noise and the minimum detectable signal

Responsivity says how much signal a watt produces; it says nothing about the smallest power that can be seen. That limit is set by noise — the random fluctuation present in every electronic measurement — because a signal smaller than the noise cannot be told apart from it. This section gives the professor's measure of a photodiode's limit, the noise-equivalent power computed from the dark current, then the two fundamental noise sources and the datasheet calculation the lecture worked through a transimpedance amplifier.

The sensitivity, or resolution, of a sensor is the smallest input it can distinguish (deck p. 17). The criterion the course uses is a signal-to-noise ratio of one: a response equal to the noise is just discernible, and a response below the noise is buried in it (deck p. 20; Figure 5). The input that produces a signal equal to the noise is therefore the minimum detectable input.

For a photodiode in the dark, the professor took the dark current as the noise level. Dividing that current by the responsivity converts it to the optical power that would produce the same current: the noise-equivalent power (NEP). Light weaker than this produces a photocurrent smaller than the dark current and, on this criterion, cannot be distinguished from it.

Two definitions of NEP. The lecture's (21.4) treats the dark current as the noise. In the detector literature the NEP is the signal power that gives a signal-to-noise ratio of one in a 1 Hz output bandwidth, in W/√Hz: the steady part of the dark current can be measured with the shutter closed and subtracted, and what limits detection is its random fluctuation, (21.5)–(21.6). The two coincide only at the bandwidth found in E3. For anything graded in this course, use (21.4); quote a datasheet NEP in W/√Hz with (21.6).

Two noise mechanisms underlie any such fluctuation. Johnson–Nyquist (thermal) noise is the voltage fluctuation across any resistor at a temperature above absolute zero: the conduction electrons move randomly with thermal energy of order kBT, so the number crossing a given point fluctuates about its mean, and so does the voltage (deck p. 18). Its mean-square value per hertz of bandwidth is 4kBTR. Shot noise is the fluctuation of a current made of discrete charges arriving at random: the arrivals are a Poisson process, so a current I fluctuates with a mean-square value 2qI per hertz. A dark current therefore carries shot noise in proportion to the square root of its size, and so does a photocurrent.

5 · Temperature, dark current, and why infrared detectors are cold

§4 made the dark current the floor of detection. This section shows why that floor rises steeply with temperature, why the rise is worst for exactly the narrow-gap materials that infrared detection needs, and what it costs to cool a detector in space. It is the temperature argument of M17a applied at the cold end.

The dark current of a junction is carried by thermally generated carriers. In M17a §5 the two parts of the reverse current were the diffusion current, proportional to ni², and the generation current from the depletion region, proportional to ni; and ni grows as exp(−Eg/2kT). What controls both is the ratio of the gap to the thermal energy kT. The deck tabulates kT: 8.6 meV at 100 K, 17 meV at 200 K, 26 meV at 300 K, 43 meV at 500 K (deck p. 22). For silicon at 300 K, Eg/kT = 1.12/0.02585 = 43, and a silicon junction works well; the deck notes that by 500 K it no longer does. For a material with a 0.1 eV gap — a 12.4 µm cutoff, which the deck rounds to 10 µm — the same ratio is only 11.6 at 100 K, a lower ratio than silicon has at room temperature, and 23 at 50 K. The professor's conclusion on the slide was that for a 0.1 eV material "T≈50K is already too high". The narrower the gap, the colder the detector must be to keep Eg/kT large, so the operating temperature of an infrared detector falls as its cutoff wavelength rises.

Cooling has a second purpose besides suppressing dark current. Everything warm emits infrared radiation, so a detector, its optics and its enclosure glow in the band the detector is trying to measure. The design document for MIRI's cooler states that the instrument is cooled below what passive cooling can reach "to optimise the detector performance and reduce the thermal background below the detector dark current" (Wright et al. 2015).   • Passive (radiative) cooling: the detector is shaded and radiates its heat to cold space. A detector material has a gap of 0.25 eV. At what temperature does it have the same Eg/kT as silicon at 300 K, and why is that only a rough guide to its operating temperature?

6 · Infrared detector materials

§1 showed that no common binary semiconductor reaches far into the infrared. This section covers the two routes JWST uses: an alloy whose gap can be set by its composition, mercury cadmium telluride, and a silicon detector that does not use the silicon gap at all, arsenic-doped silicon. Each is explained from its band structure, then by what JWST asks of it.

Mercury cadmium telluride: a gap set by composition

Mercury telluride and cadmium telluride have nearly the same lattice constant — on the bandgap–lattice-constant chart of deck p. 10 the two points sit almost on one vertical line — and they mix in any proportion to form the alloy Hg1−xCdxTe, written HgCdTe or MCT. Their gaps are at the two extremes. CdTe is a semiconductor with a gap of about 1.5 eV at room temperature. HgTe is a semimetal: its band structure is inverted, so the band edge that is the conduction band in CdTe lies below the valence band edge (deck p. 10). The literature therefore quotes its gap as negative, and the professor's engineering summary was to treat it as zero. Mixing the two gives any gap between 0 and about 1.5 eV, so the cutoff wavelength can be chosen by choosing x. Hansen, Schmit and Casselman fitted the gap to 22 data sets over the full composition range and from 4.2 to 300 K (Figure 8):

Arsenic-doped silicon: absorption without crossing the gap

Silicon's 1.12 eV gap is useless beyond about 1.1 µm, but a dopant offers a much smaller transition. An arsenic atom substituted on a silicon site is a donor (M01 §3): its fifth electron is bound in a level just below the conduction band. A photon with energy above that binding energy can lift the electron from the donor level into the conduction band, where it conducts (Figure 9). This is extrinsic absorption, from an impurity level to a band, as opposed to the intrinsic band-to-band absorption of §1. The deck gives the arsenic level as 0.049 eV below the conduction band edge, which by (21.1) is a cutoff of 1239.8/0.049 nm = 25.3 µm (deck p. 13).

A lightly doped layer has few donors and therefore absorbs weakly, so a practical detector would need to be very thick. Raising the doping raises the absorption, but at high donor density the donor wavefunctions overlap, the discrete levels broaden into an impurity band (the hyperdoped panel of deck p. 13), and electrons can hop from donor to donor through it. That hopping is a conduction path in the dark, and in a simple photoconductor it would be an unacceptable dark current. The impurity band conduction (IBC) detector, also called the blocked impurity band (BIB) detector, solves this with a layered structure (Rieke et al. 2015):

  • an infrared-active layer 25–35 µm thick, heavily doped with arsenic (7×10¹⁷ cm⁻³ in MIRI's baseline arrays) so that it absorbs strongly and carries an impurity band;
  • an undoped intrinsic blocking layer about 3–4 µm thick between it and the pixel contact. The impurity band ends at this layer, so dark charge cannot hop through; it can leak only by thermal excitation up into the conduction band. Photoexcited electrons, which are already in the conduction band, pass through;
  • a pixel contact on the blocking layer, indium-bump bonded to the input of the readout amplifier (§7).

Because the blocking layer stops the hopping current, the active layer can be doped two orders of magnitude more heavily than a bulk photoconductor (Rieke et al. 2015), which is what makes a layer only tens of micrometres thick absorb well. The broadening of the levels at that doping also lowers the energy needed to free an electron, which is consistent with MIRI's arrays responding out to 28 µm (JWST documentation: 5–28.5 µm), where the photon energy is 0.044 eV, below the slide's 0.049 eV. The price of the small energy is a very low operating temperature: the only barrier against dark current is thermal excitation over a few tens of millielectronvolts, and at 6.7 K, where kT = 0.58 meV, the MIRI arrays reach a dark current of about 0.2 electrons per second per pixel.

Why does MIRI not use HgCdTe tuned to 28 µm, and why do the NIRCam arrays not use Si:As?

A 28 µm HgCdTe photodiode needs a gap of 1239.8/28,000 = 0.044 eV. Its intrinsic carrier density, ∝ exp(−Eg/2kT), and with it the dark current (the diffusion part ∝ ni² ∝ exp(−Eg/kT)), would be too high; the JWST documentation gives exactly this reason. It would also need the composition held extremely tightly, because near so small a gap a composition error of 0.01 shifts the cutoff by many micrometres (E6 shows 0.4 µm at 5 µm, and the shift grows as λc² for a fixed gap error). Si:As reaches 28 µm with a blocking layer that suppresses the hopping dark current. NIRCam does not use Si:As because Si:As needs 6.7 K or less, which only the cryocooler provides, while HgCdTe tuned to 2.5 and 5 µm works at about 37 K, which JWST reaches passively: the near-infrared instruments need no cooler at all.

7 · From pixel to image: arrays, readout and the detection chain

A single photodiode measures one number. A camera needs millions of them read out in order, with their tiny currents amplified and digitized, at the detector's temperature. This section follows the signal from the pixel to the digital output, explains the hybrid construction that JWST's arrays share, and compares the two readout architectures, CCD and CMOS.

Every detector, the deck stresses, needs a readout integrated circuit (ROIC): the photosensitive element generates the charge, the focal plane array arranges the elements in a grid, and the readout chain sequences them, converts the signal to digital and sends it on (deck p. 26). For a silicon visible-light sensor the photodiodes and the readout can be made in the same silicon. For an infrared array a CMOS readout chip is generally required, and it is hybridized to the infrared-sensitive layer (Goiffon 2021, §1.2.5): the two are made separately and joined in a hybrid sensor chip assembly (Figure 10). Teledyne names the three technologies this takes as the growth and processing of the HgCdTe layer, the design of the CMOS readout, and the hybridization of one to the other (Beletic 2008). The detector layer, with one p–n junction per pixel, is connected pixel by pixel to a silicon readout chip through an array of indium bumps, a soft, cold-compatible form of the flip-chip bumps of M18 §6, with epoxy filling the gap. Teledyne's cross-section on deck p. 28 shows the arrangement for HgCdTe: light enters a bulk n-type HgCdTe layer through an anti-reflection coating, implanted p-type regions collect the holes, and each implant connects through its indium bump to the MOSFET input of its own pixel circuit in the silicon multiplexer. The substrate on which the HgCdTe was grown is removed so the light can enter from the back. MIRI's Si:As arrays are joined to their readout by indium bumps in the same way, but keep their substrate: it is transparent, high-resistivity silicon about 500 µm thick, and light enters through it and a buried transparent contact before reaching the infrared-active layer (Rieke et al. 2015).

The deck's six-step chain (deck p. 28) names the job of each part: (1) the anti-reflection coating and substrate removal admit the light; (2) the detector material generates charge; (3) the electric field of the junction collects it — steps 1–2 set the quantum efficiency of §3, and step 3 sets how sharply a point source is imaged, because charge that diffuses sideways before collection lands in a neighbouring pixel; (4) a source follower in each pixel converts the collected charge to a voltage; (5) the voltages are transferred out, by random access to chosen pixels or by reading the full frame; (6) a separate chip digitizes them. On JWST that chip is the SIDECAR ASIC (System for Image Digitization, Enhancement, Control And Retrieval), one per detector in NIRCam (JWST documentation). It sits in the cold next to the array, operating at about 37 K, and was designed to provide 16-bit conversion within a power budget of less than 10 mW per detector, because every milliwatt dissipated in the cold has to be radiated away by the passive cooling (Loose et al. 2005).

The professor drew a wider lesson from this stack. Space electronics is usually generations behind commercial technology (M03), yet the most capable space detectors are heterogeneously integrated systems — a compound-semiconductor detector, indium bumps, a silicon readout chip and a cryogenic mixed-signal ASIC — of the kind M18 §7 described as the frontier of integration. JWST's near-infrared spectrograph NIRSpec adds a MEMS device in front of its detectors, in the spectrograph's aperture plane: a micro-shutter array (deck p. 25; M14b §1) of four quadrants of 365 × 171 individually addressable shutters, 249,660 in all, silicon nitride membranes on a pitch of about 100 × 200 µm, opened by a moving magnet and latched electrostatically (Li et al., NASA Goddard; JWST documentation). Light passes the filter wheel, then the open shutters, then the disperser and the camera optics, and only then reaches NIRSpec's two H2RG arrays, so opening chosen shutters admits the light of chosen objects to the spectrograph and blocks the rest of the field.

Two readout architectures appear on deck p. 26 (Figure 11). In a charge-coupled device (CCD) the signal charge is held in potential wells under clocked gates and moved packet by packet along the columns and through a serial register to a single output amplifier; the charge transfer efficiency per transfer, compounded over thousands of transfers, decides how much of the packet arrives (EEE 5416 M12b §4). In a CMOS image sensor, an active-pixel design, each pixel has its own amplifier and is addressed by row and column switches, with the analog readout and often one analog-to-digital converter per column on the same chip (Goiffon 2021). The hybrid infrared arrays of JWST are of the CMOS kind: the readout chip is a CMOS multiplexer with a source follower per pixel, which is what allows random access. The CMOS architecture moves no charge from pixel to pixel, so the transfer losses that limit a damaged CCD (§9) do not arise in it.

8 · Superconducting nanowire single-photon detectors

The detectors so far turn a photon into an electron–hole pair. The professor closed with a detector that uses a different physical effect: the photon breaks superconductivity in a short length of wire, and the detector reads the resulting voltage pulse. This section describes the superconducting state the device needs, the detection cycle, the recovery time, and why the technology is not yet ready for a space instrument.

A superconductor is a material whose resistance falls abruptly to zero below a critical temperature Tc (deck p. 32; Figure 13, left), whereas an ordinary metal's resistance falls smoothly toward a finite residual value. Below Tc the conduction electrons form bound pairs (Cooper pairs) that carry current without dissipation, up to a critical current density above which superconductivity breaks down. Superconductivity survives only below both the critical temperature and the critical current. Common nanowire materials have critical temperatures of about 16 K for NbN and NbTiN, 7.5 K for MoSi and 5 K for WSi, the last two amorphous films (Holzman and Ivry 2019); in the few-nanometre films of a detector the superconducting properties are suppressed further.

A superconducting nanowire single-photon detector (SNSPD) is a film about 5–10 nm thick patterned into a wire about 100 nm wide, folded into a meander that covers the detector area (Nicolich et al. 2019). It has no p–n junction (deck p. 31). It is held well below Tc and driven with a steady bias current just below its critical current (Natarajan, Tanner & Hadfield 2012). The detection cycle (Figure 12; deck p. 32):

  1. The wire is superconducting, the bias current Ib flows with no resistance, and the voltage across it is zero.
  2. An absorbed photon breaks hundreds of Cooper pairs and forms a small resistive hotspot, narrower than the wire.
  3. The supercurrent is forced around the hotspot, so the current density in the remaining strips exceeds the critical value; superconductivity breaks across the full width, and the resistive section diverts the bias current into the load resistor RL. A voltage pulse appears across the load.
  4. With the current diverted, the resistive section stops heating, cools, and returns to the superconducting state; the current flows back into the wire, and the voltage returns to zero.

The return of the current is slowed by the kinetic inductance Lk of the nanowire, an inductance that comes from the inertia of the Cooper pairs rather than from a magnetic field, and which grows with the wire's length. In the lumped model of deck p. 32 the wire is Lk in series with a switch and resistor that represent the hotspot, in parallel with the load. Once the hotspot has closed, the current re-enters Lk through RL, an LR circuit:

These properties are what make the technology attractive: single-photon sensitivity, very low dark counts, timing jitter of tens of picoseconds and recovery in nanoseconds. Reported best values include 93 % system detection efficiency, 10⁻⁴ dark counts per second and 4.6 ps jitter at 1550 nm, though no single device has shown all of them at once (Holzman and Ivry 2019). The professor noted the speed as the reason optical communication is interested in the same device. The challenges for space are the ones the deck lists (p. 33):

  • Radiation: few radiation tests exist and there is no established rad-hard design. Testing is itself hard, because the device must be held at cryogenic temperature inside the beam.
  • Temperature stability: the detector's operating point depends on the bias current's distance below the critical current, which itself depends on temperature, so the base temperature must be held steady while the spacecraft's thermal environment cycles.
  • Cooling power: the operating temperatures on deck p. 33 — NbN 1–4 K, MoSi 2.5 K, NbTiN below 8.4 K — are lower than MIRI's 6.7 K for most materials, and need stronger active cooling, hence more power and vibration.

Source note — how the nanowire is operated. Deck p. 31 says the device must be "operated at a critical temperature of the superconductivity (~10K)", and Lecture 24 described holding the wire just below Tc, so that a small heating crosses the transition. The deck's own p. 32 figure shows a current-biased wire, and in practice the wire is held well below Tc with the bias current just below the critical current; the photon pushes the local current density over its critical value (Natarajan et al. 2012). Both pictures share the essential point: the wire is poised at the edge of the superconducting state, so a single photon tips it over.

Why does an SNSPD need no p–n junction, and what limits how often it can detect photons?

An SNSPD does not separate electron–hole pairs: its signal is the change of a superconducting wire from zero resistance to a finite resistance, which diverts the bias current into the load and produces a voltage. No built-in field is needed to collect anything, so no junction is needed. After each detection the wire must cool back into the superconducting state and the current must return through the kinetic inductance, with time constant Lk/RL (21.10). A photon arriving before the current has recovered meets a wire biased further below its critical current and is less likely to be detected, so the recovery time sets the maximum count rate. With 2 µH and 50 Ω, τr = 40 ns, four times E8's; a longer meander, which covers a larger area, has a larger Lk and is slower.

9 · Radiation and photodetectors

The professor's closing point was that every detector in this module must be tested under radiation. A detector is built to turn small amounts of deposited energy into signal, and radiation deposits energy too. This section sorts what radiation does into the three mechanisms of the course — transient ionization, total ionizing dose and displacement damage — and applies each to the detectors of §6–§8, with the radiation-effects literature on imagers as the reference.

Most infrared detectors, the HgCdTe photodiodes among them, are reverse-biased p–n junctions (MIRI's Si:As IBC arrays are an exception), like the photodiodes of visible-light CMOS sensors, and they are turned into imaging devices by a hybridized CMOS readout chip; Goiffon's short course on image-sensor hardening notes that the radiation literature on infrared detectors is less developed than on CMOS sensors, but that these similarities allow its recommendations to be transposed in part (Goiffon 2021, §1.2.5). The same short course names the dark current increase as the main radiation-induced degradation to be mitigated in image sensors. The three mechanisms reach the dark current, and the image, by different routes.

Transient ionization: cosmic-ray hits

A charged particle crossing a pixel ionizes along its track (M08), and the pairs it frees are collected exactly like photogenerated pairs. The pixel records a signal that no photon made. In a JWST exposure the readout samples each pixel repeatedly while the charge accumulates (a "ramp"), so a hit appears as a near-instant jump in the ramp; the processing pipeline's jump step detects and flags it, and the rest of the ramp is still usable. Most hits affect one or two pixels. Large events, called snowballs in the near-infrared arrays and showers in the mid-infrared arrays, affect hundreds; the JWST documentation estimates roughly one snowball every 20 s in each near-infrared array, and about half that rate of showers in MIRI. For planning, the exposure time calculator assumes 8 events per second per square centimetre, each affecting 9 pixels.

Plan. Energy deposited = stopping power × density × path length; pairs = energy / 3.6 eV. The hit rate per pixel is the flux times the pixel area. The trap is the treatment of a doped layer as silicon: at 7×10¹⁷ cm⁻³ the arsenic is a small fraction of the atoms, so silicon's stopping power applies; the pair energy at 6.7 K is taken as the course's room-temperature value, an approximation.

Pairs. 13,560 eV / 3.6 eV = 3770 pairs, which is 3770/14 = 269 times the read noise. This is an upper estimate: part of the mean energy loss is carried out of a thin layer by energetic secondary (delta) electrons, so the typical deposit, and the most probable one even more, is smaller. A hit stands far above the read noise, which is why it can be flagged, but at this rate it cannot be ignored either.

Total ionizing dose: the readout chip and the surfaces

The readout chip and the SIDECAR ASIC are CMOS integrated circuits, and for those circuits the effects of total ionizing dose and their hardening are the same as for any CMOS IC (Goiffon 2021): trapped oxide charge and interface traps shift thresholds and open leakage paths (M14). In a silicon photodiode the dose adds interface traps at the oxide around the diode's perimeter, and the generation current from those traps raises the dark current in proportion to their density (Goiffon 2021). The SIDECAR ASIC was tested with 63 MeV protons while operating at 37 K, and accumulated 80 krad (material not stated in the source) with no noticeable degradation of performance (Loose et al. 2005).

Displacement damage: hot pixels, dark current and charge transfer

Protons and neutrons that displace atoms leave defects with levels in the gap (M11 §6). A defect inside a reverse-biased depletion volume is an efficient Shockley–Read–Hall generation centre, so it adds dark current; a single strong one can make a pixel saturate in the dark, a hot pixel (Goiffon 2021, §3.1.2). Some defects switch between two generation rates, so the pixel's dark current jumps between discrete levels at random, a random telegraph signal; it has been observed in irradiated CCDs, CMOS sensors and HgCdTe, InGaAs and InSb infrared detectors (Goiffon 2021, §1.3.4.3). In a CCD the same defects in the transfer channel capture charge from passing packets and release it later, which lowers the charge transfer efficiency and smears the image (EEE 5416 M12b §4). A 5 µm × 5 µm silicon pixel with a 3 µm depleted depth receives 3×10⁸ MeV/g. What mean dark current does displacement damage add at 300 K, and how much dark charge does it collect in a 10 s exposure? Which radiation effect would show up first in a CCD with the same damage?

In a CCD the same defects also sit in the transfer channel, and each pixel's charge passes through many transfers on its way to the output, so the loss of charge transfer efficiency — smearing and trailing behind bright features — is the effect most directly tied to displacement damage (EEE 5416 M12b §4). A CMOS or hybrid array shows the dark current and hot pixels but no transfer loss.

The superconducting detector of §8 is the open case. The deck's summary is that radiation testing is limited and no rad-hard design yet exists; Lecture 24 added that the professor's group, which tests electronics under radiation, is interested in it for that reason. The class discussion raised the two obvious concerns, that a particle depositing energy in the wire heats it as a photon does and could disturb its operation; how large such effects are in practice is what the testing has yet to establish.

For each of the three radiation mechanisms, name the part of a hybrid HgCdTe sensor chip assembly it acts on and the symptom in the image.

Transient ionization acts on the detector layer, and in principle on the readout: a particle frees charge in a pixel's absorber, which the pixel collects as signal. Symptom: a jump in that pixel's ramp, one or two pixels usually, hundreds for a snowball; flagged and removed by the processing.

Total ionizing dose acts mainly on the silicon readout chip and the cryogenic ASIC, which are CMOS circuits: threshold shifts and leakage in their transistors, and extra dark current where an oxide borders a junction. Symptom: drifts in bias and offset, rising dark level, eventually circuit failure.

Displacement damage acts on the detector material's crystal: defects in the depletion volumes become generation centres. Symptom: rising mean dark current, hot pixels, and pixels with random telegraph switching. The array, being CMOS-type, has no charge-transfer loss.

Summary

  • A photon is absorbed only if hc/λ ≥ Eg; the cutoff is λc(nm) = 1239.8/Eg(eV) (21.1). Si 1.09 µm, Ge 1.85, InAs 3.76, InSb 7.75 µm.
  • A p–i–n photodiode in reverse bias separates the pair in the depletion field; light shifts the I–V curve down by Iph (21.2). Reverse bias: detector. Fourth quadrant, VI < 0: solar cell. Near breakdown: avalanche photodiode, gain with more noise.
  • ℜ = Iph/P = ηqλ/(hc) (21.3); 0.45 A/W at 700 nm is η = 0.80.
  • The course's NEP is Idark/ℜ, in W (21.4); the standard NEP is the noise current density over ℜ, in W/√Hz, with Pmin = NEP√Δf (21.6). Johnson noise 4kTR and shot noise 2qI per hertz (21.5). A datasheet NEP is found by dividing the output noise by the transimpedance gain, then by ℜ.
  • Dark current is thermally activated (21.7); the narrower the gap, the colder the detector: about 150 K short-wave, 77 K mid-wave, 40 K and below long-wave. JWST: HgCdTe at about 37 K passively, Si:As at 6.7 K or less with a helium cryocooler that costs power and must not vibrate.
  • HgCdTe's gap is set by composition (21.8): x = 0.46 for a 2.5 µm cutoff, 0.30 for 5.3 µm at 37 K. Si:As IBC detectors absorb from an impurity band into the conduction band, with a blocking layer that stops the hopping dark current, out to 28 µm.
  • Infrared arrays are hybrids: detector layer, indium bumps, silicon CMOS readout, cryogenic ASIC. CMOS-type readout addresses each pixel; a CCD moves charge and so suffers transfer loss.
  • An SNSPD is a current-biased superconducting nanowire: a photon creates a hotspot, the wire goes resistive, the current pulses into the load and recovers with τr = Lk/RL (21.10). Single-photon sensitivity and nanosecond recovery, but colder operation, temperature sensitivity and little radiation data.
  • Radiation: cosmic-ray hits are flagged as jumps in the ramp; dose acts on the CMOS readout; displacement damage raises dark current and makes hot and telegraph pixels (21.11). Every detector must be tested under radiation.
