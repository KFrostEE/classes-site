Module 20

Space Power: Generation and Storage

How a spacecraft makes, stores and delivers electrical power: what the electrical power system must do, why the bus runs at 28 V or higher, how a solar cell converts sunlight and why heat and radiation take part of it away, how an array is sized for an orbit and a distance from the Sun, how a battery carries the spacecraft through eclipse and is recharged, and where radioisotope generators take over from sunlight.

Prerequisites M01 (the p–n junction), M03 §2–§3, M07 §2, M11 §4–§7, M17a §2, §4–§5, M19 §7 Read time ~60 min Sources Lecture 22 deck (CubeSat Power Systems) and Lecture 22; Deck 16b pp. 28–30, 36; NASA and JPL mission data; Spectrolab XTJ Prime data sheet

Every function a spacecraft performs is paid for in electrical energy, and in space there is no supply to fall back on. M03 §3 stated the constraint in one line: each watt must be generated, and then radiated away as heat. This module opens that constraint into the system that meets it. The electrical power system (EPS) generates power, almost always from sunlight; stores it in a battery for the parts of the orbit with no sunlight; distributes it on a bus; and regulates and protects it. Each stage rests on physics already taught in this course. The solar cell is the p–n junction of M01 operated as a current source. Its loss of output in orbit is the displacement damage of M11. Its loss at high temperature is the intrinsic-carrier physics of M17a. The parts that switch and regulate the power are derated by the rules of M19 §7, and a high-voltage bus meets the single-event burnout of power transistors. The module ends with the radioisotope generator, the source used where sunlight is too weak for an array of practical size.

1 · The electrical power system and what it must do

Before any component is chosen, the power system is specified by what the rest of the spacecraft needs from it: how much power, at which voltages, at which times, and with what protection. This section defines the vocabulary the Lecture 22 deck uses, lists the requirements, and shows why a power system is sized by two numbers, the average and the peak, rather than one.

Power, energy and charge. Power P is the rate of energy transfer, in watts; for a direct-current load it is the product of voltage and current. Energy E is power integrated over time. Spacecraft budgets express it in watt-hours (1 Wh = 3,600 J), because loads are specified in watts and orbits in minutes. A battery's capacity is stated as charge in ampere-hours (Ah), the current it can deliver multiplied by the time. Charge and energy are related by the voltage at which the charge is delivered:

P = VI  E = P · t  Q = EV
(20.1)
P power (W) · V voltage (V) · I current (A) · E energy (Wh when t is in hours) · t duration (h) · Q charge (Ah). E = Pt holds for a constant load; a varying load is integrated, E = ∫P dt. Q = E/V uses the battery's voltage while it delivers the charge. Formula sheet

The four functions. The deck divides the EPS into four blocks (Figure 1). The power source generates; energy storage stores; distribution transmits power from source and storage to the loads; regulation and control holds the bus voltage within limits and protects the system against faults. Lecture 22 added a fifth function that the slide leaves out, monitoring: the EPS measures its own voltages, currents and temperatures and reports them as health and status telemetry, so that a failed regulator is detected and its loads can be switched to a redundant path.

The vocabulary of a power budget. The power equipment list (PEL) is the table of every load with its voltage, current and duty, from which the average power is computed. The power profile is the load against time over an orbit or a mission phase, from which the peak power is read. Power margin is the difference between the power available and the power required, kept positive at every point of the profile. Power efficiency η is the fraction of input power that a converter or a line delivers to its output; the rest becomes heat. Power protection is the set of devices that disconnect a fault: a fuse opens once, a circuit breaker can be reset. Power quality describes how closely the bus voltage holds its nominal value: ripple and noise from switching converters, dips and swells when loads change, and transients.

The requirements. The deck lists what a typical EPS must provide:

  • continuous power to every subsystem for the whole mission, including night and eclipse;
  • safe distribution and control of all the power generated;
  • enough power, with margin, for both the average and the peak loads;
  • downstream converters for loads that need other voltages;
  • isolation between upstream and downstream loads, so a fault in one load does not pull down the bus;
  • health and status: voltage, current and temperature;
  • protection of itself and of other subsystems against electromagnetic interference, transients, bus faults and load faults, by filtering, overvoltage protection and short-circuit protection.

Four further requirements are derived rather than stated: the average power from the PEL, the peak power from the profile, the mission requirements, and the orbital or site parameters that set how much sunlight reaches the array and for how long. The EPS also exchanges heat and mechanical load with the thermal and structural subsystems, which is why the deck names thermal, structures, command and data handling, and payloads as its major interfaces.

Average and peak. A spacecraft's load is not constant. Instruments, transmitters and heaters switch on and off, so the profile has a peak well above its average. The two numbers size different parts. Energy drawn over an orbit must be replaced by the array over the same orbit, so the orbit-average power (OAP), the energy per orbit divided by the orbit period, sizes the array. The peak sets the current that the battery, the converters and the wiring must carry; Lecture 22's rule is to design each wire and converter for the peak current, not the typical one. A load above the array's supply is carried by the battery for as long as it lasts, and the battery is recharged when the load falls below the supply.

E1 · Average and peak from a power profile

Given the power profile on p. 13 of the Lecture 22 deck (Figure 2), sampled at one-minute intervals from 0 to 36 min, and the orbit-average power available from the Pathfinder Technology Demonstrator (PTD) bus, 45.1 W, drawn on the same chart. Find the energy the profile draws, its average power, its peak, and the energy a battery must supply while the draw exceeds 45.1 W.

Plan. Integrate the profile for energy with the trapezoidal rule, divide by the duration for the average, and integrate only the part above 45.1 W for the battery's share. The values were read off the slide by calibrating its axis gridlines, so each is good to about ±1.5 W.

Energy over the profile. The samples are about 20 W for minutes 0–4, 34–39 W for minutes 5–8, 27 and 24 W at 9 and 10, about 97 W from 11 to 18, 22 W from 19 to 23 and 7.6 W from 24 to 36. The trapezoidal sum over 36 one-minute intervals is 1,266 W·min, which is 1,266/60 = 21.1 Wh.

Average power. Pavg = 21.1 Wh / (36/60 h) = 35.2 W.

Peak. The highest sample is 97.2 W, 2.76 times the average.

Energy above 45.1 W. Between about minute 10.3 and minute 18.7 the draw exceeds 45.1 W by about 52 W. Integrating only the excess, including the two crossing segments, gives 6.65 Wh. Why it matters: a bus whose sources are sized to 45.1 W meets the average with 9.9 W to spare, but not the peak; the battery must deliver 6.65 Wh during the peak and recover it afterwards from the margin between 45.1 W and the lower loads.

21.1 Wh drawn; 35.2 W average; 97.2 W peak; 6.65 Wh from the battery above 45.1 W. Check: the average lies between the PTD line (45.1 W) and the LISA-T line (17.9 W), as Figure 2 shows, so the profile fits the first bus on average and not the second.

A profile repeats every 36 minutes and draws the same energy per period as the one in E1, but its 97 W peak lasts 12 minutes instead of about 8, with correspondingly lower power elsewhere. Which parts of the power system change size, and which do not?

The array does not change: it is sized by the energy per orbit, and an unchanged average means unchanged energy. The wiring and converters do not change either, because they are sized by the peak current, and the peak is still 97 W. The battery does change. The energy it supplies is the integral of the draw above the array's supply, so a peak half as long again removes about half as much charge again: 12 min × (97 − 45.1) W ≈ 10.4 Wh above 45.1 W, instead of 6.65 Wh. (A 16-minute peak would not fit: 97 W for 16 minutes is 25.9 Wh, more than the whole period's 21.1 Wh.) The battery must hold that charge within its allowed depth of discharge (§6), and the margin between the array's supply and the lower loads must recharge it before the next peak.

2 · The bus: voltage, distribution and architecture

The bus is the conductor that carries power from the sources to the loads. Its voltage is the first system-level choice on the deck's list, because it sets the current for a given power and therefore the loss in every wire and the voltage rating of every part. This section derives the loss, explains why higher voltages are now chosen and what limits them, and describes how the bus is regulated and protected.

Why the current matters. A harness of round-trip resistance R carrying current I dissipates I²R as heat. For a load of fixed power on a bus of voltage V, the current is P/V, so

Ploss = I²R = (PV)2R
(20.2)
Ploss power dissipated in the harness (W) · P power delivered to the load (W) · V bus voltage (V) · R round-trip resistance of the supply and return conductors (Ω). Valid when the drop IR is small compared with V. At fixed P and R the loss scales as 1/V².
E2 · Harness loss at 28 V and at 100 V

Given a 100 W load at the end of a harness whose round-trip resistance is 0.20 Ω. Find the current and the harness loss on a 28 V bus and on a 100 V bus, and the ratio of the losses.

Plan. Current from (20.1), loss from (20.2). The ratio depends only on the voltages, because P and R cancel.

Current at 28 V. I = 100 W / 28 V = 3.57 A.

Loss at 28 V. Ploss = (3.57 A)² × 0.20 Ω = 2.55 W.

Current and loss at 100 V. I = 100/100 = 1.00 A; Ploss = 1.00² × 0.20 = 0.200 W.

Ratio. 2.55/0.200 = (100/28)² = 12.8. Why the square: the current falls by the voltage ratio, 3.57, and the loss depends on the current squared.

3.57 A and 2.55 W at 28 V; 1.00 A and 0.200 W at 100 V; the loss falls by a factor of 12.8. Check: both drops, 0.71 V and 0.20 V, are small against their bus voltages, so (20.2) applies.

Lecture 22 used this comparison and put the saving at a factor of four. Four is the ratio of the currents, rounded; the heat is the square of the current ratio, about 13. The difference is the reason the industry is moving to higher-voltage buses.

28 V and higher. The 28 V DC main bus is the long-standing standard for spacecraft and aircraft, and the voltage Lecture 22 called the main DC bus. Small spacecraft often run lower: NASA's 2026 survey of small-spacecraft technology describes a traditional small-spacecraft EPS with a main battery bus of 8.2 V and regulated outputs at 5.0 V and 3.3 V, and CubeSats with body-mounted cells, small batteries and a single low-voltage bus. Lecture 22 described larger vehicles moving the other way, to buses near 100 V, for the reason E2 shows. Two limits keep the voltage from rising further. First, a power MOSFET is more susceptible to single-event burnout and single-event gate rupture at higher drain–source voltage, so a transistor in space is operated well below its rating: the safe operating area that radiation testing establishes is a fraction of the datasheet voltage (EEE 5416 M16 §4 for the mechanism and §7 for the safe operating area, and the derating factors of M19 §7). Second, higher voltage requires larger spacing between conductors to prevent arcing. Lecture 22 described 100 V as the point that balances lower current against these two costs.

Conversion at the point of load. Loads need 12 V, 5 V, 3.3 V and lower. The EPS either converts once and distributes each voltage, or distributes the main bus and converts at each load, which is point-of-load conversion. Point-of-load conversion keeps the distributed current low, by (20.2), and isolates the loads from each other: a short in a 3.3 V sensor collapses only its own converter, not the bus. The converter type matters when the input varies. A buck converter steps down and stops regulating once its input falls toward its output; a SEPIC (single-ended primary-inductor converter) regulates whether its input is above or below its output, which suits a bus that sags as a battery discharges. Each converter is chosen, by the deck's list, for its input voltage range and maximum output current, for the worst-case steady-state and transient conditions of its load and its input bus, for radiation and temperature, for its thermal path, and for protection, stability and input and output filtering. The thermal path is conduction to a cold plate: fans are not used on CubeSats, and in vacuum there is no air for them to move (M03 §2).

Regulating a bus fed by an array. A solar array behaves as a current source (§3): its current is set by the sunlight, and the voltage at its terminals is set by whatever it is connected to. The deck's three bus architectures all use direct energy transfer (DET): the array is connected to the bus through a power diode, with no converter in series, and the power the loads do not use is diverted by a shunt regulator (SR), a controlled path across the array that absorbs the surplus current so that the bus voltage stays within limits (Figure 3).

  • Unregulated bus, full shunt (deck p. 27): the shunt spans the whole array and the battery sits directly on the bus. The bus voltage follows the battery's voltage, which falls through eclipse and rises during charge, so downstream converters must accept that range.
  • Unregulated bus, partial shunt (deck p. 28): the array is split into two sections in series and the shunt is tapped across one section, so it handles only part of the array voltage.
  • Fully regulated bus (deck p. 29): a battery discharger and charger stands between the battery and the bus, so the bus is held at one voltage in sunlight and in eclipse, at the cost of a converter's loss and mass.

The alternative to direct energy transfer is a series converter between the array and the bus that sets the array's operating voltage, which allows the array to be held at its maximum power point (§3). The power diode in every architecture prevents the battery from discharging back into the array when the array's voltage falls in eclipse.

Protection. A fault in a load must be disconnected before it pulls down the bus. A fuse opens permanently; a circuit breaker can be reset; Lecture 22 also described resettable devices whose resistance rises sharply when a fault current heats them and recovers when the fault clears. A wire undersized for the fault current heats and fails as the fuse would, so wiring is sized for the protected peak current. The deck adds the hazards specific to stored energy: the battery's leads must not be shorted, and many power parts are sensitive to electrostatic discharge during handling.

A designer proposes raising a spacecraft's bus from 28 V to 120 V to cut harness loss, keeping the same 100 V MOSFETs in the converters. What does the change gain, and why must the transistors change?

At fixed load power the current falls by 120/28 = 4.29, so by (20.2) the harness loss falls by 4.29² = 18.4. The transistors must change because each must block at least the bus voltage plus its transients, so a 100 V part on a 120 V bus is above its rating, a stress ratio above 1 (M19 (19.9)); it is rejected outright. Even a part rated above 120 V must be derated, both by the derating factor for drain–source voltage (0.75 in EEE-INST-002, M19 §7) and by the safe operating area from heavy-ion testing, because burnout and gate rupture become more likely as the drain–source voltage rises. The higher bus also needs larger conductor spacing against arcing.

3 · The solar cell and the array

Sunlight is the source on almost every spacecraft, and the solar cell is the device that converts it. This section describes the cell as the p–n junction of M01 operated under illumination, derives the operating point at which it delivers most power, explains why a hot cell delivers less, and builds cells into an array.

From photon to current. A photon whose energy exceeds the semiconductor's band gap Eg can be absorbed and create an electron–hole pair. A pair created in the depletion region of a p–n junction is separated by the junction's built-in field and delivered to the terminals before it can recombine (M01, the depletion region as the collecting element of every photodiode and solar cell). A pair created in the neutral material beyond it is collected only if its minority carrier diffuses to the junction before recombining, which it does from within about a diffusion length L = (Dτ)1/2 of the junction, where D is the diffusion coefficient and τ the minority-carrier lifetime (EEE 5416 M12b §4). The collected pairs form a photocurrent IL proportional to the absorbed photon flux. The illuminated junction is therefore a current source in parallel with the junction's own diode. The diode conducts the forward current that the cell's own voltage drives, and that current subtracts from the photocurrent:

I = IL − I0 [exp (qVnkT) − 1]  Voc = nkTq ln (ILI0 + 1)
(20.3)
I current delivered to the load (A) · IL photocurrent, equal to the short-circuit current Isc (A) · I0 dark saturation current of the junction (A) · V cell voltage (V) · q electron charge (C) · k Boltzmann constant (J/K; kT/q = 0.02585 V at 300 K) · T cell temperature (K) · n ideality factor, 1 for an ideal diode · Voc open-circuit voltage, where I = 0 (V). The single-diode model; it omits series and shunt resistance.

The maximum power point. At short circuit the cell delivers Isc at zero voltage; at open circuit it holds Voc at zero current; both deliver zero power. Between them the product VI has a maximum, at the maximum power point (Vmp, Imp) near the knee of the curve (Figure 4). The fill factor measures how square the curve is, and the conversion efficiency compares the maximum power with the sunlight on the cell:

FF = VmpImpVocIsc  η = VmpImpS · A = FF · VocJscS
(20.4)
FF fill factor (dimensionless, below 1) · η conversion efficiency at the maximum power point (dimensionless) · S solar irradiance on the cell (W/m², or mW/cm² with J in mA/cm²) · A cell area · Jsc = Isc/A short-circuit current density. Efficiencies of space cells are quoted at the air-mass-zero (AM0) spectrum, sunlight above the atmosphere, at 28 °C.

Why a hot cell delivers less. Raising the temperature leaves IL almost unchanged but increases I0 steeply. The dark saturation current is set by thermal generation across the gap, so it scales with the square of the intrinsic carrier density, ni² ∝ T³ exp(−Eg/kT) (M17a §2, §5). By (20.3), Voc falls as ln I0 rises. For a single-diode silicon cell with Voc = 0.600 V at 300 K, the model gives 0.499 V at 350 K, −2.0 mV/K, and the maximum power falls by 21 % over those 50 K (Figure 4, computed in figs-m20.py). The short-circuit current rises slightly, because the band gap narrows and more of the spectrum is absorbed, but much less than the voltage falls.

Lecture 22 explained the temperature coefficient the other way round, as heat letting electrons "move freer" and cold reducing mobility. Near room temperature the mobility in silicon falls as temperature rises, because carriers scatter more often from lattice vibrations (M17a §4, (17a.3)). The loss at high temperature comes from the open-circuit voltage, and the deck's own sizing table enters temperature as a loss: a factor of 0.88 on the cell's rated output (§5).

Multijunction cells. A single junction loses as heat the part of each photon's energy that exceeds its band gap, and absorbs none of the photons below it. A multijunction cell stacks junctions of decreasing band gap, connected in series, so each absorbs the part of the spectrum its gap suits and passes the rest to the junction beneath. The current through the stack is that of the weakest subcell, so the subcells are designed to generate equal currents; the voltages add. The labelled one of the three cell types on deck p. 14 is Spectrolab's 29.5 % NeXt Triple Junction (XTJ), photographed with its interconnects, coverglass and bypass diode; the others are a crystalline-silicon wafer cell and a flexible thin-film sheet; its successor, XTJ Prime, is rated at 30.7 % at AM0 and 28 °C. The NREL chart of best research-cell efficiencies on deck p. 15 tops out at 47.6 %, a four-junction cell from Fraunhofer ISE measured at a concentration of 665 suns; that is a laboratory record under concentrated light, not the efficiency of a flight cell in unconcentrated sunlight.

E3 · A flight cell from its data sheet

Given the Spectrolab XTJ Prime data sheet (7/27/22) at AM0 (135.3 mW/cm²) and 28 °C: Voc = 2.720 V, Jsc = 18.0 mA/cm², Vmp = 2.406 V, Jmp = 17.5 mA/cm²; temperature coefficients from 15 to 125 °C, dVmp/dT = −6.3 mV/°C and dJmp/dT = +5.0 µA/cm²/°C. Plan. (20.4) at 28 °C. For temperature, move Vmp and Jmp linearly by their coefficients and multiply. Watch the units: the voltage coefficient is in mV, the current coefficient in µA/cm².

Maximum power density. VmpJmp = 2.406 V × 17.5 mA/cm² = 42.1 mW/cm².

Fill factor. FF = 42.1/(2.720 × 18.0) = 42.1/49.0 = 0.860.

Efficiency. η = 42.1/135.3 = 0.311. The sheet states 30.7 %. Every value in its table is a production average over more than 100,000 cells, and the product of the average Vmp and Jmp comes out 0.4 points above the stated efficiency. Rounding cannot explain the gap (it moves the product by under 0.1 point), and the sheet does not reconcile the two; an average of products need not equal the product of averages. Use the stated 30.7 %.

At 70 °C. ΔT = 42 °C. Vmp = 2.406 − 0.0063 × 42 = 2.141 V; Jmp = 17.5 + 0.0050 × 42 = 17.71 mA/cm². Power ratio = (2.141 × 17.71)/(2.406 × 17.5) = 0.901. The voltage loses 11 % and the current gains 1.2 %.

Temperature for a factor of 0.88. Solving (2.406 − 0.0063ΔT)(17.5 + 0.0050ΔT) = 0.88 × 42.1 gives ΔT = 50.6 °C, a cell at 78.6 °C.

42.1 mW/cm², FF 0.860, η 31.1 % from the average VmpJmp (30.7 % stated); 0.901 of the 28 °C power at 70 °C; 0.88 at 78.6 °C. Check: the combined coefficient, −0.26 %/°C + 0.03 %/°C = −0.23 %/°C, is about half the −0.43 %/°C of the silicon model above. Differentiating (20.3) with I0 ∝ ni² gives dVoc/dT ≈ −(Eg/q − Voc + 3kT/q)/T, so the fractional loss per kelvin is smaller for a cell whose Voc is a larger fraction of its band gaps.

From cells to an array. An array, in the deck's words, is series and parallel interconnected cells covered with a protective coating and bonded to a mechanical substrate. Cells in series add their voltages to reach the bus voltage; strings in parallel add their currents to reach the power. A string must still exceed the bus voltage at the hottest operating temperature and at end of life, which is why strings carry more cells than the 28 °C voltage alone requires: at 70 °C an XTJ Prime cell holds 2.141 V at maximum power, so a 28 V bus needs at least 13.1 cells' worth before any margin for radiation or diode drops. The coverglass shields the cell from low-energy protons and ultraviolet; the substrate carries it through launch. A shaded or broken cell in a series string cannot pass the string current and is driven into reverse bias, where it dissipates the power of the rest of the string; the bypass diode across each cell conducts instead and limits the loss to that cell's share.

Maximum power point tracking. The maximum power point moves as the sunlight, the temperature and the cell's age change. A maximum power point tracker (MPPT) is a converter between the array and the bus that continuously adjusts the array's operating voltage to keep it at the maximum of VI, for example by perturbing the voltage and keeping the direction that raises the power. Lecture 22 described MPPT as an array that moves to follow the Sun. That is sun pointing, a mechanical function of the attitude control system or the array drive, which raises the incident irradiance; MPPT is electrical and extracts the most power from whatever irradiance arrives. A spacecraft can use either, both or neither, and the deck's sizing table books the tracker's loss together with line and diode losses as a factor of 0.85.

An array operates at a fixed voltage just above the cell's 28 °C maximum power point. The array then warms to 80 °C. Explain, from (20.3), what happens to the power it delivers, and what an MPPT would do instead.

Heating raises I0, which lowers Voc and moves the whole knee of the curve to lower voltage; the current at low voltage barely changes. A voltage that was just above the 28 °C maximum power point now lies on the steep part of the hot curve beyond its knee, close to the new Voc, where the current is far below Imp. The power therefore falls much more than the 12 % that the cell's own maximum power falls. An MPPT lowers the operating voltage to the hot curve's new knee, so the array delivers the 0.88 of its 28 °C maximum (E3) rather than a fraction of it. This is why a direct-energy-transfer bus fixes its operating voltage with the hot, end-of-life curve in view.

4 · Radiation degradation of the array

Solar cells are the least shielded electronics on a spacecraft, behind only a coverglass, and they are minority-carrier devices. Both facts make displacement damage their governing radiation effect (M11 §7). This section connects the mechanism to the quantity used to predict it and to the end-of-life factor that enters the array's sizing.

Mechanism. A proton or electron that transfers enough energy to a lattice atom displaces it, leaving a vacancy and an interstitial, a Frenkel pair (M11 §2). The defect levels these create in the band gap act as recombination centres, which shorten the minority-carrier lifetime τ (M11 §6). The diffusion length (Dτ)1/2 shrinks with it, so pairs generated deep in the cell recombine before reaching the junction. Isc falls first; the added recombination also raises I0, so Voc falls as well (M11 §7). Displacement damage degrades the efficiency of the junction itself, unlike the darkening of a coverglass, which only reduces the light that reaches it.

Displacement damage dose. The damage depends on the particle and its energy through the non-ionizing energy loss, NIEL, the rate at which the particle transfers energy to the lattice through displacing collisions (M11 §4). The product of fluence and NIEL is the displacement damage dose Dd, the total absorbed non-ionizing dose, in MeV/g, by analogy with ionizing dose. The NRL method of Summers, Walters and co-workers showed that, plotted against Dd, a cell type's degradation under protons of all energies falls on one curve, and under electrons on a second; a single equivalency factor Rep shifts the electron curve onto the proton curve (for electrons the NIEL also enters with a fitted exponent). A few ground irradiations with each particle therefore characterize a cell for any mixed orbital spectrum, where the older equivalent-fluence method needed many energies:

Dd = Φ · NIEL  PP0 = 1 − C log (1 + DdDx)
(20.5)
Dd displacement damage dose (MeV/g) · Φ particle fluence (cm⁻²) · NIEL non-ionizing energy loss (MeV·cm²/g) · P/P0 remaining fraction of a cell parameter, such as maximum power, after the dose · C, Dx fitting constants for a cell type, from ground data. The first relation is M11 (11.2), for particles of one energy; for a spectrum, fluence × NIEL is summed over energies. The second is the semi-empirical fit used in the NRL method (Walters et al., NASA/CP-2007-214494).

What the deck shows. The chart on deck p. 16 plots the efficiency of eight cell technologies, relative to their initial efficiency, against displacement damage dose from 10⁸ to 2×10¹² MeV/g, with a dashed line marking "dose in orbit per year" at about 5×10¹⁰ MeV/g. The orbit is not named on the slide. Figure 5 gives each technology's relative efficiency at that line, read by calibrating the slide's axes. Silicon and GaAs/Ge retain about 0.55 and 0.53; InGaP/InGaP and the InP/GaAs/Ge cell keep about 0.67 and 0.63; InP on silicon keeps 0.88; and CdTe shows no loss at that dose, falling only above about 10¹¹ MeV/g and then steeply. A thin-film cell such as CdTe or CIGS also starts at a lower efficiency than a triple-junction cell, so the end-of-life comparison is of absolute efficiencies, not of these ratios alone.

The slide's bullets beside the chart state "efficiency reduction ~70 % in 1 year" and "~50 % in 10 years". Read as reductions, they fit no curve. Read as the efficiency remaining, they match one technology only: InP/InP is at 0.70 at the one-year line and about 0.51 at ten times that dose, the ten-year dose if it accumulates linearly. The conventional cells are already near 0.55 at the one-year line. Use the chart's readings, with the cell type and the dose stated, rather than the bullets as a rule. The conclusion the slide draws, that such cells cannot support long missions in that environment, holds for the conventional cells on its own chart.

From dose to design. A flight cell's data sheet gives its remaining factors after equivalent fluences of 1 MeV electrons, the JPL convention against which the NRL dose method was validated. For XTJ Prime, the maximum-power efficiency retains 0.94 after 10¹⁴ e/cm² (the sheet's 10-year LEO case), 0.88 after 5×10¹⁴ and 0.85 after 10¹⁵ e/cm² (its 15-year GEO case) by the US standard AIAA S-111; the European ECSS standard, which includes photon and temperature annealing, gives 0.87 at 10¹⁵, which is the sheet's headline 26.7 % end-of-life efficiency (30.7 % × 0.87). The array is sized with the end-of-life factor, not the beginning-of-life output: the deck's sizing table carries an end-of-life environment factor of 0.93 (§5). Thicker coverglass lowers the dose from low-energy protons at the cost of mass, and the damage partly anneals at the cell's operating temperature (M11 §8).

Two arrays must each deliver 100 W at end of life after the deck's one-year dose. One uses GaAs/Ge cells, the other InP/Si cells, both starting at the same efficiency. By what factor does the GaAs/Ge array have to be larger at beginning of life, and what else would change the answer?

At the one-year line GaAs/Ge keeps 0.53 of its efficiency and InP/Si 0.88 (Figure 5). Equal end-of-life power from equal starting efficiencies requires areas in inverse proportion to the remaining fractions, 0.88/0.53 = 1.66: the GaAs/Ge array must be 66 % larger. Three things change the answer. Real cells do not start at the same efficiency; the comparison is of beginning-of-life efficiency times remaining factor. The dose depends on the orbit and on the coverglass: the chart's one-year value applies to one environment and one shielding. And annealing at the operating temperature recovers part of the damage, which ground data taken at room temperature does not include.

5 · Sizing the array: distance, orbit and the sizing chain

The array must deliver the power the loads need, at end of life, through every loss between sunlight and the bus, and only while it is lit. This section takes the three things that set its size in turn: the sunlight available at the spacecraft's distance from the Sun, the fraction of each orbit spent in sunlight, and the chain of factors that reduce sunlight to power delivered to the EPS. It ends with the deck's sizing calculation worked in full.

Distance from the Sun. Sunlight spreads over a sphere, so the irradiance falls as the inverse square of the distance, S(r) = S0(1 AU/r)² (M03 (3.1)). The value of S0 depends on the reference. M03 uses 1,361 W/m², the total solar irradiance that Kopp and Lean (2011) reported from SORCE/TIM measurements at the 2008 solar minimum (1,360.8 ± 0.5 W/m²). The deck's sizing table uses 1,366.1 W/m², the solar constant to which the ASTM E-490 AM0 reference spectrum is normalized for the aerospace community. The two differ by 0.4 %, less than any of the factors in the sizing chain below; use the one the calculation at hand specifies. At Jupiter, 5.2 AU from the Sun, the irradiance is 1/5.2² = 3.7 % of its value at Earth (Figure 6).

Deck p. 17 turns the inverse square into a limit on where solar power is practical. It plots the empirical specific power of arrays, power divided by array mass, against distance, for arrays of 10, 100 and 1,600 W/kg at Earth, each falling as 1/r² to a floor of 1 W/kg, and shades the distances at which an array smaller than 100 m² can still supply 100, 300 or 1,000 W. The slide quotes specific power as typically 200–300 W/kg; NASA's 2026 small-spacecraft survey finds flown arrays clustered around 30 W/kg, none below 1 W/kg and a maximum of 200 W/kg, so the slide's "typical" 200–300 W/kg lies at and above the highest value flown, an order of magnitude above the typical flown array. For comparison, the slide gives the power consumption of Hubble as about 2,100 W and of JWST as about 1,000 W.

E4 · Europa Clipper's arrays at Jupiter

Given two arrays of 14.2 m × 4.1 m each, about 700 W at Jupiter (deck p. 18; JPL), Jupiter at 5.2 AU, and S0 = 1,361 W/m². Find the irradiance at Jupiter as a fraction of Earth's, the sunlight falling on the arrays, the fraction of it delivered as electrical power, and what the same arrays would deliver at 1 AU at the same fraction.

Array area. 2 × 14.2 m × 4.1 m = 116.4 m².

Sunlight on the arrays. 50.3 W/m² × 116.4 m² = 5,860 W, at normal incidence.

Overall fraction. 700/5,860 = 0.119; per unit area the arrays deliver 700/116.4 = 6.0 W/m².

The same arrays at 1 AU. 700 W / 0.0370 = 18,900 W at the same overall fraction. Orbit and eclipse. A spacecraft in Earth orbit passes through Earth's shadow on most orbits. In the shadow the array delivers nothing and the battery carries the whole load. When the array cannot see the Sun. The deck's two lunar landers fail on this requirement from different directions. 6 · Batteries: storage, recharge and temperature

The battery carries the spacecraft through eclipse and through peaks above the array's supply, and it is recharged by the array in sunlight. This section describes what a cell is and how it discharges, compares the chemistries the deck lists, works the deck's recharge calculation, and explains why temperature limits batteries as much as capacity does.

What a cell is. An electrochemical cell holds two electrodes of different chemical potential separated by an electrolyte that conducts ions but not electrons. On discharge, the negative electrode is oxidized and releases electrons into the external circuit, the positive electrode is reduced and accepts them, and ions cross the electrolyte to keep both electrodes neutral. The cell's voltage is set by the two electrode reactions, which is why each chemistry has its own nominal voltage: 3.6 V for lithium-ion, 1.3 V for nickel-cadmium and nickel-hydrogen, 1.5 V for silver-zinc (deck p. 24). A battery is cells in series for voltage and in parallel for capacity. A primary cell is used once; a secondary cell is recharged by driving current backwards through it, which reverses the reactions. Lecture 22 noted that satellites almost always use secondary cells.

Discharge and depth of discharge. The fraction of the rated capacity removed is the depth of discharge (DoD). At constant load, a cell's voltage follows the curve of deck p. 25 (Figure 9): a quick drop from the starting voltage within seconds to a few minutes (A), a long, gently sloping plateau that lasts minutes to days depending on the load (B), and a steep fall near exhaustion (C). On deck p. 24 the lithium-ion curve falls from about 4.2 V to about 3.4 V across most of its discharge and drops sharply near 90–95 % DoD; the nickel and silver-zinc curves are flatter, near 1.3–1.5 V. A lithium-ion cell's voltage therefore indicates its state of charge, which is the deck's "energy gauge" entry for it. Repeated cycling ages the cell: NASA's 2026 survey names temperature, charge and discharge rate, depth of discharge and storage conditions as the variables, and requires life testing under the mission's own conditions. Each recharge also changes the cell slightly, so the curve sinks and its top voltage falls with age.

Lithium-ion has displaced the nickel chemistries on new spacecraft because it stores two to four times the energy per kilogram and gauges its own charge by voltage. Its limit is temperature: the deck's operating range, 0 to 60 °C, is the widest in Table 1, but NASA's lithium-ion guidelines put the optimum between 20 and 40 °C, because chemical activity falls and the internal resistance rises at low temperature. Capacity is also lost to storage at high temperature and high state of charge, to high discharge rates, and to charging at low temperature. Overcharge can deposit lithium metal that shorts the cell internally and break down the electrolyte, raising the internal pressure. These failure modes, with the stored energy itself, are why the deck's summary treats every battery as a potential hazard.

Recharge as an additional load. Every ampere-hour drawn in eclipse must be returned in sunlight, and more must be returned than was drawn, because charging loses part of the input to side reactions in the cell and to the charger and lines. The deck expresses this as a "recharge efficiency" of 112 %: the charge returned divided by the charge removed. As a ratio greater than 1 it is the reciprocal of an efficiency; the corresponding efficiency, charge removed over charge returned, is 1/1.12 = 89 %. Depth of discharge and battery size. The 72.0 Wh drawn in E7 is not the battery's capacity. A battery that cycles every orbit, about 15 times a day in this orbit, lasts longer the shallower each cycle, so the drained energy is held to a fraction of the capacity. The fraction is set by life testing under the mission's conditions; at an allowed depth of discharge of 30 %, for example, the battery must store 72.0/0.30 = 240 Wh, 8.57 Ah at 28 V.

Batteries in the cold, and in the heat. Lecture 22 summarized the temperature problem as chemistry: heat speeds a reaction and cold slows it, so a cell rated for a given power cannot deliver it at −30 °F. The physical mechanism is the reduced chemical activity and the rise in internal resistance named above; the internal resistance also heats the cell under load as I²Rint, which is the battery's self-heating. A cold battery must be kept warm by heaters, and heaters draw from the battery. The deck attributes the loss of Vikram and Pragyan to this: no sunlight for 14 days, no array power, so no heater power, and electronics too cold to wake. ISRO reported only that no signal was received; neither craft was designed to survive the lunar night. At the other extreme, the Venus surface probes of M17c need batteries that work near 465 °C. The LLISSE probe's battery (Deck 16b p. 28) stores 95.6 Wh at 40 V (2.39 Ah) in 1.07 L and 2.83 kg, for 60 Earth days at 465 °C on the Venus surface at 90 bar, supplying 0.2 A with pulses up to 12 A. NASA's high-temperature battery projects aim higher: the HiTALL primary battery targets more than 150 Wh/kg and 30 days of operation at 500 °C and 92 bar, against under two hours for the state of the art (Deck 16b p. 29), and a molten lithium–sulfur rechargeable cell targets 300 Wh/kg and 100–150 cycles at 100 % depth of discharge, up to 500 °C (p. 36). The matching source is the low-intensity, high-temperature (LIHT) cell, a dual-junction GaInP/GaAs cell with high-temperature tunnel junctions and contacts and an Al₂O₃ corrosion coating, meant to survive 450–500 °C on the Venus surface (p. 30).

7 · Radioisotope power and other sources

Batteries, fuel cells, radioisotope generators and solar arrays are the four sources on the deck's list (p. 11). Solar arrays dominate wherever sunlight is strong enough; the others take over where it is not, or where the mission is too short to need one. This section explains the radioisotope thermoelectric generator, which powers missions beyond the practical range of solar arrays and through long nights, and places the other two.

The radioisotope thermoelectric generator. An RTG converts the heat of radioactive decay into electricity with no moving parts. Its heat source is plutonium-238 dioxide, a ceramic, clad in iridium. Pu-238 decays by alpha emission with a half-life of 87.7 years; the alpha particles stop within the fuel, and their kinetic energy becomes heat (the decay physics is M04's). The conversion uses the Seebeck effect: when two dissimilar conductors are joined in a circuit and the two junctions are held at different temperatures, a voltage appears. An RTG's thermocouples have their hot junctions against the heat source and their cold junctions on the housing, whose fins radiate the waste heat to space (Figure 11). The thermocouple legs are doped semiconductors, one n-type and one p-type, so the carriers in both diffuse from the hot end to the cold end and their voltages add around the couple.

NASA's Multi-Mission RTG (MMRTG), which powers the Curiosity and Perseverance rovers, carries 4.8 kg of plutonium dioxide in eight general-purpose heat source modules. It starts with about 2,000 W of heat and produces about 110 W of electricity at launch, from 768 thermocouples of lead telluride and TAGS (germanium, silver and antimony tellurides), at 30 V. It weighs about 45 kg and is designed to deliver an estimated 72 W after 17 years, a design life that includes three years of fueled storage before launch. Lecture 22 described the nuclear source instead as gamma rays from cobalt-60 converted to electron–hole pairs, a solar cell tuned to gamma rays, using "the Einstein effect". That describes neither an RTG nor its fuel: an RTG converts heat thermoelectrically, and its fuel is an alpha emitter chosen because its radiation is easy to contain and its half-life suits a mission of decades. Direct conversion of radiation in a semiconductor junction does exist, in betavoltaic cells, at far lower power.

Fuel cells and primary batteries. A fuel cell is an electrochemical cell whose reactants are supplied continuously rather than stored in the electrodes. In the hydrogen–oxygen cell Lecture 22 described, hydrogen is oxidized at one electrode, the ions cross an electrolyte, and they combine with oxygen at the other to form water. Its energy is limited by the reactants carried, so it suits missions of limited duration rather than satellites that must run for years. A primary battery is likewise limited to the energy it is launched with, which is why it appears on short missions and in developments such as the HiTALL cell above, for a surface with no means of recharge. Lecture 22 ranked solar first, as the common source of almost every spacecraft, with the deck's summary naming battery-only and solar-array-plus-battery as the common CubeSat architectures.

A mission to Saturn, 9.5 AU from the Sun, needs 300 W of electrical power for 14 years. Compare what a solar array and MMRTGs would require, using E4's overall fraction and the MMRTG fact-sheet values.

Solar: the irradiance at 9.5 AU is 1,361/9.5² = 15.1 W/m². At E4's overall fraction of 0.119, an array delivers 1.80 W/m², so 300 W needs 167 m², larger than the slide's 100 m² limit for 300 W (deck p. 17) and before any allowance for the lower intensity and colder cells. MMRTGs: the fact sheet gives 110 W "at launch" and 72 W at the end of a 17-year design life that includes three years of storage before launch. Read literally, a generator delivers 72 W 14 years after launch, so 300 W at end of mission needs 300/72 = 4.2, that is five generators, carrying 24 kg of plutonium dioxide and weighing about 225 kg. With E9's reading (110 W at the start of the 17 years, 2.5 % a year), 110 × 0.97514 ≈ 78 W, and four generators (312 W) would just suffice; that margin is too thin to rest on an ambiguous sheet, so plan on five. The comparison is why missions beyond Jupiter have used radioisotope power, and why a solar-powered Jupiter mission such as Europa Clipper needs arrays of 116 m².

Summary

  • The EPS generates, stores, distributes, regulates and protects, and monitors every block. It is sized by two numbers: the orbit-average power sizes the array, the peak sizes the battery current, the converters and the wiring (20.1).
  • Harness loss scales as (P/V)²R (20.2): going from 28 V to 100 V cuts it by about 13. Higher buses are limited by single-event burnout and gate rupture in power MOSFETs and by arcing.
  • Direct energy transfer connects the array to the bus through a diode and diverts surplus current with a shunt regulator; the bus is unregulated (full or partial shunt) or fully regulated by a battery charger and discharger.
  • A solar cell is a current source in parallel with its junction diode (20.3); the maximum power point, the fill factor and the efficiency follow (20.4). Heat lowers Voc through I0 ∝ ni²: about −2 mV/K in silicon, −0.23 %/°C in power for a triple-junction cell. MPPT is electrical; sun pointing is mechanical.
  • Displacement damage shortens the minority-carrier lifetime; degradation follows the displacement damage dose Φ·NIEL (20.5). Arrays are sized at end of life.
  • Irradiance falls as 1/r²: 3.7 % of Earth's at Jupiter. Eclipse takes up to about 35–40 % of a low orbit (37 % at 574 km) (20.6). The array area follows from the sizing chain (20.7): the deck's 120 W needs 0.661 m².
  • A recharge returns more charge than the eclipse removed (the deck's ratio 1.12), as an extra load on the array in sunlight (20.8): 2.62 A for the deck's 144 W, 30-minute eclipse.
  • Batteries are limited by temperature as much as by capacity: lithium-ion works best between 20 and 40 °C, a cold battery needs heater power, and Venus probes need cells that run near 465–500 °C.
  • An RTG converts Pu-238 alpha-decay heat thermoelectrically, at about 5.5 % for the MMRTG; its heat falls with the 87.7-year half-life and its electrical output faster (20.9).
