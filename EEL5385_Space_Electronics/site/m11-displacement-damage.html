Module 11

Displacement Damage

What happens when an incident particle strikes a nucleus hard enough to knock the atom out of its lattice site: the defects that result, the four things a defect level does to a semiconductor, and why slowing a particle down can make the damage worse.

Prerequisites M08, M01 Read time ~40 min Sources Deck 5; Lecture 5

Every mechanism so far has involved the incident particle interacting with the electrons of the target. Ionization removes them, excitation promotes them, bremsstrahlung is produced by deflection in the field of the nucleus without touching it. The damage is electrical, and much of it is transient: carriers recombine, excited atoms de-excite, and the crystal is unchanged.

Displacement damage is different in kind. Here the incident particle transfers enough momentum to a nucleus to knock the entire atom out of its position in the crystal lattice. The atom does not return. What is left is a permanent structural defect that changes the electronic properties of the material at that point, and those changes accumulate for as long as the device is exposed. This is a volumetric effect — the whole silicon volume accumulates damage, not just an interface or an oxide — and it degrades the bulk properties that minority-carrier devices depend on.

The module builds the mechanism from the collision, follows the cascade of secondary displacements it produces, gives the quantity that predicts the damage from a known environment, and then works through the four distinct things a defect level in the bandgap does to a device. It closes on the counter-intuitive shielding consequence that the lectures returned to explicitly.

1 · Striking the nucleus

M08 established that the nucleus occupies roughly 10−15 of the volume of an atom, which is why an uncharged particle interacts so rarely. The same geometry means that nuclear collisions are rare for charged particles too — a charged particle undergoes an enormous number of electron interactions for every nuclear one. Ionization therefore accounts for almost all of a particle's energy loss, and displacement damage accounts for almost none of it.

The disproportion between energy and consequence is the point. A single displaced atom creates a defect that persists for the life of the device and affects carrier behaviour in the volume around it. A million ionizations create carriers that recombine within microseconds and leave nothing behind. A fraction of a percent of the deposited energy produces effects out of all proportion to its share.

Which particles cause displacement damage follows from which can transfer enough momentum to a nucleus. Neutrons are the classic case, because a nuclear collision is the only interaction available to them. Protons and heavy ions do it readily, having both charge for a Coulomb interaction with the nucleus and enough mass to transfer significant momentum. Electrons can do it in silicon only above about 220 keV — the energy at which the relativistic two-body transfer first reaches the 21 eV threshold of §2 — because an electron is too light to transfer much momentum to an atom 51,000 times its mass. Photons cannot do it directly at all; they cause displacement only through the energetic electrons they liberate.

2 · The threshold energy and the Frenkel pair

An atom in a crystal sits in a potential well formed by its bonds to its neighbours. Removing it requires a minimum energy, the threshold displacement energy Ed, and a collision that transfers less than that merely sets the atom vibrating — the energy becomes heat and the lattice is undamaged. For silicon Ed is about 21 eV, roughly twenty times the bandgap and some nine times the 2.3 eV energy of a single Si–Si bond, because the atom must break free of four bonds at once and then push past its neighbours to reach a site it can rest in.

When the transfer exceeds the threshold, the atom leaves its site and comes to rest somewhere between lattice positions. Two defects are created at once and they are always created together:

  • a vacancy — the empty lattice site the atom left, where four bonds now terminate on nothing; and
  • an interstitial — the displaced atom itself, wedged into a position the crystal structure does not provide for.

The pair is called a Frenkel pair, and it is the elementary unit of displacement damage. Both members disturb the local bonding, and both introduce allowed electron energy levels inside the bandgap where the perfect crystal has none — which is the entire electrical significance of the defect, developed in §6.

The value of Ed is not a sharply defined constant. It depends on the direction of the recoil relative to the crystal axes, and published values for silicon range from about 13 eV to 25 eV depending on the measurement and the convention. Twenty-one electron-volts is the value most often used in displacement-damage calculations. The choice matters directly for simulation: a vacancy count computed with Ed = 15 eV differs from one computed at 21 eV by roughly 40 %, and the assumed threshold is an input to the SRIM calculations this course is built around (M13).

3 · The primary knock-on atom and the cascade

A collision that displaces an atom rarely transfers only the threshold energy. Typical transfers from an energetic incident particle are thousands of times larger, and the displaced atom therefore leaves its site with substantial kinetic energy. It is now itself an energetic heavy ion inside the crystal, and it is called the primary knock-on atom.

The maximum energy an incident particle of mass m can transfer to a target nucleus of mass M is given by the elastic-collision expression already met in M04. For a 1 MeV neutron striking a silicon nucleus the factor 4Mm/(M+m)² evaluates to 0.133, so the primary knock-on atom can carry away up to 133 keV — more than six thousand times the displacement threshold.

That atom then travels through the lattice, displacing further atoms, each of which may displace more. The result is a branching collision cascade: a tree of displacements spreading from the initial event, ending when every moving atom has fallen below the threshold energy and the remaining energy has become lattice vibration. A single incident particle can therefore produce hundreds or thousands of Frenkel pairs concentrated in a region tens of nanometres across.

4 · Non-ionizing energy loss

A particle crossing a material divides its energy loss between two accounts: the part that goes into exciting and ionising electrons, and the part that goes into displacing atoms. The second is the non-ionizing energy loss, universally abbreviated NIEL, and it is the quantity that predicts displacement damage from a known particle environment.

NIEL is defined as the rate of non-ionizing energy deposition per unit path length, normalised by material density, in MeV·cm²/g. It depends on the incident species and strongly on its energy, and it plays the same role for displacement damage that linear energy transfer plays for single-event effects and that dose plays for total ionizing dose. Multiplying NIEL by particle fluence gives the displacement damage dose, the accumulated non-ionizing energy per gram, and the central experimental result of the field is that device degradation correlates with this quantity largely independently of which particle produced it. A proton environment and an electron environment that produce the same displacement damage dose produce approximately the same degradation, which is what makes ground testing with a single species meaningful. The scaling is an approximation with documented limits: it breaks down for protons below a few MeV, and for effects that depend on defect clustering rather than on total defect number — charge transfer efficiency in an imager is the standard example — because a dense cascade and a sparse one deposit the same non-ionizing energy in very different spatial distributions.

5 · Why a particle that stops is worse than one that passes

This is the result the lectures flagged explicitly, and it reverses the intuition that more shielding is always better.

A particle's energy loss to nuclear collisions is not uniform along its path. At high velocity the particle spends very little time near any one nucleus, the momentum transfer per encounter is small, and almost all of its energy goes to electrons instead. As it slows, the interaction time with each nucleus lengthens, momentum transfers grow, and the nuclear contribution rises steeply. Near the end of the range, where the particle is moving too slowly to ionise efficiently, nuclear stopping overtakes electronic stopping; it peaks and then falls again at the very lowest energies as electron screening weakens the interaction. The last micrometres of a particle's track therefore contain the large majority of the displacements it will ever produce.

The shielding consequence follows immediately. Consider an energetic proton that would pass entirely through a die, depositing a small fraction of its energy and producing a correspondingly small number of displacements. Place a shield in front of it that is thick enough to slow the proton but not thick enough to stop it, and the proton now arrives at the die with reduced energy and comes to rest inside the sensitive volume — delivering its full end-of-range displacement peak exactly where it does the most harm. The shield has converted a passing particle into a stopping one, and increased the damage.

This does not make shielding useless; it makes shield thickness a quantity that must be computed rather than maximised. A shield thick enough to stop the incident spectrum entirely is unambiguously helpful. A shield in the intermediate regime can be worse than none at all for part of the spectrum. Determining which regime a given design sits in requires knowing the particle ranges in the shield material, which is what SRIM computes and why the tool sits at the centre of this course.

The same reversal appears in the laboratory. A test that uses a lower-energy beam because it is more readily available can produce more displacement damage per incident particle than the higher-energy environment it is meant to represent, because the lower-energy particles stop within the device while the flight particles pass through. Comparing test and flight environments by fluence alone is invalid; they must be compared by displacement damage dose, which is what equation (11.2) is for.

6 · What a defect level does

A perfect semiconductor crystal has no allowed electron states between the valence band maximum and the conduction band minimum. That empty gap is the reason a semiconductor behaves as one: an electron can only move from valence to conduction band by acquiring the full bandgap energy, and at room temperature very few do.

A vacancy or an interstitial breaks the periodicity that produced the gap. The dangling or distorted bonds around the defect have energies that fall inside the forbidden range, so the defect introduces localised allowed states there — trap levels. An electron can now cross the gap in two steps instead of one, or be captured and held. Deck 5 identifies four distinct consequences, and each corresponds to a different device symptom.

(a) Thermal carrier generation increases, raising leakage current. An electron in the valence band needs 1.1 eV to reach the conduction band directly, and thermal energy at room temperature supplies 0.026 eV. With a trap level near mid-gap the journey becomes two steps of about 0.55 eV each, and the probability of completing both is enormously higher than the probability of the single large step. Generation rate rises, and in a reverse-biased junction every generated pair is swept out as leakage current. This is the dominant displacement-damage symptom in a diode or an imaging pixel, and it appears as increased dark current.

(b) Recombination increases, destroying minority-carrier lifetime. The same two-step path runs in reverse: a conduction-band electron is captured by the trap and then falls to the valence band, annihilating a hole. Minority-carrier lifetime is the average time before this happens, and it is the parameter that every bipolar and photovoltaic device depends on. A solar cell converts light to current only if a photogenerated carrier survives long enough to reach the junction; a bipolar transistor has current gain only if injected minority carriers cross the base without recombining. Displacement damage reduces both, which is why bipolar devices and solar cells are the parts most sensitive to it while MOS devices — majority-carrier devices — are far less affected.

(c) Free-carrier trapping is enhanced, delaying and losing signal charge. A trap can capture a carrier and release it later rather than passing it to the other band. The carrier is not lost permanently but it is removed from the signal for a time set by the trap's emission rate. In a charge-coupled device, where a packet of signal charge must be shifted through thousands of transfers, this appears as charge transfer inefficiency: each pixel leaves a little of its signal behind and picks up some of the previous pixel's, smearing the image. The random capture and release also generates noise, and in the most damaged pixels produces random telegraph signal — a dark current that switches between discrete levels.

(d) Donor–acceptor trap pairs compensate the doping, reducing free-carrier concentration. Some defect complexes act as donors and others as acceptors. An acceptor level introduced into n-type material captures an electron from the donor population, removing it from the conduction band without any carrier having recombined. The net doping falls, resistivity rises, and depletion widths change. This is carrier removal, and in heavily irradiated silicon it can drive the material toward intrinsic behaviour or even invert its type.

Displacement damage is a bulk, minority-carrier effect, so it is the governing concern for solar arrays, optoelectronics and imagers, and a secondary one for digital logic. Digital parts are instead governed by charge trapped in oxides, which is total ionizing dose (M14), and by prompt charge collection, which is single-event effects (M15). A complete radiation analysis carries all three quantities separately, because no one of them predicts the others.

8 · Annealing

Displacement damage is permanent in the sense that the lattice does not spontaneously return to its original configuration, but the defect population does evolve after the irradiation stops. Vacancies and interstitials are mobile at room temperature in silicon, and several things follow.

Most Frenkel pairs recombine within a few to a few tens of picoseconds of creation, while the cascade is still quenching, because the vacancy and the interstitial are formed only a few lattice spacings apart and the interstitial is attracted back to the empty site. Equation (11.1) counts displacements rather than survivors; molecular-dynamics simulations put the surviving fraction near 30 % for a dense cascade and close to unity for a near-threshold recoil that produces one isolated pair. Of the defects that do survive, many migrate and combine with impurities or with each other to form stable complexes — a vacancy with a phosphorus donor, a vacancy with an oxygen atom, or two vacancies together — and it is these complexes rather than the isolated point defects that produce most of the electrically active levels in an irradiated device.

The measurable consequence is that a device's degradation is time-dependent after exposure. Part of the damage anneals within minutes to hours at room temperature, and further annealing can be induced deliberately by heating. A measurement made immediately after irradiation and one made a week later give different answers, so displacement-damage test standards specify the interval between exposure and measurement.

A spacecraft's solar array and its digital processor are exposed to the same proton environment. The array loses 15 % of its power over the mission and the processor shows no measurable displacement effect. Both are silicon. Why?

Both accumulate the same displacement damage dose and therefore roughly the same density of defect levels. What differs is whether the device's operation depends on a parameter those levels degrade.

A solar cell is a minority-carrier device. A photon absorbed in the bulk generates an electron–hole pair that must diffuse to the junction before it recombines, and the fraction that succeeds is set by the minority-carrier diffusion length, which scales as the square root of the lifetime. Mechanism (b) attacks that lifetime directly, so collected current falls and delivered power falls with it. This is why array sizing is done against an end-of-life figure computed from the mission's displacement damage dose rather than against the initial output.

A digital MOSFET is a majority-carrier device. Current flows by drift of carriers already present in an inversion layer a few nanometres thick, over a channel length well under a micrometre. There is no diffusion over a long path and no dependence on minority-carrier lifetime, so degrading the lifetime changes almost nothing. The processor is not immune to radiation — it is exposed to total ionizing dose and to single-event effects — but displacement damage is not the mechanism that will limit it.

Summary

  • Displacement damage is a nuclear-collision effect: the incident particle knocks an atom off its lattice site permanently. It is volumetric, cumulative, and accounts for a tiny fraction of the deposited energy while producing effects out of proportion to that share.
  • Displacing an atom requires the threshold energy Ed, about 21 eV in silicon, and produces a Frenkel pair — one vacancy and one interstitial, created together.
  • The displaced atom is the primary knock-on atom and usually carries far more than the threshold: a 1 MeV neutron can give a silicon nucleus up to 133 keV. It then displaces further atoms in a branching cascade, giving hundreds to thousands of displacements from one particle.
  • Nd = 0.8Tdam/2Ed counts displacements, not survivors. Most Frenkel pairs recombine within picoseconds, leaving roughly 30 % of a dense cascade behind; the survivors form stable complexes with impurities, and those carry the electrical activity.
  • Non-ionizing energy loss (MeV·cm²/g) times fluence gives displacement damage dose, and degradation correlates with that quantity largely independently of species — which is what makes single-species ground testing valid. The scaling fails below a few MeV for protons and for cluster-sensitive effects such as charge transfer efficiency.
  • Nuclear stopping overtakes electronic stopping near the end of a particle's range, so a particle that stops inside the device does far more damage than one that passes through. A shield too thin to stop the spectrum can convert passing particles into stopping ones and increase the damage.
  • A defect level in the gap does four things: (a) two-step thermal generation → leakage and dark current; (b) recombination → lost minority-carrier lifetime and gain; (c) trapping and delayed release → charge transfer inefficiency and noise; (d) donor–acceptor compensation → carrier removal.
  • Solar cells, bipolar transistors, imagers and optocouplers are governed by displacement damage because they are minority-carrier devices. Digital MOS is largely unaffected and is governed by total ionizing dose and single-event effects instead.
