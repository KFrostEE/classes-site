Module 04

Radiation: Species, Decay, and Laboratory Sources

What each kind of radiation is, how each is produced, how deeply each penetrates and why, and the arithmetic that turns a quantity of radioactive material into a number of particles per second.

Prerequisites M01 Read time ~45 min Sources Deck 3; Lecture 3

The course is organised around a chain of three questions, and every module from here to the end of the radiation half of the course sits at one link in it. First: what radiation exists in space, of what type and at what energy. Second: how radiation of that type and energy interacts with matter. Third: what that interaction does to a device. The chain runs in one direction — the species and its energy determine the interaction, and the interaction determines the failure mechanism — so nothing later can be reasoned about until the inventory at the front is established.

This module builds that inventory. It covers the particles and photons that make up space and terrestrial radiation environments, the nuclear processes that emit them, the ordering of their penetrating power and the single physical property that explains the ordering, and the exponential law that governs how a radioactive source weakens with time. It closes on the two isotopes that appear in almost every radiation-effects laboratory, and on the units in which absorbed radiation is measured. M05 then asks where the space environment gets its supply.

1 · The split the course uses: mass or no mass

Radiation is energy in transit. The course divides it in one place and keeps the division throughout: radiation with mass, meaning particles that carry rest mass and therefore kinetic energy, and radiation without mass, meaning photons, which carry energy and momentum but no rest mass. The division is useful because the two groups deposit energy in matter by different mechanisms and are stopped by different materials, and because the space environment supplies both in quantity.

Within the massive group, a second property does most of the work in predicting behaviour: electric charge. A charged particle interacts with every atom whose electron cloud it passes near, through the Coulomb force, which acts continuously and at a distance. An uncharged particle has no such handle on the electron cloud and must strike a nucleus to interact at all. That single difference sets ranges apart by orders of magnitude, and it is developed in §5.

X-ray and gamma ray are the same physical object. Both are photons, and a 662 keV X-ray and a 662 keV gamma ray are indistinguishable once emitted. The two names record where the photon was made: gamma rays come from a nuclear transition, X-rays from processes involving atomic electrons. That distinction matters because it tells you what energies to expect. Nuclear transitions are discrete, so gamma sources emit sharp lines at fixed energies. Electron deceleration is continuous, so an X-ray tube emits a broad continuum whose upper limit is set by the accelerating voltage — treated in M09.

2 · The charged massive particles

Three of the charged species reach a spacecraft directly from the environment — protons, electrons and heavy ions — and a fourth, the alpha particle, matters both as a component of the environment and as the most familiar product of radioactive decay. Each is described here by what it is, how it is produced, and the one property that governs how it loses energy.

2.1 Alpha particles

An alpha particle is a helium-4 nucleus: two protons and two neutrons bound together, with no electrons attached, giving a net charge of +2e and a mass of about 4 atomic mass units. It is emitted when a heavy nucleus sheds that four-nucleon cluster to move toward a more stable configuration. Alpha decay reduces the atomic number by two and the mass number by four, so the daughter is a different element two places lower in the periodic table.

Alpha particles from radioactive decay emerge with kinetic energies between about 3 and 7 MeV, the range Deck 3 quotes; across the full set of natural and artificial alpha emitters the range runs from roughly 4 to 9 MeV. Either way it is narrow compared with the other species. That narrowness follows from the emission mechanism: the alpha carries away a fixed fraction of a fixed nuclear energy difference, so every alpha from a given isotope has very nearly the same energy. A 3 MeV alpha travels at about 4 % of the speed of light, roughly 1.2 × 107 m/s, obtained from ½mv² with m = 6.64 × 10−27 kg.

Source discrepancy. Deck 3 gives the alpha velocity as "approximately 4 % of the speed of light, 1.2 × 106 m/s". The percentage is correct; the figure in metres per second is a factor of ten low. Four percent of c = 3.00 × 108 m/s is 1.2 × 107 m/s, which is also what ½mv² returns for a 3 MeV alpha.

On the lead-210 example. Deck 3 uses ²¹⁰Pb → ²⁰⁶Hg + α to illustrate alpha decay. The branch is real and the alpha energy is 3.72 MeV, but it is one of the rarest decay modes measured: its branching ratio is about 1.9 × 10−8, so fewer than two lead-210 nuclei in a hundred million take it. Essentially all lead-210 decays by β− emission to bismuth-210. The half-life is 22.2 years, not the 22.8 years printed on the slide. Neither point affects the physics the slide is illustrating, but the numbers should be right. ENSDF/NNDC evaluated decay data for 210Pb.

2.2 Beta particles

A beta particle is an electron that originates in the nucleus rather than in an atomic shell. Nuclei contain no electrons; the electron is created at the moment of decay, when a neutron inside the nucleus converts into a proton. The conversion emits two particles that did not previously exist — an electron and an electron antineutrino — and conserves charge, because the neutral neutron becomes a positive proton and a negative electron.

Because the available energy is shared between the electron and the antineutrino in any proportion, beta particles from a given isotope emerge with a continuous spectrum of energies from zero up to a maximum. That maximum, the endpoint energy, is the quantity quoted for a beta source; the average energy is roughly a third of it. This is the sharpest contrast with alpha decay, where every particle carries almost the same energy.

Carbon-14 is the course's example. It decays to nitrogen-14 with a half-life of 5,700 years and a beta endpoint energy of 156 keV. The mass number is unchanged because a neutron became a proton; the atomic number rises by one.

2.3 Protons and heavy ions

A proton is a hydrogen nucleus, charge +1e, mass 1,836 times the electron's. In space it arrives from three distinct populations — trapped in Earth's magnetic field, ejected by the Sun, or arriving from outside the solar system — and the energies differ by orders of magnitude between them. Those populations are the subject of M05 and M06.

A heavy ion is the nucleus of any element heavier than helium, travelling with most or all of its electrons stripped away. Its charge is +Ze, where Z is the atomic number, and since the rate of energy deposition scales as the square of the charge, an iron nucleus with Z = 26 deposits energy some 676 times more densely than a proton of the same velocity. Heavy ions are rare compared with protons — iron is a few parts in ten thousand of the cosmic-ray flux by number — but that concentration of deposited energy makes them the species responsible for the most destructive single-particle failures in electronics, which is why the course returns to them repeatedly.

3 · The neutron

The neutron is a nuclear constituent with almost the same mass as a proton and no electric charge. Its lack of charge changes everything about how it moves through matter: it exerts no Coulomb force on atomic electrons and feels none from nuclei, so it passes through the electron cloud of an atom without interacting at all. A neutron only interacts when it comes within the range of the nuclear force of a nucleus, which means a near-direct hit on a target occupying roughly 10−15 of the volume of its atom.

The consequence is that neutrons are the most penetrating of the massive particles, and that they do their damage indirectly. A neutron does not ionise; it transfers energy to a nucleus, and the recoiling charged nucleus then ionises along its own track. A neutron can also be captured, leaving the absorbing nucleus in an excited state that decays by emitting a gamma ray, or leaving it radioactive so that it later emits alpha, beta or gamma radiation of its own. Every observable effect of a neutron is therefore the effect of a secondary charged particle or photon.

Free neutrons are not stable. Away from a nucleus a neutron decays with a half-life of about 10 minutes into a proton, an electron and an electron antineutrino, releasing 0.78 MeV — the same process that produces beta particles inside a nucleus. Ten minutes is long compared with the time a neutron spends crossing any apparatus, so this instability never matters for shielding, but it is the reason free neutrons do not accumulate.

When a neutron does reach a nucleus, several outcomes are possible, and which one occurs depends on the neutron's energy and on the nuclide it strikes. In elastic scattering the neutron bounces off and the nucleus recoils, taking kinetic energy but remaining the same nuclide; this is the moderation process of §6 and the main way neutrons deposit energy in silicon. In inelastic scattering the nucleus is left in an excited state and returns to its ground state by emitting a gamma ray. In radiative capture, written (n,γ), the neutron is absorbed outright and the new, heavier nuclide sheds the binding energy as a gamma ray; this channel dominates at low neutron energies, where the neutron moves slowly enough to spend a long time near the nucleus. A neutron can also be absorbed and a charged particle emitted instead, written (n,p) or (n,α). The (n,α) reaction on boron-10 is the clearest example of why this matters for electronics: boron is present in the borophosphosilicate glass used as an interlayer dielectric, and the alpha particle and lithium recoil it produces deposit charge directly in the circuitry below. Fission, the splitting of a heavy nucleus into two large fragments, is the last outcome, and §3.1 takes it up.

Charge is the property that decides whether a particle interacts continuously or only on impact. Charged particles interact with every atom they pass near, lose energy in small increments, and stop within a well-defined distance. Uncharged radiation — neutrons and photons — interacts only in discrete, improbable events, travels far between them, and is attenuated rather than stopped. Every entry in the penetration ordering of §5 follows from this one distinction.

3.1 Why a neutron can split a heavy nucleus

A charged projectile has to work to reach a nucleus. A proton approaching uranium is repelled by the charge of 92 protons, and it reaches the range of the nuclear force readily only if it arrives with energy approaching the height of that repulsion at the nuclear surface, the Coulomb barrier, which for uranium is 12–15 MeV. A neutron meets no barrier, so it reaches the nucleus at any energy down to thermal. Once absorbed it is bound, and the binding energy released — the neutron separation energy of the new nuclide, a few MeV — is not carried away but stays in the nucleus as internal excitation, shared among all its nucleons. A nucleus in this transient excited state is called a compound nucleus. Radiative capture sheds the excitation as a gamma ray; in the heaviest nuclei the compound nucleus can instead split.

Fission is that split. A nucleus is held together by the short-range attraction of the nuclear force and pushed apart by the long-range Coulomb repulsion between its protons. In a heavy nucleus the two are nearly balanced, and only a modest energy, the fission barrier, separates the compact shape from an elongated one in which Coulomb repulsion wins and the fragments fly apart. A captured neutron splits the nucleus when the excitation it brings exceeds that barrier. Captured by uranium-235, it releases 6.55 MeV into a uranium-236 compound nucleus whose barrier is 5.67 MeV, so even a neutron with essentially no kinetic energy induces fission. Captured by uranium-238, it releases only 4.81 MeV into uranium-239, whose barrier is 6.45 MeV, and the neutron must supply the 1.6 MeV difference as kinetic energy. The measured onset agrees: fission of uranium-238 is negligible below 1 MeV, becomes appreciable between 1.2 and 1.5 MeV, beginning slightly below 1.6 MeV because the nucleus can tunnel through the top of the barrier, and holds nearly steady from 2 to 6 MeV. Above about 6 MeV it roughly doubles, because the compound nucleus can then emit a neutron and still retain enough excitation to split.

The asymmetry between the two isotopes comes from nucleon pairing: uranium-235 has an odd neutron number, so the added neutron completes a pair and binds more strongly. Fission is peripheral to this course, which is concerned with neutrons as they are encountered in test facilities and in the atmospheric secondary environment rather than in reactors, but it is the same competition between nuclear attraction and Coulomb repulsion that makes heavy nuclei radioactive in the first place.

3.2 What a neutron does in silicon

Silicon is far too light to fission, so a neutron that reaches a silicon nucleus scatters from it or is absorbed. Elastic scattering dominates. Each collision can hand the nucleus up to 13 % of the neutron's energy (§5), and the recoiling silicon atom ionises along its short track and displaces further atoms: it is the primary knock-on atom of M11. Above a few MeV, reactions that emit a charged particle open as well. Silicon-28, 92 % of natural silicon, undergoes ²⁸Si(n,α)²⁵Mg above 2.75 MeV and ²⁸Si(n,p)²⁸Al above 4.0 MeV; both consume energy rather than release it, which is why each has a threshold. The alpha particle or proton and the heavy magnesium or aluminium recoil deposit their energy densely over short tracks. These recoils and reaction products are the route by which the high-energy neutrons of the atmospheric secondary environment upset memory cells on the ground.

At thermal energy the exception is boron-10, 20 % of natural boron, which absorbs slow neutrons more than twenty thousand times as readily per nucleus as silicon does. The reaction ¹⁰B(n,α)⁷Li leaves the lithium nucleus excited in 94 % of events, and the products are then a 1.47 MeV alpha particle, a 0.84 MeV lithium ion and a 0.48 MeV gamma ray; in the remaining 6 % the alpha particle carries 1.78 MeV and the lithium ion 1.01 MeV. The alpha particle stops within about 5.5 µm of silicon and the lithium ion sooner, so a capture in a boron-bearing layer just above the transistors, such as the borophosphosilicate glass named above, deposits its charge in the circuitry below. Removing that glass, or making it with boron-11, which absorbs almost no neutrons, removed a major cause of upsets in memory.

4 · Radiation without mass

A photon is a quantum of the electromagnetic field: it carries energy and momentum, travels at the speed of light in vacuum, and has no rest mass and no charge. The electromagnetic spectrum is a continuum in photon energy, and the familiar names — radio, infrared, visible, ultraviolet, X-ray, gamma — mark ranges within it rather than different physical entities. Photon energy and wavelength are linked by E = hc/λ, which in the units the course uses is E(eV) = 1,240 / λ(nm).

Gamma rays are emitted when a nucleus that is left in an excited state after alpha or beta decay drops to a lower state. Nuclear energy levels are discrete and widely spaced, so the emitted photons are monoenergetic lines in the hundreds of keV to several MeV range. A source characterised by a single well-known gamma energy is exactly what a test laboratory wants, and §8 shows which isotopes provide it.

5 · Penetration, and why charge sets the ordering

The standard ordering of penetrating power is alpha stopped by a sheet of paper, beta by a few millimetres of aluminium, gamma attenuated by lead, and neutrons moderated by water or paraffin. The ordering is not arbitrary and it is not a property of the materials alone. It follows from charge, mass, and one property of the target material that only matters for neutrons.

The alpha particle is stopped first because it is doubly charged and slow. The Coulomb force it exerts on a nearby electron is twice that of a singly charged particle, and its energy loss per unit path scales as the square of that charge — four times a proton of the same speed. Because it is roughly 7,000 times the mass of an electron, it is barely deflected by the collisions and travels in a straight line, dumping its energy into a short, dense track. A 5 MeV alpha stops within about 4 cm of air or 25 µm of silicon.

The beta particle travels further because it is singly charged and light. It loses energy one quarter as fast as an alpha of the same velocity. Being the same mass as the electrons it scatters from, it can be deflected through large angles in a single collision, so its path is a random walk and its penetration depth is substantially less than its total path length. A few millimetres of aluminium stops a beta of a few MeV. Being light also makes it the only common species that radiates appreciably when deflected, which is the subject of M09.

The gamma ray is attenuated rather than stopped. Having no charge, it does not interact continuously; it either undergoes a discrete interaction with an atomic electron or a nucleus, or it does not. A beam of gamma rays therefore loses intensity exponentially with depth and has no definite range — some fraction always penetrates. Lead is used because the probability of the dominant interactions rises steeply with atomic number, so a high-Z material provides the most attenuation per unit thickness. The mechanisms behind that Z dependence are the subject of M10.

The neutron is moderated rather than absorbed, and the best moderator is hydrogen. Since a neutron loses energy only by colliding with nuclei, the useful quantity is how much energy one collision removes. In an elastic head-on collision between a neutron of mass m and a nucleus of mass M, the fraction of kinetic energy transferred is 4Mm/(M+m)². That expression is maximised when the masses are equal, and a hydrogen nucleus is a single proton of almost exactly the neutron's mass: one head-on collision can remove all of the neutron's energy. The same collision with a lead nucleus removes at most 1.9 %. Water and paraffin are used because they are dense in hydrogen, and lead — the best gamma shield available — is nearly useless against neutrons.

A shield chosen for one species can be actively harmful against another. Lead attenuates gamma rays efficiently and moderates neutrons almost not at all, while adding mass that generates secondary radiation. Aluminium stops beta particles but converts part of their energy into penetrating X-rays in the process (M09). A shield thick enough to slow an energetic ion but not thick enough to stop it before it reaches the die will increase the damage that ion does, because a particle deposits energy fastest just before it stops (M11). Shielding is treated as a system problem in M16 for this reason.

6 · Radioactive decay

A radioactive nucleus has a fixed probability of decaying in any given interval, and that probability does not change with the age of the nucleus or with temperature, pressure or chemical state. A population of identical nuclei therefore shrinks at a rate proportional to how many remain, which is the definition of exponential decay. The constant of proportionality, the decay constant λ, is the probability per unit time that any one nucleus decays.

N = N0 e−λt

7 · Activity, and what a source is worth

The quantity that matters in a laboratory is not how many nuclei a source contains but how many decays it produces per second. That is the activity, and it is the product of the decay constant and the population: A = λN. Its SI unit is the becquerel, defined as one decay per second. The older unit, the curie, is 3.7 × 1010 Bq — originally the activity of one gram of radium-226 — and remains in use for the sealed sources found in test cells.

Activity is usually needed for a given mass of material rather than a given number of nuclei, so the population is obtained from the mass, the atomic mass and Avogadro's number. Combining that with equation (4.3) gives the form the course uses.

A = mma NA ln 2t1/2
(4.4)
A activity (Bq = decays/s) · m mass of the radioactive isotope present (g) — not the mass of the element, unless the element is pure isotope · ma atomic mass of that isotope (g/mol) · NA Avogadro constant, 6.022 × 1023 mol−1 · t1/2 half-life in seconds. The middle two factors are the number of nuclei, N = nNA; the last factor is λ. Formula sheet §6

The distinction between the mass of the element and the mass of the isotope is where this calculation is most often lost. Natural potassium is only 0.0117 % potassium-40, so one gram of potassium contains 117 µg of the radioactive isotope. With ma = 39.964 g/mol and the slide's half-life of 1.3 × 109 years — about 4 × 1016 s — equation (4.4) returns roughly 30 Bq per gram of potassium. The current evaluated half-life is 1.248 × 109 y, which raises the result to 31 Bq/g and changes nothing else. An adult body holds about 140 g of potassium, giving the 4,430 Bq of potassium-40 quoted in Deck 3. Every person is a radioactive source at that level continuously, and the number is a useful reference point against which laboratory sources can be scaled.

Source clarification. Deck 3 lists "Human Body: 4430 Bq, Carbon-14: 8.5 × 1018 Bq" on one line, which reads as two per-body figures. The first is correct and is the body's potassium-40 activity. The second is the global natural inventory of carbon-14 across the whole biosphere and atmosphere. A human body carries roughly 3,000–4,000 Bq of carbon-14, so total body activity is around 7,000–8,000 Bq.

8 · The laboratory gamma standards: caesium-137 and cobalt-60

Testing a part for total ionising dose requires a source that delivers a known, uniform, penetrating photon field at a predictable rate for years without maintenance. Two isotopes meet that specification better than any others, and between them they account for almost all gamma irradiators in use.

Caesium-137 has a half-life of 30.1 years. It decays by beta emission, 94.7 % of the time to a metastable state of barium-137 which then emits a single gamma ray at 662 keV, and 5.3 % of the time directly to the barium-137 ground state with a higher-energy beta and no gamma. The metastable state lives 2.55 minutes, far too short to matter, so the source behaves as though it emits the 662 keV photon directly — in 85.1 % of all decays, the remainder of the metastable decays proceeding by internal conversion instead. A single clean line and a thirty-year half-life make caesium-137 the reference source for dosimetry: its output falls by only about 2 % per year, so a calibration remains usable for a long time.

Cobalt-60 has a half-life of 5.27 years. It decays by beta emission with a 317 keV endpoint to an excited nickel-60 nucleus, which then relaxes through two successive gamma emissions of 1.17 MeV and 1.33 MeV, essentially one of each per decay. The mean photon energy of 1.25 MeV is roughly twice that of caesium-137, which makes cobalt-60 the choice when a thick or dense test article must be penetrated uniformly. The price is the shorter half-life: activity falls about 12 % per year, so exposure times must be recalculated continually and the source replaced every decade or so.

Why these two and not others. A useful gamma source needs four properties at once: a half-life long enough that the source does not need constant replacement but short enough that a practical mass is intensely active; a gamma energy high enough to penetrate a test article uniformly; a simple decay scheme, so the delivered spectrum is known; and availability in quantity. Caesium-137 and cobalt-60 are both fission or activation products available as by-products of the nuclear fuel cycle, which supplies the fourth. The properties that make them good sources also make them difficult to dispose of — a thirty-year half-life means a retired caesium source remains significantly active for centuries, and disposal is a recognised cost of operating a test facility.

9 · Dose and its units

Everything so far describes the source. What a device or a person experiences is the energy the radiation actually deposits in them, and that is absorbed dose: energy deposited per unit mass of the absorbing material. Dose is the quantity that total-ionising-dose testing is specified in, and every hardness figure quoted for a space part is a dose.

D = Eabsorbedm
(4.5)
D absorbed dose (Gy = J/kg; 1 rad = 0.01 Gy) · Eabsorbed energy deposited in the material (J) · m mass of the material absorbing it (kg). Dose is always dose in a stated material — rad(Si) and rad(SiO₂) differ for the same photon field, because the two materials absorb differently. Formula sheet §6

Equal doses of different radiation species do not do equal biological damage, because the damage depends on how densely the energy is deposited along a track rather than on the total. An alpha particle delivers its energy within a few tens of micrometres, concentrating the damage; a gamma ray spreads the same energy over centimetres. The quality factor Q accounts for this, and multiplying absorbed dose by it gives equivalent dose, measured in sieverts or in rem.

The unit names. The gray (Gy) is one joule per kilogram of absorbed dose. The rad is the older unit, one hundredth of a gray. The sievert (Sv) is the corresponding unit of equivalent dose, and the rem is one hundredth of a sievert. Rem abbreviates Roentgen Equivalent Man; Deck 3 renders it "Roentgen Equivalent Mammal". The becquerel describes the source, the gray describes what the target absorbs, and the sievert describes the biological consequence — three different quantities that are easy to conflate because all three are quoted for the same experiment.

10 · The terrestrial background

Radiation is not an exclusively space phenomenon, and the terrestrial background provides the scale against which space environments should be read. The average annual effective dose to a member of the United States population is about 620 mrem, or 6.2 mSv, split roughly equally between natural and medical sources. The largest single contributor is radon, an inert radioactive gas that seeps from uranium-bearing rock and is inhaled.

Two features of this budget carry directly into the space discussion. The cosmic-ray contribution of 20–25 mrem per year at sea level is what survives after the atmosphere has absorbed the primary flux — the equivalent of about ten metres of water overhead — and it rises with altitude, reaching roughly a hundred times the sea-level rate at aircraft cruising altitude and far more beyond the atmosphere entirely. Deck 3 puts Denver, at 1,600 m, at 75–100 mrem per year — a figure that covers its elevated natural exposure as a whole; the cosmic-ray component alone is about 50 mrem, roughly double the sea-level 20–25. The atmosphere and the geomagnetic field are the two shields a spacecraft leaves behind, and M06 quantifies what each was providing.

The second feature is the comparison of scale. A part qualified to 100 krad(Si) tolerates 108 mrem, some 160,000 times the annual dose to a person. The two numbers describe different absorbers and different kinds of damage — broken chemical bonds in tissue against trapped charge in an oxide — and they are not interchangeable, but the ratio makes the point that the radiation levels relevant to electronics qualification are far above any terrestrial human exposure.

A sealed caesium-137 source was calibrated at 5.0 Ci in 1996. What is its activity in 2026, and what would the same calculation give for a cobalt-60 source of the same initial activity?

Thirty years is one caesium-137 half-life, so the activity is 5.0 / 2 = 2.5 Ci, or 9.3 × 1010 Bq. No exponential arithmetic is needed when the elapsed time is a whole number of half-lives.

For cobalt-60 the same thirty years is 30 / 5.27 = 5.69 half-lives. Applying equations (4.2) and (4.3), the remaining fraction is 2−5.69 = e−0.693 × 5.69 = 0.0194, giving 0.097 Ci — about 2 % of the original. A cobalt cell is a consumable on the scale of a decade; a caesium cell is not. That difference, and not the photon energy, is usually what decides which source a facility installs.

Summary

  • Radiation divides into species with rest mass (alpha, beta, proton, heavy ion, neutron) and species without (X-ray, gamma). Within the massive group, electric charge decides whether the particle interacts continuously with atomic electrons or only on a near-direct nuclear hit.
  • Alpha particles are helium nuclei, +2e, 3–7 MeV in the course's range (4–9 MeV across all emitters), monoenergetic per isotope, and stopped within tens of micrometres of solid. Beta particles are nuclear-origin electrons with a continuous spectrum up to an endpoint, because the decay energy is shared with an antineutrino.
  • Heavy ions deposit energy as Z², so an iron nucleus deposits ~676 times as densely as a proton at the same velocity. They are rare and disproportionately destructive.
  • The neutron carries no charge, ignores the electron cloud, and is slowed only by nuclear collisions. Energy transfer per collision is 4Mm/(M+m)², maximised at M = m, which is why hydrogen-rich materials moderate neutrons and lead does not.
  • A neutron meets no Coulomb barrier, so it is captured at any energy and leaves a compound nucleus excited by the released binding energy. Uranium-235 capture gives 6.55 MeV against a 5.67 MeV fission barrier and splits with a slow neutron; uranium-238 capture gives 4.81 MeV against 6.45 MeV, and fission becomes appreciable only at 1.2–1.5 MeV. In silicon a neutron recoils nuclei elastically, drives (n,α) above 2.75 MeV and (n,p) above 4.0 MeV, and boron-10 captures thermal neutrons to give a 1.47 MeV alpha particle and a 0.84 MeV lithium ion.
  • X-rays and gamma rays are the same object named by origin: gamma from nuclear transitions and therefore discrete lines, X-rays from electron processes and therefore a continuum.
  • N = N0e−λt with λ = ln2/t1/2. Activity A = λN in becquerel; 1 Ci = 3.7 × 1010 Bq. Compute N from the mass of the isotope, not of the element.
  • Caesium-137: 30.1 y, one gamma line at 662 keV in 85.1 % of decays. Cobalt-60: 5.27 y, two lines at 1.17 and 1.33 MeV, mean 1.25 MeV. Caesium for stable calibration, cobalt for penetrating thick articles.
  • Absorbed dose is energy per unit mass — gray or rad — and is always stated for a named material. Equivalent dose multiplies it by a quality factor and applies to biological risk only; device hardness is quoted as absorbed dose.
  • US average background is ~620 mrem/year, half of it medical, with radon the largest natural term. Cosmic-ray dose rises steeply with altitude because the atmosphere is the shield being removed.
