Module 13

SRIM and TRIM

The course's simulation tool: what a binary-collision Monte Carlo calculation computes, how an ion and a layered target are specified, how each output converts to range, ionization, displacement and sputtering, and which physics the program leaves out.

Prerequisites M12, M11, M08 Read time ~45 min Sources Deck 7; Lecture 7; SRIM manual (srim.org); NIST PSTAR; Ziegler et al. (2010); Stoller et al. (2013)

M12 defined stopping power, range, straggling and linear energy transfer for an average particle, and closed by describing the binary-collision Monte Carlo method that turns those averages into distributions. This module covers the program the course uses to perform that calculation: the SRIM package and its transport engine, TRIM. Given an ion species, an energy and a stack of target layers, TRIM follows thousands of individual ions and the target atoms they set in motion, and reports where the ions come to rest and, depth by depth, how their energy divides between target electrons, displaced atoms and lattice vibration.

A calculation of this kind is needed because the quantities that decide a device's response are not the averages that stopping-power tables list. A device is a stack of layers that differ in composition and density, and what matters is where in that stack the ions stop, where their ionization concentrates and where vacancies form. Part of each ion's energy is also carried away from its own path by the target atoms it strikes, which then ionize and displace atoms of their own. No closed-form expression gives these depth distributions for a layered target; a calculation that follows individual histories and accumulates statistics over many of them does.

Every output of the program is a quantity already defined in the course. The ion-range distribution is the projected range and straggling of M12 §5. The ionization profile, divided by the electron–hole pair creation energy of M08, gives the charge liberated per ion, and summed over a fluence it gives the total ionizing dose of M14. The vacancy profile is the displacement damage of M11. The module treats the physics the program implements first, then the specification of an ion and a target, then each output and the conversions that make it a device quantity, and finally the program's accuracy, its limits, and the practical matters of installing it and saving its results.

1 · SRIM and TRIM

SRIM names a package of programs; TRIM names one program inside it. The distinction matters because the two answer different questions — one returns tabulated averages for a single material in under a second, the other follows ions through a layered target and returns distributions — and the course uses the second.

SRIM, the Stopping and Range of Ions in Matter, is a group of programs written by J. F. Ziegler, J. P. Biersack and M. D. Ziegler that calculates the stopping and range of ions from 10 eV to 2 GeV per nucleon in any element or compound. Its stopping powers are not computed from first principles: they are fits to a compilation of measured stopping values, which the 2010 release extended to more than 28,000 data points. The package began in 1983 as a DOS program and moved to Windows in 1989. The current release is SRIM-2013; the program is free for non-commercial use. The authors' published description of the 2010 revision (Ziegler, Ziegler and Biersack, 2010) states that, besides adding data, it improved the corrections for compounds and the stopping of heavy ions. The version notes on srim.org describe SRIM-2010 differently, as leaving the basic calculations of SRIM-2003 unchanged while fixing reported bugs and adding features.

The SRIM main menu offers two routes into the calculation. The Stopping/Range Tables module takes one ion and one homogeneous material and tabulates, against ion energy, the electronic and nuclear stopping powers, the projected range, and the longitudinal and lateral straggling. These are the quantities of M12 equations (12.2), (12.6) and (12.7), evaluated with SRIM's stopping powers, and the table appears in under a second. The TRIM Calculation route opens TRIM, the Transport of Ions in Matter, which accepts targets of up to eight layers built from compound materials, follows ions one at a time, and calculates both the final three-dimensional distribution of the ions and the energy-loss phenomena along their paths: target damage, sputtering, ionization and phonon production. The computing time per ion grows with the number of collisions that must be followed, so heavier ions, higher energies and the more detailed damage options all lengthen a run, and the accuracy of every result is set by the number of ions followed.

Monte Carlo calculation

A Monte Carlo calculation estimates an average by simulating many independent random histories, each built from steps whose outcomes are drawn from known probability distributions, and averaging over them. In TRIM one history is one ion: the impact parameter of each collision is drawn at random, the collision's outcome follows from the interatomic potential, and the ion's position and energy after each step follow from the previous ones. The method is required because the quantity of interest — for example the probability that an ion stops at a given depth in a given layer — results from thousands of random collisions per ion, each depending on the outcome of the one before, and no analytic treatment follows that chain through several layers. Its cost is statistical: a result built from N histories carries a random error that falls only as 1/√N (§3).

Deck 7 p. 20 sets out the procedure the course follows, and the rest of the module keeps its order: specify the incident ion; specify the layers, first single-element materials such as silicon and then compounds such as SiO₂; run the TRIM calculation; save the data. The Stopping/Range Tables remain useful beside it as a fast independent check, because a TRIM run on a single homogeneous layer must return a mean stopping depth close to the tabulated projected range for the same ion and energy.

2 · The physics inside TRIM

TRIM implements the binary-collision Monte Carlo method of M12 §8 with four specific choices: a rule for selecting collisions, an interatomic potential for computing them, an electronic stopping model for the path between them, and a set of assumptions about the target. Each choice fixes what the output can contain, and the limits of §8 follow directly from them.

The binary collision approximation treats the ion's interaction with the target as a sequence of isolated two-body elastic collisions, each with a single target atom, separated by straight segments of free flight along which the ion loses energy continuously to target electrons. The approximation holds while the moving atom's kinetic energy is far above the few-electronvolt energies that bind atoms in a solid, because each collision is then brief and involves only the nearest atom. TRIM therefore ends each trajectory once the moving atom's energy falls to a low cut-off: in the full-cascade mode a recoil is followed until its energy drops below the lowest displacement energy among the target atoms.

To save computation, TRIM does not calculate a collision in every atomic layer. It uses statistical algorithms that let the ion jump between calculated collisions and averages the collision results over the intervening path, a scheme called the free-flight path. The alternative Monolayer Collision Steps option calculates a collision in every monolayer, whose spacing TRIM takes as N−1/3 for a target of N atoms per cm³ — 2.7 Å in silicon. It returns the same averaged quantities and takes far longer. It is used where every individual collision must be recorded, and it underlies the option for calculating surface sputtering, because whether an atom is sputtered is decided in the outermost monolayers.

Each collision is computed from a screened Coulomb potential. Two nuclei repel as bare point charges when they are much closer together than the radius of their inner electron shells, and hardly at all when they are far apart, because each nucleus is then surrounded by its own neutralising electron cloud. TRIM uses the universal potential of Ziegler, Biersack and Littmark (ZBL), which multiplies the bare Coulomb interaction by a single screening function of the separation measured in a screening length that depends on both atomic numbers.

V(r) = Z1Z2e²4πε0 r Φ(raU),   aU = 0.8854 a0Z10.23 + Z20.23
(13.1)
V potential energy of the ion–atom pair (eV) · r separation of the two nuclei (Å) · Z1, Z2 atomic numbers of the ion and the target atom · e²/4πε0 = 14.40 eV·Å · Φ the universal screening function (dimensionless), equal to 1 at r = 0 and falling to 0 at large r, a sum of four exponentials fitted to calculated potentials for many ion–atom pairs · aU universal screening length (Å) · a0 = 0.529 Å, the Bohr radius. For hydrogen on silicon aU = 0.165 Å; for oxygen on silicon 0.136 Å. Describes only the elastic, nuclear-stopping collisions of M12 §3; it carries no information about energy given to electrons. On the formula sheet as (13.1).

For each collision TRIM draws an impact parameter at random, obtains the scattering angle from the potential through a fast analytic approximation to the classical scattering integral (introduced by Biersack and Haggmark in 1980, the origin of TRIM's range algorithms), and from the angle obtains the energy transferred to the struck atom. The transfer follows from conservation of energy and momentum alone.

T = Tmax sin² θ2,   Tmax = 4M1M2(M1 + M2)² E
(13.2)
T kinetic energy given to the struck atom (eV) · E ion kinetic energy before the collision (eV) · θ scattering angle in the centre-of-mass frame (rad) · M1, M2 masses of ion and target atom (u; only the ratio enters). The head-on case θ = π gives Tmax, the result of M04 equation (4.1) applied to an ion. The mass factor 4M1M2/(M1 + M2)² is 0.134 for hydrogen on silicon and 0.925 for oxygen on silicon. Non-relativistic, elastic. On the formula sheet as (13.2).

Between collisions the ion loses energy to target electrons at a rate taken from SRIM's fitted electronic stopping powers for that ion, energy and material. For a heavy ion the charge state inside the target is represented by an effective charge: a velocity-dependent charge that allows for the electrons the ion carries and for screening by the target's own electrons, and that replaces the bare charge number in the scaling of M12 equation (12.8). This continuous loss is the quantity TRIM reports as ionization.

A compound target needs the stopping power of a mixture. The starting point is Bragg's rule, which treats each atom as stopping the ion independently of its neighbours, so that the stopping of the compound is the atom-fraction-weighted sum of the elemental values.

εc = Σi xi εi,   Sc = N εc
(13.3)
εc, εi stopping cross-section of the compound and of element i, the energy lost per atom per unit areal density (eV·cm²/atom) · xi atom fraction of element i (1/3 Si and 2/3 O for SiO₂) · Sc linear stopping power of the compound (eV/cm) · N atomic density of the compound (atoms/cm³). Equivalent to weighting mass stopping powers by mass fraction, the form M10 equation (10.9) uses for attenuation coefficients. Neglects chemical bonding; SRIM's compound correction, applied to the materials of its Compound Dictionary, adjusts the result by up to 20 %, most for light compounds such as hydrocarbons. On the formula sheet as (13.3).

Three assumptions about the target complete the model. The target is amorphous: atoms are placed at random within their cells rather than on a lattice. It is at zero dose: every ion enters a perfect target, unaffected by the damage or the implanted atoms left by earlier ions. It is at 0 K: nothing diffuses and no damage anneals. The zero-dose assumption is what makes the ion histories independent, as the Monte Carlo average requires; the amorphous and 0 K assumptions simplify the collision and defect models. Each is also a respect in which TRIM differs from a real device.

An amorphous target has no channels

In a crystal, the atoms along certain directions form rows with open space between them, and an ion entering nearly parallel to such a direction is steered along the channel by a succession of gentle collisions with the rows. A channeled ion undergoes few close collisions, loses energy mainly to electrons, and penetrates much deeper than the same ion entering in a random direction; this is why implantation into crystalline silicon depends on the wafer's orientation and tilt. Because TRIM places target atoms at random, no channel exists in its target, and no TRIM setting reproduces channeling or any other dependence on crystal orientation. A TRIM depth profile corresponds to a random-direction or amorphous target. Codes that track a crystal lattice are required for channeling.

3 · Setting up a calculation

A TRIM calculation is specified entirely in one window, TRIM Setup, opened from the TRIM Calculation button of the SRIM main menu. The window is divided into regions that are filled from top to bottom: the type of calculation, the incident ion, the layers of the target, the elements of each layer, the run parameters, and the optional output files. Figure 3 maps those regions; this section follows them in order, with the course's choice at each step and the reason for it. Composition and density, which carry most of the risk of a wrong result, have §4 to themselves.

Region 1, the type of calculation. The DAMAGE menu decides how the target atoms set in motion by the ion are treated. The course uses the default, Ion Distribution and Quick Calculation of Damage. In this mode TRIM follows every ion in full but does not follow the recoils; it estimates the displacements each recoil would produce from the Kinchin–Pease analytic count as modified by Sigmund and by Norgett, Robinson and Torrens, which is close to the latter authors' standard displacement count, M11 equation (11.1). The final distribution of the ions, their ionization energy loss, the energy they transfer to recoils, and the backscattered and transmitted ions all come out exactly as in the detailed mode, and the ion ranges are identical, because TRIM draws ions and recoils from separate random-number sequences. The alternative, Detailed Calculation with Full Damage Cascades, follows every recoil until its energy falls below the lowest displacement energy among the target atoms, which multiplies the number of trajectories per ion. Sputtering (§5) needs one of the options that follow recoils, and the SRIM manual names the Surface Sputtering option, which forces a collision in every monolayer, as essential for an accurate yield. The Basic Plots menu selects only the live display drawn during the run and does not change the calculation; the course keeps its default.

Region 2, the ion. The PT button opens a periodic table. Selecting hydrogen specifies a proton, and the atomic number and mass fill in automatically, the mass being that of the most abundant isotope rather than the natural atomic weight. The energy is entered in keV, with a default of 10 keV. The angle of incidence is measured from the surface normal: 0°, the default and the course's choice, is normal incidence, and any angle up to 89.9° tilts the beam within the plane of the depth axis and one lateral axis.

Regions 3 and 4, the target. Each row of the layer table is one layer, ordered from the surface inward, with a name, a width and a density. The width defaults to ångströms, and a unit menu offers micrometres, millimetres, metres and kilometres; the course works in micrometres. Adding a layer creates an empty row and an empty element table, and each element must then be added with Add New Element to Layer and the PT button; a layer holds up to twelve elements. Beside each element the table lists three energies that govern damage: the displacement energy Ed needed to move an atom off its site, the lattice binding energy Elatt that a displaced atom loses in leaving its site, and the surface binding energy Esurf that an atom must overcome to escape the surface. TRIM's defaults for silicon are 15, 2 and 4.7 eV.

Region 5, the run parameters. Total Number of Ions sets how many ion histories are computed; its default of 99999 is meant to be interrupted once the statistics are adequate. AutoSave at Ion #, 10000 by default, sets how often TRIM saves its state so that an interrupted run can resume. The Random Number Seed has a fixed default, so identical inputs reproduce identical ions until the seed is changed. Plotting Window Depths sets the depth range over which TRIM accumulates its depth distributions, in 100 equal bins; the default covers the whole target. Region 6 lists files that record every individual ion or collision; ticking them here records from the first ion (§9). Save Input & Run TRIM writes the setup to a text file, TRIM.IN, and starts the calculation.

The number of ions sets the statistical error of every output, and the course trades accuracy for time: it reduces Total Number of Ions to 500 for runs made during the lecture. The cost of that choice follows from the statistics of independent samples.

σR̄ = σR√N,   δnn = 1√n
(13.4)
σR̄ standard error of the mean stopping depth (Å) · σR straggling, the standard deviation of individual stopping depths (Å) · N number of ions followed · n number of events recorded in one depth bin · δn/n relative random error of that bin's height. The first form is the standard error of a mean of N independent values; the second is Poisson counting error and holds for the ion-range histogram, where each ion adds one count. In cascade outputs such as vacancies one ion adds many correlated events, and the error must be estimated per ion, for example by repeating the run with a different seed. On the formula sheet as (13.4).

With 500 ions the mean stopping depth is known to σR/22, about 4.5 % of the straggling, which is ample. A depth histogram is another matter: 500 stopping positions spread evenly over 100 bins would leave five per bin and a bin-to-bin scatter of about 45 %. Where the stopping positions concentrate in a few bins, those bins hold more ions and scatter less, and the sparsely populated tails scatter more. Averages converge quickly and depth profiles slowly. The SRIM tutorial's guideline is that 1000 ions typically give better than 10 % accuracy, and repeating a run with a different random seed shows directly how much of a profile's structure is noise.

Why the quick damage option is also the recommended one

A systematic comparison by Stoller and co-workers (2013) found that the vacancy count from a full-cascade run is about twice the Kinchin–Pease count — ratios of 2.06 to 2.43 across the cases they tested — even though the two modes agree to within 5–10 % on the damage energy, the energy that goes into atomic collisions. The full-cascade vacancy file is therefore not a reliable measure of displacements per atom. They recommend the quick Kinchin–Pease option wherever ion damage is to be compared with neutron damage or with the standard displacement count, with the lattice binding energy set to zero and the displacements computed from the damage energy through the standard formula, M11 equation (11.1), rather than read from the vacancy file. The course's default is the option the literature supports.

4 · Compounds, densities and gases

A layer is defined by what it is made of and how densely its atoms are packed, and TRIM takes both from the user. The composition fixes the stopping through equation (13.3) and decides which atoms the ion can strike; the density converts every mass-based quantity into a length. An error in either one propagates into every output, which is why this is the step that most often decides whether a result is right.

The Atom Stoich or % column takes atom fractions, and TRIM normalises them, so Si 1 with O 2, Si 100 with O 200, and Si 33 with O 67 all specify the same SiO₂ layer. A composition quoted by weight must be converted to atom fractions before it is entered. The course builds SiO₂, the composite example Deck 7 p. 20 names, as Lecture 7 demonstrated: add silicon, add oxygen with a second element row, and change oxygen's stoichiometry from 1 to 2.

TRIM then proposes a density for the layer, computed from the densities of its elements weighted by their stoichiometry. For a single element in its normal solid form the proposal is SRIM's tabulated density for that element and is adequate: silicon's, 2.32 g/cm³, lies within 0.3 % of the measured 2.329 g/cm³ and needs no change. For a compound the proposal is not the compound's density. Each elemental density describes that element in its own standard condensed state, a compound packs its atoms in a different structure, and oxygen and nitrogen have no solid state at room temperature at all, so no weighted average of elemental densities has any reason to reproduce the density of an oxide. The proposed value must be replaced by a measured one: 2.20 g/cm³ for amorphous SiO₂, the form of a thermally grown gate or field oxide.

x = Rmρ
(13.5)
x a depth or range as a length (cm) · Rm the same quantity as a mass per unit area (g/cm²), which depends on composition but not on density · ρ layer density (g/cm³). Every TRIM depth distribution scales this way, so a density wrong by some fraction makes every computed depth in that layer wrong by the same fraction in the opposite direction. Holds while the stopping per atom is independent of packing, which is true for solids; the phase difference between a gas and a solid is handled separately by the Gas option. On the formula sheet as (13.5).

The Compound Dictionary button offers an alternative route: a list of more than 300 compounds, each stored with its stoichiometry and a typical density. About 150 of them, marked with a star, also carry the chemical-bonding information from which SRIM computes a stopping correction; the correction concerns bonds of the light elements H, C, N, O, F, S and Cl. Choosing an entry fills the element table and the density, and the correction where one exists. A layer built element by element, as the course builds SiO₂, uses Bragg's rule with no bonding correction — the Compound Corr column reads 1. The correction is largest for light compounds such as hydrocarbons. The density still needs checking on either route.

A gas needs one more setting. Ticking the Gas box for a layer makes TRIM use gas-phase stopping powers for hydrogen, helium, nitrogen, oxygen, neon, argon, krypton and xenon; any other element in a gas layer is still treated as a solid. The phase matters because, compared at the same number of atoms per cm² traversed, an ion loses less energy in a solid than in the corresponding gas. Above 200 keV per nucleon the difference is below 10 %; below that it can approach a factor of two. TRIM applies the gas correction for standard temperature and pressure. The density column behaves differently between versions. The SRIM-2003-era tutorial ticks the box and then has the user overwrite a density that SRIM computed assuming a solid; in SRIM-2013, as the Lecture 7 demonstration showed, ticking the box replaces the solid-phase proposal with a gas-phase estimate of order 10⁻³ g/cm³. That estimate is built from the elements, not measured for the actual mixture, so on either version the entry is checked and set to the measured value: 1.205×10⁻³ g/cm³ for dry air near sea level. Oxygen bound in a solid, as in SiO₂, belongs in a layer declared solid.

The composition of air follows from the same rule of atom fractions. Dry air is 75.5 % nitrogen, 23.2 % oxygen and 1.3 % argon by mass, which converts to 78.4 % nitrogen, 21.1 % oxygen and 0.47 % argon by atom. The course's two-element air, nitrogen and oxygen in the atom ratio 80 : 20, is within two percentage points of this; argon, which is on TRIM's list of gases, is the refinement. Deck 7 p. 25 asks how to simulate air, and the answer assembles the pieces of this section: one layer, nitrogen and oxygen (and argon) entered as atom fractions, the Gas box ticked, the density typed in, and a width in millimetres rather than micrometres.

Deck 7 p. 24: which phase of Ga₂O₃

Deck 7 p. 24 specifies 0.5 µm of Ga₂O₃ with ρ = 6.44 g/cm³ on 1 µm of GaAs with ρ = 5.32 g/cm³. The GaAs value is the standard one. The Ga₂O₃ value is a real density, but of the α (corundum-structure) phase: the density calculated from α-Ga₂O₃'s lattice constants is 6.47 g/cm³. The thermodynamically stable phase, and the one grown as bulk substrates for gallium-oxide power devices, is monoclinic β-Ga₂O₃, whose lattice constants give 5.96 g/cm³ and whose tabulated density is 5.88 g/cm³. Use 6.44 to reproduce the course's exercise. For a β-Ga₂O₃ layer, equation (13.5) places every computed depth in that layer 8–10 % deeper than the 6.44 g/cm³ run does: 8 % with the lattice-constant density, 9.5 % with the tabulated one.

5 · Reading the outputs

Once a run starts, the TRIM window lists its outputs in a table of distributions, each with a Plot button that opens a graph and a File button that writes the same data as text. Plots update after every ion while the calculation continues, and every average is taken over all ions computed so far; open plots slow the calculation. A second panel, Calculation Parameters, carries the energy-loss table and the sputtering yield. Every depth distribution is normalised per ion and per unit depth and is accumulated in the 100 bins of the plotting window. This section takes the outputs in turn and gives, for each, the conversion that turns it into a device quantity.

Ion ranges

The Ion Distribution plot, titled ION RANGES, is the histogram of the depths at which the ions came to rest. TRIM reports its mean, which is the projected range, its standard deviation, which is the longitudinal straggling, and its skewness and kurtosis — the quantities of M12 §5, now obtained as moments of a computed distribution for the actual layered target. The Lateral Range plot gives the spread across the beam direction. The ordinate is in (atoms/cm³)/(atoms/cm²), a concentration per unit fluence, so multiplying by the fluence gives the concentration of implanted atoms directly.

n(x) = Φ · h(x)
(13.6)
n concentration of implanted ions at depth x (atoms/cm³) · Φ fluence, ions per unit area (cm⁻²) · h TRIM's range distribution ((atoms/cm³)/(atoms/cm²), which is cm⁻¹). The text file RANGE.txt lists the same distribution in atoms/(Å·ion); multiply by 10⁸ Å/cm to obtain h in cm⁻¹. The integral of h over depth is the fraction of ions that stop in the target. Valid only at fluences low enough that the implanted atoms do not change the target, the zero-dose assumption of §2. On the formula sheet as (13.6).

Ionization

The Ionization plot is the energy the ions and recoils give to target electrons per unit depth, averaged per ion, in eV/(Å·ion), shown as two curves: ionization by the ions and ionization by the recoils. Ionization is TRIM's name for all electronic energy loss, excitation included. It is the output that connects TRIM to electrical effects, because the energy given to electrons is what creates electron–hole pairs.

The ordinate is a density — energy per unit depth — not an energy. The energy an ion deposits in a layer is the area under the curve across that layer, which TRIM's 100-bin depth grid turns into a sum. The same holds for every depth plot on the page: the phonon plot integrates to energy per ion and the vacancy plot to vacancies per ion.

Elayer = ∫x1x2 I(x) dx ≈ Σk Ik Δx
(13.7)
Elayer energy given to electrons within the layer, per ion (eV) · I ionization density, the sum of the ion and recoil curves (eV/(Å·ion)) · x1, x2 depths of the layer's boundaries (Å) · Ik value in depth bin k · Δx bin width, one hundredth of the plotting window (Å). The units reduce as eV/(Å·ion) × Å = eV per ion. On the formula sheet as (13.7).

Three conversions turn Elayer into device quantities. Dividing by the electron–hole pair creation energy of M08 §5 gives the number of pairs, and multiplying by the elementary charge gives the charge liberated per ion.

Neh = ElayerEpair,   Q = q Neh
(13.8)
Neh electron–hole pairs generated per ion in the layer · Elayer from equation (13.7) (eV) · Epair mean pair creation energy (eV): 3.6 eV in silicon, about 17 eV in SiO₂ · Q charge liberated per ion (C) · q = 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ C. Counts pairs generated; how many survive recombination and are collected is outside TRIM (§8). The course's own order-of-magnitude arithmetic divides by 99.42 eV instead; as M08 explains, 3.6 eV is the value that matches a SRIM output or a measurement. On the formula sheet as (13.8).

Near the surface, where every ion is still moving and nearly normal to it, the height of the ions' ionization curve is their electronic linear energy transfer. Deeper, it is a beam average per incident ion, which falls where ions have stopped or been deflected, not only where the stopping of each moving ion falls. In silicon, 1 eV/Å is 10 keV/µm, which is 0.0429 MeV·cm²/mg and liberates 0.445 fC/µm through M12 equation (12.5); equivalently, 96.4 MeV·cm²/mg liberates 1 pC/µm with the 2.33 g/cm³ that equation uses, or 96.5 with the 2.329 g/cm³ of §4. For a fluence rather than a single ion, the energy per ion becomes an absorbed dose, the quantity of M14.

D = Φ · Elayerρ · t
(13.9)
D absorbed dose in the layer's material (eV/g; divide by 6.242×10¹³ eV/g per rad, or by 6.242×10¹⁵ eV/g per gray) · Φ fluence (cm⁻²) · Elayer energy per ion from equation (13.7) (eV) · ρ layer density (g/cm³) · t layer thickness (cm). A dose must name its material — rad(SiO₂) for an oxide layer, rad(Si) for silicon. The layer average hides any variation in depth; for a thin sensitive region, integrate over that region alone. The same conversion from dose to pairs per unit volume, through the pair-generation constant g0, is developed in EEE 5416 M04. On the formula sheet as (13.9).
A beam of 100 keV protons strikes 0.5 µm of SiO₂ (2.20 g/cm³) on silicon at normal incidence. The NIST PSTAR proton range table, applied along a straight path, shows each proton leaving the oxide with about 43 keV, and electronic stopping carries more than 99 % of the loss at these energies. Estimate the electron–hole pairs each proton generates in the oxide, and the oxide dose from a fluence of 10¹⁰ protons/cm².

The energy given to oxide electrons is about 100 − 43 = 57 keV per proton, which is what integrating TRIM's ionization curve across the oxide, equation (13.7), should approximately return. At 17 eV per pair in SiO₂, equation (13.8) gives 5.7×10⁴ / 17 ≈ 3.4×10³ pairs per proton.

Equation (13.9): D = 10¹⁰ × 5.7×10⁴ eV / (2.20 g/cm³ × 5×10⁻⁵ cm) = 5.2×10¹⁸ eV/g, and dividing by 6.242×10¹³ eV/g per rad gives about 83 krad(SiO₂). The straight-path estimate slightly underestimates the energy deposited, because a real proton's path through the layer is longer than the layer's thickness — the detour of M12 equation (12.7) — and that difference is one of the things the TRIM run supplies.

Phonons

The Phonons plot gives the energy per unit depth that ends as lattice vibration, that is, as heat, again split between the ions and the recoils, in the same eV/(Å·ion) units and integrated in the same way. Phonon energy arises wherever energy reaches the lattice without displacing an atom: collisions that transfer less than Ed, and the final energy of every moving atom that comes to rest. For a heavy ion more than 90 % of the phonon energy is produced by the recoils rather than by the ion, because recoils are slow and spend their energy in many low-energy collisions.

Damage events

The Damage Events menu opens the Collision Events plot, which gives three distributions against depth in vacancies/(Å·ion): total displacements, total vacancies, and replacement collisions, each also separated by target element. TRIM books each collision by the rule of Figure 6. A displacement occurs whenever the struck atom receives more than its displacement energy. If the projectile also keeps more than the displacement energy, both move on and the struck atom's site is left as a vacancy. If the projectile is left with less, and is of the same element as the atom it struck, it drops into the vacated site: a replacement collision, which moves an atom but leaves no vacancy.

Ndisp = Nvac + Nrepl,   Nvac = Nint + Nout
(13.10)
Ndisp displacements · Nvac vacancies · Nrepl replacement collisions · Nint atoms that come to rest as interstitials · Nout atoms that leave the target volume through a surface. All per ion; the plotted versions are per ion per Å. To obtain vacancies per cm³, multiply the plotted value by 10⁸ Å/cm and by the fluence; dividing by the atomic density (4.99×10²² atoms/cm³ in silicon) expresses the result per atom. For a standard displacements-per-atom figure the damage-energy route of §3 is preferred to the vacancy count. The counts are of displacements made at 0 K with no recombination, not of defects that survive (§8). On the formula sheet as (13.10).

In the quick mode the recoils are not followed, and the vacancy count comes instead from the modified Kinchin–Pease estimate of §3, close to M11 equation (11.1), applied to the energy each recoil receives. Either way, the displacement energy entered in the element table sets the count.

TRIM's silicon threshold is 15 eV, not 21 eV

TRIM's default displacement energy for silicon is 15 eV, the typical value the SRIM manual gives for semiconductors. M11 and the formula sheet use about 21 eV; published values for silicon span a wide range. For recoils well above threshold the modified Kinchin–Pease count is inversely proportional to Ed, so a run with the default returns about 21/15 = 1.4 times as many vacancies as the same run with 21 eV. The factor is somewhat larger when many recoils receive energies near the threshold, because a recoil given between 15 and 21 eV is displaced only under the lower threshold. Neither value is a TRIM error; the threshold is an input, and a vacancy count means nothing without the threshold that produced it.

The energy-loss table

The % Energy Loss table in the Calculation Parameters panel summarises where the incident energy went. Its two columns are the carriers, Ions and Recoils; its three rows are the channels, Ionization, Vacancies and Phonons. Each cell is the percentage of the incident ions' energy that the carrier dissipated in that channel. The Vacancies row books the energy spent in creating vacancies, a few electronvolts each, so it is always small. The six cells sum to 100 % less the energy carried out of the target by backscattered, transmitted and sputtered atoms. The Recoils column exists only because the ion transferred energy to target atoms; its size relative to the Ions column is the clearest single measure of how much of the ion's energy went into nuclear collisions, and §6 compares it for a light and a heavy ion.

Sputtering

Sputtering is the ejection of target atoms through the surface by the collision cascade an ion sets off. A recoil moving back toward the surface is sputtered if, as it crosses the surface plane, its energy resolved normal to the surface exceeds the surface binding energy Esurf — 4.7 eV for silicon by default, a value for which the heat of sublimation is usually a good estimate. The quantity TRIM reports is the yield.

Requires an option that follows recoils, because the quick mode does not follow them and only recoils are sputtered: Surface Sputtering or Monolayer Collision Steps, which the SRIM manual calls essential for accurate yields, or Full Damage Cascades. Because only the outermost atomic layers contribute, a thin target suffices. For ions heavier than 20 u the SRIM manual suggests 40–50 Å and SRIM Tutorial #2 suggests 300 Å (for light ions such as helium, 300–500 Å and up to 1000 Å respectively), and both advise increasing the thickness until the yield stops changing. The reference value against which a silicon result can be judged is about 0.87 atoms per ion for 1 keV argon at normal incidence, from the semi-empirical fit of Seah and co-workers to measured yields, which reproduces the data to about 10 %. TRIM's yields depend on the surface binding energy; the SRIM tutorial rates them accurate to about 30 % with respect to it, and in the tutorial's example of xenon on silicon even reducing Esurf to zero only doubled the calculated yield.

6 · Light ion and heavy ion

Deck 7 pp. 21 and 22 run the same kinds of target with a proton and with an oxygen ion, and the comparison isolates what the ion's mass and charge do to every output. Nothing in it is new physics. The velocity at a given energy sets the electronic stopping, as M12 §2 established, and the mass and charge set the nuclear stopping, as M12 §3 established; the partition of the energy between the channels of §5 follows from the balance between the two.

At equal kinetic energy, velocity scales as the square root of energy per unit mass, so a 100 keV oxygen ion, at 16 u, moves four times more slowly than a 100 keV proton: it carries 6.25 keV per nucleon against the proton's 100 keV. The proton sits near the 55 keV maximum of its electronic stopping in silicon. The oxygen ion sits far below the velocity at which it would be stripped of its electrons, in the low-velocity regime where electronic stopping is proportional to velocity and the effective charge is small. Its nuclear stopping, by contrast, is large on both counts that equations (13.1) and (13.2) identify: the potential carries the product Z1Z2, eight times larger for oxygen than for hydrogen, and the mass factor lets oxygen give a silicon atom up to 0.925 of its energy in one collision, against 0.134 for a proton. The Lindhard reduced energy combines these dependences into a single number.

ε = aU M2 EZ1Z2 (e²4πε0) (M1 + M2) = 32.53 M2 EkeVZ1Z2 (M1 + M2)(Z10.23 + Z20.23)
(13.12)
ε reduced energy (dimensionless) · aU screening length of equation (13.1) (Å) · E ion energy (eV in the first form; keV, EkeV, in the second) · M1, M2 masses of ion and target atom (u) · Z1, Z2 atomic numbers · e²/4πε0 = 14.40 eV·Å. The ratio of the collision energy to the Coulomb energy at the screening distance: in reduced units the nuclear stopping of every ion–target pair follows one universal curve of ε, and the share of the loss that goes to nuclear collisions grows as ε falls. In silicon at 100 keV, ε ≈ 79 for a proton and ε ≈ 5.4 for an oxygen ion. On the formula sheet as (13.12).

The consequences appear in every output. The oxygen ion's range is a small fraction of the proton's: the deck sizes its oxygen targets at 0.5 µm against 1.5 µm for hydrogen, where the 100 keV proton's projected range in silicon is 0.84 µm. Its track is short and branched rather than long and straight, because each strong nuclear collision both deflects it and launches an energetic recoil that cascades in turn (Figure 8). A projectile lighter than the target atom, as both hydrogen and oxygen are relative to silicon, can be turned back through the surface by a large-angle collision, and TRIM records such ions as backscattered; large-angle collisions are far more frequent for the slow heavy ion. Each history also takes longer to compute, because there are more collisions and more recoils to follow.

The energy-loss table quantifies the shift. For a proton, the PSTAR tables put nuclear stopping in silicon at 0.18 % of the total at 100 keV and 0.29 % at 50 keV; integrated over the whole slowing-down, nuclear collisions take about 1 % of a 100 keV proton's energy and at most 3 % of a 50 keV proton's, the uncertainty being the final keV, below the table's lowest energy. At least 97 % therefore goes directly to ionization, and the Recoils column of the table is nearly empty. For a heavy ion the SRIM tutorials document a full-cascade run of 190 keV phosphorus in silicon: the ion itself deposits 44 % of the incident energy, almost all as ionization, and passes 56 % to recoils, which spend 24 % on ionization, 3 % on vacancies and 29 % on phonons, producing about 2300 vacancies per ion. Phosphorus at 190 keV has ε ≈ 3.8, not far from the 5.4 of 100 keV oxygen, so an oxygen run on silicon is expected to show the same character in somewhat milder form, since ε alone does not fix the split: a large Recoils column, a phonon share of tens of percent, and ionization far below the proton's 97 %. The exact partition is what the run supplies.

Why recoils heat the lattice instead of ionizing it

Target electrons take energy most efficiently from particles moving at speeds comparable to their own. A recoiling silicon atom is launched with a fraction of the ion's energy and nearly thirty times a proton's mass, so it moves far more slowly than the ion that struck it, and its electronic stopping is correspondingly weak. It spends its energy instead in further nuclear collisions, most of them below the displacement threshold, and that energy ends as phonons. Transferring energy to recoils is therefore the same thing as diverting it from ionization to heat and damage, which is why the Recoils column of the energy-loss table tracks the ion's mass.

Protons, p. 21. The two energies lie on either side of the 55 keV maximum of proton electronic stopping in silicon, and neither produces a sharp Bragg peak: a 100 keV proton's stopping rises only slightly as it slows toward that maximum, and a 50 keV proton's falls only slowly at first while its path per unit depth lengthens as it scatters. Both ionization profiles are therefore nearly level near the surface and fall away across the stopping region. In the oxide-capped target the two energies behave differently: the 50 keV projected range in SiO₂, 0.57 µm, barely exceeds the 0.5 µm oxide, so the protons' stopping distribution straddles the oxide–silicon interface, while 100 keV protons cross the oxide with about 43 keV remaining and stop in the silicon. The oxide hardly changes the depth scale: the mass stopping powers of SiO₂ and silicon at 100 keV, 533 and 504 MeV·cm²/g, multiplied by their densities of 2.20 and 2.33 g/cm³, give nearly equal linear stopping powers. What it changes is the charge: energy deposited in the oxide produces a pair for every 17 eV rather than every 3.6 eV, nearly five times fewer carriers.

Oxygen, p. 22, repeats the comparison of §6 on thinner targets matched to its shorter range. Argon, p. 23, at 1 keV has a reduced energy of about 0.014 in silicon, where energy loss is overwhelmingly to nuclear collisions and the cascade stays close to the surface. The 0.02 µm target, 200 Å, is four to five times the manual's figure for ions heavier than 20 u and two-thirds of the tutorial's; the convergence test of §5 decides whether it suffices. This is the only setup whose output list includes the sputtering yield. In the oxide-capped version the yield is reported separately for oxygen and silicon, and the top layer is the one sputtered. Ga₂O₃ on GaAs, p. 24, is the only setup that prints its densities; as with SiO₂ on pp. 21–23, neither compound's density can be left to TRIM's proposal.

Air, p. 25. The 1 MeV proton's range of 2.38 cm in air at sea-level density places the end of its track inside the 30 mm layer, provided the layer carries air's density. The same configuration run at 250 MeV, near the maximum of the galactic cosmic-ray proton spectrum at solar minimum (about 270 MeV in the 2009 minimum; the population is introduced in M05 §7), shows the atmosphere acting as a shield. The PSTAR CSDA range is 42.9 g/cm², which is 356 m of air at sea-level density. The atmosphere, however, is not a slab of uniform density; its shielding is measured by its mass per unit area, 1033 g/cm² at sea level (M06 §10), equal to 8.6 km of sea-level air. A 250 MeV proton's electromagnetic range therefore ends within the top 4.2 % of the atmosphere's mass, far above the ground.

What the air calculation leaves out

TRIM includes no nuclear reactions, and the SRIM manual notes that above about 5 MeV per nucleon an ion may lose energy inelastically in ways the calculation omits. A 250 MeV proton is fifty times above that level. Crossing tens of g/cm² of air, such a proton can strike a nucleus and knock out protons and neutrons, which carry part of its energy deeper into the atmosphere than its own electromagnetic range. It lies below the 280 MeV threshold for creating a pion in a collision between two free nucleons; inside a nucleus the motion of the bound nucleons permits a small yield below that threshold, but the muons that dominate the charged-particle flux at sea level come from primaries of far higher energy. The TRIM run therefore describes where the primary proton would stop by ionization alone. The same limit applies to any TRIM run of high-energy protons into shielding, and to the PSTAR ranges, which are stopping-power integrals.

The air layer of Deck 7 p. 25 is entered with its density left at a solid-phase value of order 1 g/cm³. What does the run report for the 1 MeV proton, and which output reveals the error?

Equation (13.5): every depth scales as 1/ρ. The proton's mass range in air is 2.87×10⁻³ g/cm², which is 2.4 cm at 1.205×10⁻³ g/cm³ but about 29 µm at 1 g/cm³ — a factor of roughly 800 shorter. The ion-range plot shows every proton stopping in the first few hundredths of a millimetre of a 30 mm layer, and the ionization curve collapses into the same sliver. The Gas box's stopping correction changes only the stopping per atom, by less than 10 % at 1 MeV per nucleon, not the number of atoms per cm³; only the density entry sets the depth scale.

8 · Accuracy and limits

A TRIM result carries three kinds of error, and a fourth category of physics lies outside it altogether. Statistical error comes from the finite number of ions and shrinks with a longer run (§3). Data error comes from the stopping powers and from the user's densities, and is controlled by checking inputs. Model error comes from the damage physics and the idealised target, and bounds what a TRIM number can be taken to mean. The processes TRIM does not model at all determine which questions it cannot answer.

Stopping powers. The SRIM authors compare their stopping powers with the experimental literature in two slightly different tables. The accuracy table on srim.org ("Details of SRIM-2013") gives a mean deviation of 4.0 % for hydrogen ions (8300 data points, 87 % of them within 10 %), 3.9 % for helium, 4.8 % for lithium and 5.8 % for heavier ions from beryllium to uranium, 4.6 % overall and 4.0 % once points deviating by more than 25 % are set aside. Table 1 of the 2010 paper (Ziegler, Ziegler and Biersack), over about 28,100 points, gives 3.9 % for hydrogen (about 9000 points, 85 % within 10 %), 3.5 % for helium, 4.6 % for lithium, 5.6 % from beryllium to uranium, 4.3 % overall and 3.9 % without those points. The larger heavy-ion figure partly reflects greater scatter among the measurements themselves. Ranges, as integrals of the stopping, are limited by the same uncertainty, and the least well constrained case is a heavy ion at low velocity. Inputs add their own error: a density error transfers one for one into every depth (13.5), the gas–solid phase difference reaches a factor of nearly two below 200 keV per nucleon, and compound bonding corrections reach 20 %.

Damage. The damage outputs are far less certain than the ranges, for reasons that all follow from the assumptions of §2. A real cascade does not leave every displaced atom where TRIM puts it: many vacancies and interstitials meet and recombine within the cascade, and molecular-dynamics simulations in iron give about one-third of the standard displacement count. A real target is not at 0 K: for a low dose of ions in crystalline silicon the SRIM manual notes that more than 99 % of the damage may anneal at room temperature, the process of M11 §8. The count also depends on the damage option (about a factor of two, §3) and on the threshold energy (roughly inversely, §5). At high doses the opposite bias appears: damage lowers the displacement energy of the material around it, which TRIM's zero-dose target cannot represent, so accumulated damage may be underestimated.

What a TRIM vacancy count measures

A vacancy count from TRIM is a count of displacement events in a perfect, amorphous target at 0 K, computed with a stated threshold energy and damage option. It is not the density of defects that survive in a device. It is a useful relative measure when two runs share the same threshold and damage option; for an exposure in displacements per atom the recommended route is the damage energy of a quick-mode run converted through the standard formula (§3). The electrically active defect population of M11 is a small and temperature-dependent fraction of it.

What TRIM does not model. The program transports ions through static amorphous matter and records where their energy goes; every process outside that description is absent from its output.

9 · Installing SRIM and saving results

SRIM is a Windows program written in Visual Basic 5, and it depends on support files that current versions of Windows no longer include. Installing it is the main practical obstacle to using it, and its outputs persist only if they are saved deliberately. This section gives the installation route documented with the program and the files a run produces.

Installation. The SRIM-2013 download from srim.org comes in two forms, Standard (10 MB) and Pro (34 MB, which adds about 500 plots comparing experimental and theoretical stopping). Both are self-extracting archives named SRIM-2013.e, a name chosen so that firewalls that block executable downloads let the file through. The file is placed in a new folder, renamed to SRIM-2013.exe, and run; it extracts more than 500 files. On a computer that has never run SRIM, the document _SRIM Setup Message in that folder lists up to seven support files that may be missing: the Visual Basic 5 run-time (MSVBvm50.exe), five ActiveX controls (ComCtl32, ComDlg32, MSFlxGrd, RichTx32 and TabCtl32, each an .ocx file), and the LineDraw font. The SRIM-Setup folder supplies them. Its batch file, _SRIM-Setup (Right-Click).bat, run with Run as Administrator, installs the run-time, registers the five controls with the Windows regsvr32 utility from C:\Windows\SysWOW64 on 64-bit Windows, and opens the font for installation. If it fails, the same registration can be done by hand: copy the five controls into C:\Windows\SysWOW64 and, in a command prompt opened as Administrator, register each in turn, for example C:\Windows\SysWOW64\regsvr32.exe COMCTL32.OCX. A third route is to install SRIM-2008 in a separate folder first, since its installer sometimes supplies the missing files. Either version runs the setups of Deck 7.

Two further requirements govern a working installation. TRIM must run with the Windows regional format set to English (United States): where the decimal separator is a comma, the SRIM authors report erroneous results, and under languages that use 16-bit characters TRIM hangs. The program can also crash mid-run; the SRIM tutorial's advice is to return to TRIM Setup and press Save Input & Run TRIM again. SRIM has no native macOS or Linux version. It runs under the Wine compatibility layer, and nanoHUB hosts SRIM-2013 as a browser-based tool behind a free login.

Results are overwritten by the next run

Every text file TRIM writes goes to the SRIM Outputs folder under a fixed name, and the next run writes over it. The SRIM manual instructs renaming the output files after each calculation; the practical rule is to copy the whole folder to a folder named for the run as soon as it finishes. A plot's File button writes the data accumulated up to the moment it is pressed, so it is pressed after the run has reached the intended number of ions, not before.

Saving and resuming. The setup itself is written to TRIM.IN in the SRIM folder when the run starts; it is a text file that can be kept, edited and rerun. When TRIM is closed it asks whether to save the calculation, and a saved calculation, stored as SRIM Restore\TDATA.sav, is reloaded and continued with Resume saved TRIM calc. in the setup window; AutoSave writes the same state every N ions. The distributions are saved with the File buttons of §5, and the per-ion files are requested in region 6 of the setup window (Figure 3) so that they record from the first ion.

Summary

  • SRIM is the package; its Stopping/Range Tables give the averages of M12 for one ion in one material. TRIM, the program the course uses, follows ions one at a time through up to eight compound layers and returns every quantity as a distribution in depth.
  • TRIM computes binary collisions from the ZBL screened potential (13.1), with energy transfer set by the mass factor 4M1M2/(M1 + M2)² (13.2), and subtracts electronic loss from fitted stopping tables between collisions. Compounds follow Bragg's rule in atom fractions (13.3). The target is amorphous, at 0 K and at zero dose: no channeling, no annealing, no accumulation.
  • The course's setup: quick Kinchin–Pease damage, which gives the same ion ranges and the same ionization by the ion as full cascades, does not follow recoils, and is the recommended option for damage calculations; energy in keV; 0° for normal incidence; layers from the surface, each with its own element table; 500 ions. The mean depth converges as σ/√N (13.4); depth profiles converge slowly, about 45 % scatter per bin if 500 ions spread over all 100 bins.
  • Stoichiometry is entered as atom fractions, and a compound's density must be typed in: SiO₂ 2.20, dry air 1.205×10⁻³ g/cm³; TRIM's 2.32 for Si is within 0.3 % of the measured 2.329 g/cm³. Every depth scales as 1/ρ (13.5). The Gas box switches to gas-phase stopping and, in SRIM-2013, to a gas-phase density estimate that still has to be checked.
  • Every depth distribution is per ion per unit depth: per Å for ionization, phonons, vacancies and the depth-distribution text files, RANGE.txt included; per cm, as (atoms/cm³)/(atoms/cm²), for the ion-range plot, which multiplied by the fluence gives a concentration (13.6). Integrate to obtain energy per ion (13.7), divide by 3.6 eV (Si) or 17 eV (SiO₂) for pairs (13.8), and use ΦElayer/(ρt) for dose (13.9). In silicon 1 eV/Å = 0.0429 MeV·cm²/mg = 0.445 fC/µm.
  • Displacements = vacancies + replacement collisions (13.10). TRIM's silicon threshold is 15 eV, which gives about 1.4 times the vacancies of the 21 eV used elsewhere in the course, somewhat more for recoils near threshold. Sputtering (13.11) needs an option that follows recoils: Surface Sputtering or Monolayer Collision Steps, which the SRIM manual calls essential for accurate yields, or Full Damage Cascades; 1 keV argon on silicon yields about 0.87 atoms/ion.
  • At 50–100 keV a proton gives at least 97 % of its energy to ionization. A 100 keV oxygen ion has a reduced energy (13.12) of 5.4 against the proton's 79; the documented 190 keV phosphorus case passes 56 % of its energy to recoils, and oxygen is expected to behave similarly in milder form.
  • A 1 MeV proton travels 2.38 cm in sea-level air. A 250 MeV proton's range, 42.9 g/cm², is 4.2 % of the atmosphere's 1033 g/cm²; TRIM omits the nuclear reactions such a proton can undergo.
  • Stopping powers agree with experiment to 4–6 % on average; vacancy counts are displacement events, not surviving defects. TRIM does not model photons, electrons, nuclear reactions, channeling, temperature or charge collection. Outputs in SRIM Outputs are overwritten by the next run.
