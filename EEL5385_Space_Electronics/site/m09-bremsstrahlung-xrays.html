Module 09

Bremsstrahlung and Characteristic X-rays

The third energy-loss channel for a charged particle: radiating a photon when its own trajectory is bent. It converts electrons into penetrating X-rays, is the operating principle of every X-ray tube, and is the reason a beta shield can make matters worse.

Prerequisites M08 Read time ~35 min Sources Decks 5, 7; Lectures 5, 7

M08 covered the two energy-loss mechanisms in which the incident particle gives energy to atomic electrons. This module covers the one in which it gives energy to the electromagnetic field instead. When a charged particle passes close enough to a nucleus for the Coulomb attraction to bend its path, the acceleration causes it to radiate, and the radiated photon carries away part of its kinetic energy. The German name, Bremsstrahlung, means braking radiation.

Two things make this worth a module. It is the only mechanism that converts charged particles into photons, which changes the shielding problem completely: a material that stops a beta particle produces penetrating X-rays in the process, and those X-rays must then be stopped by something else. And it is how X-rays are manufactured — every X-ray tube, including the irradiator in the Research 1 building that this course uses, is a device for stopping electrons in a metal target and collecting what comes out. The module works through the physics, the two dependences that govern its magnitude, the spectrum it produces, and how a cabinet irradiator turns that spectrum into a stated dose.

1 · Why a deflected charge radiates

An electric charge moving at constant velocity carries a field with it and radiates nothing. A charge that accelerates radiates electromagnetic energy, and the radiated power grows as the square of the acceleration. This is a general result of electromagnetism, not a property of any particular particle: it is equally the operating principle of a radio antenna, where electrons are accelerated back and forth along a conductor.

Inside a solid, a charged particle passing near a nucleus experiences the Coulomb force of equation (8.1) — attractive for an electron approaching a positive nucleus — and is deflected. The deflection is an acceleration, so the particle radiates, and the energy it radiates is subtracted from its kinetic energy. The emitted photon can carry any energy from nearly zero up to the entire kinetic energy of the particle, depending on how sharply the trajectory was bent, which is the origin of the continuous spectrum treated in §5.

2 · Why bremsstrahlung is an electron phenomenon

Bremsstrahlung is almost exclusively associated with electrons and beta particles, and the reason is Newton's second law. For a given Coulomb force the acceleration a particle undergoes is a = F/m, so a light particle is deflected far more sharply than a heavy one passing the same nucleus at the same distance. Since radiated power scales as the square of the acceleration, it scales as the inverse square of the particle mass.

The numbers are decisive. A proton is 1,836 times the mass of an electron, so at equal charge and equal encounter geometry it radiates 1/1,836² ≈ 3 × 10−7 as much. An alpha particle is 7,294 electron masses with twice the charge, giving (2/7,294)² ≈ 8 × 10−8. Heavy charged particles travel in essentially straight lines through matter, are not deflected to any significant extent, and produce no bremsstrahlung worth considering. All of their energy goes into ionization and excitation.

3 · Why it grows with the atomic number of the target

The second dependence is on the target rather than the projectile. A nucleus of atomic number Z carries charge +Ze, so by equation (8.1) the Coulomb force it exerts on a passing electron is proportional to Z. A larger force produces a larger acceleration and therefore more radiation, and squaring the acceleration makes the emission from a single nucleus scale as Z².

Bremsstrahlung is therefore most intense when two conditions hold together: the incident electrons have high energy, and the target material has a high atomic number. Both appear directly in the yield formula of §4. Practically, this means a tungsten target (Z = 74) is an efficient X-ray producer and a plastic shield (Z ≈ 6) is a poor one — which is exactly why plastics are the preferred material for stopping beta particles, a point returned to in §8.

4 · Radiation yield

The fraction of an electron's kinetic energy that is converted to bremsstrahlung rather than lost to ionization and excitation is the radiation yield, written f. Deck 5 gives two expressions for it.

Deck 5 p. 23 prints the formula as f = 0.0007 ZE and then evaluates it for a 1 MeV electron in silicon as "f = 0.007 × 14 × 1 MeV = 0.098 MeV = 98 keV". The coefficient in the worked line is ten times the coefficient in the formula above it.

Using the slide's own printed formula, 0.0007 × 14 × 1 = 0.0098, so a 1 MeV electron in silicon converts about 1 % of its energy — 9.8 keV — to bremsstrahlung. The slide's second, saturating form gives (6 × 10−4 × 14 × 1) / (1 + 6 × 10−4 × 14 × 1) = 0.0083, or 8.3 keV, which agrees with 9.8 keV and not with 98 keV. Published coefficients from the health-physics literature (Cember & Johnson give 3.5 × 10−4) point the same way. 9.8 keV is the number the slide's formula produces; 98 keV is an arithmetic slip in the evaluation line.

A second point about the same line. f is a dimensionless fraction, and multiplying it by the electron energy gives the total energy radiated as a continuum spread over all photon energies from zero to 1 MeV. It does not predict a photon line at that energy. The distinction matters in §7, where genuine lines do appear and have a different origin.

The yield is small at the energies this course deals with. For a 1 MeV electron stopping in tungsten, equation (9.1) gives f = 0.0007 × 74 × 1 ≈ 5 %, with the saturating form returning 4 %; tabulated radiative yields for tungsten at this energy are of the same order. Roughly 95 % of the beam energy therefore becomes heat in the target and a few percent becomes X-rays. This is why an industrial X-ray tube needs kilowatts of input power and active cooling: almost all of the electrical power delivered to the anode is dissipated thermally, and only a few percent leaves as the beam.

5 · The X-ray tube and its continuous spectrum

An X-ray tube is the direct application of everything above. Electrons are emitted from a heated filament, accelerated through a potential difference of tens to hundreds of kilovolts, and driven into a metal target. They decelerate in the target, radiating bremsstrahlung, and the photons that escape form the beam.

The tube itself is a sealed vacuum envelope containing two electrodes. The cathode is a tungsten filament heated until it emits electrons thermionically, seated in a shaped focusing cup held at the cathode potential, which confines the electron stream to a small spot on the anode. The anode is a block of copper with a thin tungsten target set into its face. The two materials divide the work: tungsten, with Z = 74, converts more of the electron energy to bremsstrahlung than a lighter metal would, by equation (9.1), and has the highest melting point of any metal, 3,422 °C, while copper conducts the heat away from the focal spot. The vacuum lets the electrons cross the gap without colliding with gas molecules. X-rays leave through a thin window of low-atomic-number material in the envelope wall, and everything downstream — added filters, collimator, sample — sits in air outside it.

The spectrum is continuous because each electron can lose any fraction of its energy in any single deflection, and most electrons undergo many deflections before stopping. Photon energies therefore run from essentially zero upward. The spectrum has a sharp upper limit, however: no photon can carry more energy than the electron that produced it, and the electrons all arrive with the same kinetic energy, set by the accelerating voltage. An electron accelerated through 320 kV arrives with 320 keV, so the spectrum ends at 320 keV. This endpoint is the Duane–Hunt limit, and it makes the tube voltage the single control that sets the beam's maximum energy.

Emax = eVtube

6 · Characteristic X-rays

Superimposed on the continuum are sharp lines, and they have nothing to do with bremsstrahlung. They arise when an incident electron, instead of merely being deflected, knocks an electron out of an inner shell of a target atom. That leaves a vacancy in a tightly bound level, and an electron from a higher shell falls into it, emitting a photon whose energy is exactly the difference between the two levels.

Because atomic energy levels are discrete and element-specific, these photons appear at fixed energies that identify the target material. Lines produced by filling a vacancy in the innermost shell are the K lines: Kα when the electron falls from the next shell out, Kβ when it falls from two shells out. For tungsten, Kα₁ sits at 59.3 keV and Kβ₁ at 67.2 keV. For copper, the same lines sit at 8.05 and 8.90 keV.

The two components of an X-ray tube spectrum have different physics and different uses. The continuum comes from deflection, scales with the tube voltage and with the target's atomic number, and is what delivers dose. The characteristic lines come from inner-shell vacancies, sit at energies fixed by the target element, and appear only when the tube voltage exceeds the binding energy of the shell involved — a tungsten anode produces no K lines at all below about 69.5 kV, because no incident electron has enough energy to create the vacancy. Identifying the lines in a measured spectrum identifies the anode material unambiguously.

7 · The UCF irradiator, and what its spectrum shows

The X-RAD 320 cabinet irradiator on the first floor of the Research 1 building is the facility this course refers to. It is a self-contained shielded cabinet with a 320 kVp tube of up to 4 kW, an adjustable specimen shelf, a variable collimator and a filter holder, and its published weight of about 5,500 lb — roughly 2.5 tonnes — is almost all lead shielding, needed to hold the external dose rate at background. Electrons are accelerated onto a tungsten target set into a copper anode, with an anode angle of 30°, producing the continuum of §5 with the tungsten lines of §6 on top, and the beam passes through a 3 mm beryllium window and then through whatever added filtration is installed before reaching the sample.

The window is beryllium because it must hold vacuum yet absorb as little of the beam as possible, and photoelectric absorption, which removes the low-energy end of the spectrum, rises steeply with atomic number. Beryllium, Z = 4, has the lowest atomic number of any structural metal and is stiff enough to hold vacuum as a sheet a few millimetres thick: 3 mm of it transmits 70 % of 10 keV photons and 91 % of 30 keV photons. Its drawback is toxicity. Inhaled beryllium particulate causes chronic beryllium disease, a granulomatous lung disease in sensitized individuals, and the US occupational exposure limit is 0.2 µg/m³ averaged over an eight-hour day. An intact window is not a hazard; a fractured or corroded one is. The alternative low-Z window is diamond, Z = 6, which is non-toxic and conducts heat far better but absorbs more per unit thickness: 1 mm transmits 43 % at 10 keV against 89 % for beryllium. The X-RAD 320 uses beryllium.

The simulated spectrum on Deck 5 p. 25 shows all of this: a continuum rising from a low-energy cut-off near 20 keV, peaking near 45 keV, stepping down at 69.5 keV, and falling to an endpoint near 300 keV, with two sharp lines superimposed at approximately 59 and 67 keV.

More importantly, f is a dimensionless fraction and cannot predict a line position at all — the agreement between "67 keV" and the spectrum's upper line is a numerical coincidence, and the real origin of that line is the tungsten Kβ₁ transition. With the emitting material identified as tungsten, equation (9.1) gives f = 0.0007 × 74 × 0.32 ≈ 1.7 %, or 1.4 % from the saturating form: about 98 % of the electron-beam power is deposited as heat in the target, and the copper behind it exists to remove that heat.

8 · Filtration and beam hardening

Every X-ray tube spectrum is modified by material placed in the beam, and the modification is strongly energy-dependent. The dominant absorption mechanism at these energies is the photoelectric effect, whose probability falls steeply with photon energy — roughly as 1/E³. A filter therefore removes low-energy photons far more efficiently than high-energy ones.

Two consequences follow. The mean energy of the transmitted beam rises, because the soft component has been preferentially removed — the beam becomes more penetrating, and the process is called beam hardening. And the total intensity falls, so hardening a beam always costs dose rate. Filtration is used deliberately for this trade: the softest photons in an unfiltered beam are absorbed in the first fraction of a millimetre of a sample and contribute dose to its surface without reaching its interior, so removing them makes the delivered dose more uniform through the sample's depth. The X-RAD 320 is supplied with beam-hardening filters and a filter holder for exactly this purpose.

A tube therefore has two independent controls over its spectrum. The tube voltage sets the high-energy end: by equation (9.2) no photon exceeds eVtube, so spectra taken at 30, 50, 80 and 100 kVp end at 30, 50, 80 and 100 keV. Filtration sets the low-energy end: it removes photons from the bottom of the spectrum and leaves the endpoint where it was. Filtration only removes photons; raising the tube voltage both extends the endpoint and increases the output at every lower energy. The size of the filtration effect follows from the exponential attenuation law of M10 and the NIST coefficients for copper: 0.5 mm of copper transmits 0.75 % of 30 keV photons but 49 % of 60 keV photons.

Two free calculators perform this arithmetic. SpekPy is a Python toolkit, with a web interface, that computes the spectrum of an X-ray tube — bremsstrahlung continuum and characteristic lines — from the tube voltage, anode angle, target element and a stack of filters. It returns photon fluence per unit energy, per milliampere-second of tube charge (tube current multiplied by exposure time), at a chosen distance, together with derived quantities such as the mean energy and the half-value layer; the fluence delivered therefore grows in proportion to current and exposure time. The GSI X-ray attenuation and absorption calculator solves the single-energy problem of M10: for one photon energy and one slab of a listed material it returns the transmitted and attenuated fractions, and divides the interaction probability among the photon interaction mechanisms, from NIST cross-section data. SpekPy gives the spectrum that enters a slab; the attenuation calculator gives what one energy in that spectrum does inside it.

9 · Bremsstrahlung as a shielding problem

The practical importance of this mechanism for spacecraft is that it converts a stoppable radiation into an unstoppable one. Trapped electrons in the outer belt are the easiest population to shield against: M05's fluence plot showed the electron spectrum cutting off near 7 MeV, and about 1.5 cm of aluminium removes essentially all of it — the practical range of a 7 MeV electron in aluminium is 1.3 cm. But the electrons do not simply vanish. Each one that stops in the shield radiates a fraction f of its energy as bremsstrahlung photons, and those photons are penetrating in a way the electrons were not.

Two design responses follow from equation (9.1). The yield scales with Z, so a low-atomic-number material converts less of the electron energy into X-rays: aluminium (Z = 13) is better than tantalum (Z = 73) for stopping electrons, and a polymer better still. And a graded shield — a low-Z layer first to stop the electrons with minimal radiation production, then a high-Z layer to absorb whatever bremsstrahlung was produced — outperforms either material alone at the same total mass. The same logic applies in the laboratory: beta sources are stored behind plastic rather than lead for precisely this reason.

Adding shield mass does not monotonically reduce the dose behind it. For an electron environment, a thin aluminium shield stops the electrons and produces bremsstrahlung; thickening it further attenuates the bremsstrahlung and continues to help. But replacing that aluminium with a denser, higher-Z material of the same thickness stops the electrons in a smaller distance while converting a larger fraction of their energy to X-rays, and can leave the dose behind the shield higher than before. Shield material is as much a design variable as shield mass, and the same conclusion appears again for a different reason in M11.

A 2 MeV beta source must be shielded. Compare 3 mm of aluminium against 1 mm of lead, both of which stop the electrons completely.

Both stop the electrons, so the comparison turns entirely on what is produced in the process and what happens to it.

Aluminium, Z = 13, converts f = 0.0007 × 13 × 2 ≈ 1.8 % of the electron energy to bremsstrahlung. Lead, Z = 82, converts 0.0007 × 82 × 2 ≈ 11 % — six times as much, because the yield is proportional to the target's atomic number.

Lead is, however, a far better absorber of the X-rays once they exist, so the comparison is not settled by the yield alone; it depends on whether enough lead follows the production point to reabsorb what was made. In 1 mm of lead much of the production occurs near the entrance surface and a substantial fraction of the resulting photons escape. The conventional answer is therefore aluminium or plastic for the electron-stopping layer, and if the bremsstrahlung dose is still unacceptable, a lead layer added behind it rather than substituted for it. Using the high-Z material first maximises production where it does the most harm.

Summary

  • An accelerating charge radiates, and radiated power scales as the square of the acceleration. A charged particle deflected by a nucleus therefore emits a photon and loses that energy — bremsstrahlung.
  • Since a = F/m, radiated power scales as 1/m². A proton radiates ~3 × 10−7 as much as an electron and an alpha ~8 × 10−8. Bremsstrahlung is an electron phenomenon.
  • The Coulomb force from a nucleus scales as Z, so emission per nucleus scales as Z². High-Z targets are efficient X-ray producers and poor electron shields.
  • Radiation yield f = 0.0007 ZE (dimensionless; E in MeV), or the saturating form f = (6 × 10−4)ZE / [1 + (6 × 10−4)ZE]. For 1 MeV electrons in silicon this gives 9.8 keV; the 98 keV on Deck 5 p. 23 uses a coefficient ten times the one printed above it.
  • An X-ray tube accelerates electrons from a heated filament onto a tungsten target set in a copper anode, which carries the heat away; the X-rays leave through a low-Z window, beryllium in the X-RAD 320. The spectrum is a continuum, because any fraction of the energy can be radiated in a single deflection, with an endpoint at Emax = eVtube — the Duane–Hunt limit.
  • Characteristic lines sit on the continuum and come from inner-shell vacancies, not from deflection. Their energies identify the anode element: tungsten Kα₁ 59.3 keV and Kβ₁ 67.2 keV; copper 8.05 and 8.90 keV.
  • Filtration removes low-energy photons preferentially, because photoelectric absorption falls roughly as 1/E³. The mean beam energy rises — beam hardening — and the intensity falls. The X-RAD 320 delivers 3 Gy/min at 1 mm Cu half-value layer and 1 Gy/min at 4 mm.
  • The tube voltage sets the high-energy end of the spectrum and filtration the low-energy end. For a 300 kVp tungsten beam, 4 mm of added copper leaves 9 % of the photon fluence and raises the mean energy from 65 to 155 keV; on a plot normalized to each curve's own peak the filtered spectrum still reaches 1.0.
  • Shielding trapped electrons produces bremsstrahlung that the electrons themselves could not deliver — a 7 MeV electron needs 1.3 cm of aluminium, and every electron stopped radiates a fraction f of its energy as penetrating X-rays. Use a low-Z layer to stop the electrons and a high-Z layer behind it to absorb the X-rays — never the reverse order.
