Module 14b

Radiation Effects on MEMS

Mechanical microstructures of silicon, moved and sensed by electrostatic forces: why such resonators fly, how a comb-drive resonator works, what a proton beam does to its resonance frequency while the beam is on and after it stops, and how the shift is reversed.

Prerequisites M01, M08, M11, M12, M13, M14 Read time ~40 min Sources Deck 11; Lecture 11; Deck 17 p. 39; Deck 1; Shea (2009); Bandi et al. (2013)

Every device in the preceding modules has been electronic: a junction, a channel or an oxide, degraded through the carriers or the charge it holds. A microelectromechanical system, MEMS, is a mechanical structure — a beam, a plate, an array of interleaved fingers — micromachined, most often in silicon, at the scale of micrometres and then driven and read out electrically. Its function rests on two quantities that radiation can alter: the stiffness of the structure, which sets how it moves, and the electrostatic forces that move it, which depend on charge. A MEMS device therefore responds to the same two damage mechanisms as the electronics of M11 and M14, displacement and ionization, but the property they act on is mechanical.

The course studies one device in detail: a silicon comb-drive resonator whose resonance frequency was recorded every few minutes while 1.8 MeV protons struck it (Deck 11). This module sets out why mechanical resonators fly and how a comb-drive resonator works (§1–2), what radiation can change in a mechanical part (§3), the experiment and its arithmetic (§4), where the protons deposit energy and damage (§5), the mechanism the decks assign to the temporary and the permanent shifts of frequency (§6), and how a shift is reversed (§7).

1 · Why mechanical resonators fly

A resonator is a structure that vibrates at a natural frequency fixed by its own properties. Oscillators, filters and timing references are built around one, and a mechanical resonator offers both a far smaller size and a far higher quality factor (§2) than an electrical one.

The frequency of a resonator that supports a travelling wave is the propagation speed of the wave divided by a characteristic length of the device (Deck 11 p. 27). Electrical signals propagate at 40–90 % of the speed of light; mechanical, acoustic waves travel at 300–15,000 m/s, slower by a factor of 10⁴ to 10⁶. A mechanical device that reaches the same frequency, or imposes the same signal delay, is smaller by the same factor. In a surface-acoustic-wave (SAW) delay line the signal travels as an acoustic wave along the surface of a crystal chip, and one such chip delays it by 113 µs, where a spool of low-loss coaxial cable many times its volume delays it by 2.5 µs (Deck 11 p. 26).

Size and mass are scarce on a spacecraft, so micromechanical devices have flown, or been built to fly, wherever a mechanical function can be made small: an experimental MEMS gyroscope tested in orbit on ESA's CryoSat-2, MEMS shutters on the James Webb Space Telescope, and a MEMS gyroscope on the rover that CNES and DLR built for JAXA's Martian Moons eXploration mission (Deck 11 p. 28). The same argument has put several micromechanical devices into every smartphone (Deck 11 pp. 19–20).

A resonator much smaller than the acoustic wavelength at its frequency moves as a single mass on a spring, and its frequency follows from the stiffness and the moving mass.

f = 12π √km
(14b.1)
f resonance frequency (Hz) · k stiffness, the restoring force per unit displacement (N/m) · m moving mass (kg). At fixed mass a change of stiffness shifts the frequency by Δf/f = ½Δk/k, so every frequency shift in this module is read as a change of stiffness. Added here: the decks state the dependence in words. On the formula sheet as (14b.1).

The vibratory gyroscope

A MEMS gyroscope drives a proof mass into vibration along one axis. When the device rotates at angular rate Ω, the Coriolis force 2mv × Ω, with v the proof mass's vibration velocity, drives a second vibration along the third axis whose amplitude is proportional to the rotation rate. Sensing that motion measures rotation with no spinning wheel. Of the gyroscope types the decks compare — vibratory, fluid, fibre-optic and ring-laser — the vibratory ones are the smallest and also the least precise (Deck 11 pp. 25, 49–53). They suffice for phones and cameras; making them precise enough for navigation without satellite positioning is a research goal (Lecture 11). A radiation-induced shift of the proof mass's resonance becomes an error in the measured rotation rate.

2 · The comb-drive resonator

The device in the course's experiment is a comb-drive resonator: a perforated silicon plate, the shuttle, suspended on flexible beams and moved sideways by the electrostatic attraction between interleaved fingers. Its drive, its readout and its response to charge all follow from one expression for the electrostatic force; its frequency and quality factor are mechanical, the frequency modified by the electrostatic spring.

It is etched from a silicon-on-insulator (SOI) wafer, which carries a single-crystal silicon device layer, here 5 µm thick, on a layer of silicon dioxide over a thick silicon substrate (Deck 11 p. 34). The device layer is patterned into the shuttle, its suspension beams and two combs of fixed fingers. The oxide beneath the moving parts is then etched away, which leaves them free over a gap of a few micrometres and anchored to the substrate only where oxide remains. The released structures are dried by critical-point drying, in which the rinse liquid is exchanged for liquid carbon dioxide, which is taken around its critical point and vented as a gas, so that no liquid–vapour meniscus forms between the parts and the substrate and surface tension cannot pull the parts down onto it. A shuttle with its combs spans a few hundred micrometres, and nine devices share a chip.

The fingers of each comb lie parallel to the shuttle's motion and interleave with fingers attached to the shuttle (Deck 11 p. 34), so a displacement x changes their overlap and with it the capacitance C0 between shuttle and comb. A voltage across a capacitance that varies with position produces a force. At fixed voltage V, the source supplies V² dC0 as the capacitance grows by dC0; half is stored in the field and half does work F dx on the shuttle, so the force is directed so as to increase C0.

F = 12 dC0dx V²
(14b.2)
F electrostatic force along the motion (N) · C0 capacitance between the shuttle and one comb (F) · x shuttle displacement (m) · V voltage between them (V). For N overlapping finger pairs of thickness h separated by gaps g on both sides, dC0/dx = 2Nε0h/g, the same at every overlap, so the comb pulls with a force independent of position. The force is attractive for either sign of V. The decks' equation (Deck 11 p. 35). On the formula sheet as (14b.2).

The shuttle is held at a DC bias VDC, 10 V in the experiment (Deck 11 pp. 34, 36), and a small alternating drive vac cos ωt is applied to the drive comb. Because the force goes as the square of the voltage between them, its component at the drive frequency has amplitude (dC0/dx)VDCvac: the bias turns a quadratic transducer into a linear one, and the drive force is proportional to VDC. At the sense comb the motion modulates the capacitance, and with the bias applied a current VDC(dC0/dx)(dx/dt) flows into the readout amplifier, proportional to the shuttle's velocity.

Seen from its terminals, the moving structure behaves as a series resonant circuit, the motional branch (Deck 11 p. 35). With identical drive and sense combs and η = VDC dC0/dx the coupling of each, an inductance Lm = m/η² stands for the moving mass, a capacitance Cm = η²/k for the compliance of the suspension, and a resistance Rm = b/η² for the mechanical damping coefficient b. The coupling cancels in the resonance condition, so the branch resonates at (14b.1), while the motional current, and with it the height of the measured peak, grows as VDC². In parallel with the branch lie the parasitic capacitances and resistances of the pads, the oxide and the substrate, through which part of the drive signal reaches the output without any motion. The measured transmission, the ratio of the signal at the sense comb to the drive, in decibels, therefore shows a peak where the motional branch resonates and a dip just above it, where the motional and parasitic currents cancel.

The sharpness of the peak is measured by the quality factor Q, the resonance frequency divided by the width of the peak at half power; Q is also 2π times the energy stored in the vibration divided by the energy dissipated per cycle. The experiment's resonator has f ≈ 29.485 kHz and Q ≈ 23,000 (Deck 11 p. 41), so its peak is about 1.3 Hz wide, and a shift of a fraction of a hertz can be resolved. That resolution is what makes the device a sensitive detector of anything that alters its stiffness.

At larger drive the shuttle's travel reaches a fixed stop, and the response flattens, with different curves for upward and downward frequency sweeps: the shuttle strikes the stop once per cycle. This tapping regime turns the resonator into a resonant switch, proposed for mechanical logic, charge pumps and amplifiers (Deck 11 pp. 39–41). The radiation measurements of §4 were made in the linear regime, at a drive power of −49 dBm, decibels relative to 1 mW, about 13 nW (Deck 11 p. 42).

Electrostatic softening

A voltage does more than drive the resonator: it also changes its stiffness. Where the force of (14b.2) varies with position, a displacement changes the force, and that change acts as an additional spring. Across a gap that closes as the shuttle moves, the capacitance rises faster than linearly, the electrostatic force grows with displacement, and it opposes the mechanical restoring force, so the effective stiffness falls.

f = 12π √km − kelm,   kel = 12 V² d²Cdx²
(14b.3)
km mechanical stiffness of the suspension, the k of (14b.1) (N/m) · kel electrostatic spring constant, positive when it softens (N/m) · C capacitance across a gap whose width changes with x (F) · V voltage across that gap (V) · m moving mass (kg). For parallel plates of area A at gap g, C = ε0A/(g − x) and kel = ε0AV²/g³. For ideal comb fingers C is linear in x and kel = 0; a real comb drive softens through fringing fields and the closing gaps at its finger tips and at the ends of the shuttle. Added here: the decks name the effect (Deck 11 p. 46) without a formula. On the formula sheet as (14b.3).

Electrostatic softening makes the frequency depend on every voltage that acts across such a gap. An extra potential on an electrode, or charge sitting on a surface near the moving parts, changes the effective voltage, and with it kel and f. That dependence is the route by which ionization reaches the resonance frequency (§6).

3 · What radiation can change in a mechanical part

A transistor degrades because radiation alters the carriers in its channel or the charge in its oxide. A MEMS structure has no channel, and many have no p–n junction at all: only conductors whose potentials must be defined, and the dielectric films between them (Shea 2009). Radiation acts on such a structure along a chain of four links (Deck 11 p. 33).

The incident radiation interacts with the material by ionization, by displacing atoms, by implanting the incident particle, and by heating. Each interaction leaves a material effect: charge trapped in insulators and on surfaces, broken bonds and displaced atoms in the lattice, and implanted atoms. A material effect changes a property, electrical or mechanical. The electrical properties the deck lists are carrier density, capacitance and time delay; for an electrostatic MEMS the electrical change that matters is the charge near its electrodes, which alters the electrostatic force (Deck 11 p. 46). The mechanical properties are the elastic constants, the internal stress and the hardness. Among the elastic constants the one that sets a resonator's frequency is Young's modulus EY, the ratio of tensile stress to tensile strain in the elastic range: the stiffness of a beam is proportional to it, with a factor fixed by the beam's dimensions, so km ∝ EY and, by (14b.1), f ∝ √EY. A changed property, finally, changes the device's performance, which for a resonator means its resonance frequency and its quality factor.

The literature on MEMS in radiation environments ranks these routes clearly. As a purely structural material, silicon has shown no mechanical degradation after doses above 100 Mrad, whereas complete MEMS devices, excluding their electronics, have failed at doses as low as 20 krad and others work beyond 10 Mrad; the difference lies in the actuation principle, the design and the materials, and is linked primarily to charge trapped in dielectrics (Shea 2009). Shea qualified the statement about silicon: it holds at the level of parts per thousand, and resonators sensitive to changes of parts per million in Young's modulus had not then been tested. Electrostatic devices are the most sensitive, above all those with dielectric between or beneath their moving parts, because trapped charge applies a quasi-constant electrostatic force that first shifts the device's calibration and can finally hold it actuated even at zero applied voltage.

4 · The in-situ proton experiment

The course's experiment exposed the comb-drive resonator of §2 to 1.8 MeV protons from a Pelletron, an electrostatic accelerator at Vanderbilt University, while its resonance was recorded (Deck 11 pp. 36–37). A measurement made only before and after an exposure cannot separate effects that last while the beam is on from those that remain; an in-situ measurement, made during the exposure, can.

A shutter starts and stops the exposure. The shuttle is biased at VDC = 10 V, a network analyzer supplies the drive, and the sense current passes through a low-noise amplifier back to the analyzer, which sweeps its drive frequency across the resonance and records the transmitted signal (Deck 11 p. 36). The beam is specified by its flux φ, the number of protons crossing unit area per unit time, and the accumulated exposure by its fluence Φ (M05 §9; for a test beam, M14a §6).

Φ = φ t
(14b.4)
Φ fluence (cm⁻²) · φ flux, constant over the exposure (cm⁻² s⁻¹) · t exposure time (s). Low-flux run: 2.78×10⁸ cm⁻² s⁻¹ × 1080 s = 3.00×10¹¹ cm⁻²; high-flux run: 9.14×10⁸ cm⁻² s⁻¹ × 1358 s = 1.24×10¹² cm⁻² (Deck 11 pp. 42–43). On the formula sheet as (14b.4).

In the low-flux run the frequency fell steadily while the beam was on, by about 0.7 Hz over the 18 minutes of exposure, recovered a little when the shutter closed, and then stayed low (Deck 11 pp. 42, 44). In the high-flux run it had dropped by 5.5 Hz at the first reading after the beam came on, fell a further 3.3 Hz during the exposure, and had risen by 5.6 Hz at the first reading after the beam stopped, which left it 3.2 Hz below the reading taken just before exposure; over the following half hour it crept slowly upward (Deck 11 p. 44). The deck gives the in-beam downshift of this run as 8.41 Hz (p. 43), measured from the first reading of the record; measured from the reading just before the beam came on it is 5.5 + 3.3 = 8.8 Hz, the difference being an upward drift of the frequency before exposure. A flux 3.3 times higher, applied for longer, produced a far larger shift, and the deck's conclusion is that a large flux has a dramatically larger effect.

Both traced from Deck 11 p. 44, as shifts from the frequency before exposure: 29.4854 kHz, and 29.4841 kHz just before the high-flux beam came on. The slow upward drift of the high-flux baseline before exposure is omitted.

The low-flux permanent shift printed in Deck 11

Deck 11 p. 42 gives the permanent downshift of the low-flux run as about 0.2 Hz. The deck's own plots show the frequency settling, once the beam stopped, about 0.7 Hz below its pre-exposure value on p. 44 and 0.5 Hz below it in the spectra of p. 42; about 0.2 Hz is the part that recovered from the lowest in-beam value. Per unit fluence, a 0.5–0.7 Hz offset matches the 3.2 Hz offset that the high-flux run showed just after its beam stopped (about 2.4 Hz by the end of the record) at four times the fluence, and 0.2 Hz does not. The course's figure is 0.2 Hz, as printed.

The resonator is also a detector. The deck quotes its frequency noise without beam as δf ≈ 0.05 Hz, and the 0.7 Hz shift of the low-flux run as fourteen times that noise (Deck 11 p. 42). The deck defines sensitivity as the smallest input that gives a signal-to-noise ratio of 1 (Deck 11 p. 30).

S/N = Δfδf,   φmin = φS/N
(14b.5)
S/N signal-to-noise ratio (dimensionless) · Δf frequency shift produced by the beam (Hz) · δf frequency noise without beam (Hz) · φ the flux that produced Δf (cm⁻² s⁻¹) · φmin minimum detectable flux, at S/N = 1 (cm⁻² s⁻¹). With 0.7 Hz, 0.05 Hz and 2.78×10⁸ cm⁻² s⁻¹: S/N ≈ 14 and φmin ≈ 2×10⁷ cm⁻² s⁻¹ (Deck 11 p. 42). The second relation assumes the shift is proportional to the flux for the same 1080 s exposure; since the low-flux shift accumulated through the exposure, the run's sensitivity is equally a fluence, 3.00×10¹¹/14 ≈ 2×10¹⁰ cm⁻². On the formula sheet as (14b.5).

The two runs do not bear out that proportionality. Between them the flux rose 3.3-fold and the fluence 4.1-fold, while the in-beam fall of frequency rose more than tenfold, from 0.7 Hz to 8.41 Hz in the deck's figures (pp. 42–43). The minimum detectable flux obtained by scaling from one run is therefore an estimate whose accuracy depends on how the shift scales at low flux.

5 · Where the protons deposit energy and damage

The resonator's layers are thin compared with the range of a 1.8 MeV proton, so each proton crosses the device and the gap and stops deep in the substrate. A TRIM calculation (M13) shows how the deposited energy and the displacement damage divide between those layers (Deck 11 p. 45).

The simulation stacks the 5 µm silicon device layer, the gap of about 4 µm left where the oxide was removed, and the silicon substrate. Every proton stops in the substrate, at a depth of about 45 µm, nearly all between 44 and 47 µm. Its electronic energy loss is about 28 eV/nm in the device layer, where the proton is fastest, and it rises to a peak of about 95 eV/nm just before the proton stops (M12): the device layer is crossed at the low end of the stopping-power curve. Each proton leaves about 140 keV in the 5 µm device layer, 8 % of its energy.

Displacement damage is concentrated where the proton slows down, as M11 §5 established for any particle that stops. TRIM counts about 2×10⁻⁴ vacancies per nanometre per proton in the device layer, a third of them from the proton's own collisions and the rest from the recoils it sets moving: about one vacancy per proton across the 5 µm thickness, against about 7×10⁻³ per nanometre at the end of the range (Deck 11 p. 45, panels (e) and (f)). Most of the lattice damage the beam produces therefore lies in the substrate, beneath the moving structure rather than in it. Ionization, by contrast, is spread through every layer: electron–hole pairs are created in the device layer, in the substrate under the gap, and in the oxide that still anchors the structure.

Equation (13.9) converts these figures into the dose the device layer received. At 140 keV per proton in 5 µm of silicon (2.33 g/cm³) each proton per square centimetre deposits 1.20×10⁸ eV/g, so the high-flux fluence of 1.24×10¹² cm⁻² delivered 1.5×10²⁰ eV/g, or 2.4 Mrad(Si), and the low-flux run 0.58 Mrad(Si) (13.9). The high fluence leaves 2–4×10¹⁵ vacancies per cubic centimetre in the device layer, 4–8×10⁻⁸ of its 5.0×10²² atoms/cm³. TRIM's vacancy count is itself an upper bound on the defects that survive: most Frenkel pairs recombine, promptly inside a dense cascade and, for the isolated pairs a proton makes, as the mobile vacancies and interstitials migrate at room temperature, and the survivors form stable complexes (M11 §8).

6 · The mechanism of the frequency shift

The decks read the two components of the measured shift as the effects of the two damage mechanisms, each acting through its own chain (Deck 11 p. 46, repeated as Deck 17 p. 39).

The temporary shift: charge and electrostatic softening

Ionization generates electron–hole pairs throughout the structure for as long as the beam is on. The generated charge alters the electrostatic field between the moving and the fixed parts, and with it the effective voltage across their gaps; by (14b.3) the electrostatic spring constant changes, the structure becomes electrically softer, and the frequency falls (Lecture 11). When the beam stops, generation stops, and the charge in the silicon, which is connected to the bias supply and the amplifier, flows back into the external circuit, so the electrostatic part of the shift disappears (Lecture 11). The step of 5.5 Hz at beam-on and the step back of 5.6 Hz at beam-off in the high-flux run, each completed between two readings, are this component; their near-equality identifies them as one effect that lasts only while charge is being generated.

The sign of an electrostatic shift is not fixed. Charge that adds a potential Vq to an electrode changes the electrostatic spring constant to a value proportional to (VDC + Vq)², so charge that adds to the bias across a closing gap softens the structure and charge that opposes it stiffens the structure. Where the charge sits relative to the electrodes therefore decides the direction of the shift, and the sign depends on the device's structure (Lecture 17).

The permanent shift: lattice damage and Young's modulus

Displacement damage knocks atoms from their lattice sites and leaves vacancies, interstitials and defect complexes (M11). The decks take this lattice damage to lower the Young's modulus of the silicon. The mechanical stiffness km falls with it, and because the defects remain after the exposure, the frequency stays lower: the permanent shift, which grows with fluence (Deck 11 pp. 46, 48; Lecture 11). The gradual fall during the high-flux exposure and the 3.2 Hz that remained afterwards are this component.

Δff = 12 Δkeffkeff,   keff = km − kel
(14b.6)
Δf frequency shift (Hz) · f resonance frequency (Hz) · keff effective stiffness (N/m), from (14b.3). A change of the material enters through km ∝ EY, so with kel ≪ km, Δf/f ≈ ½ΔEY/EY; a change of charge enters through kel, with the opposite sign. The persistent 3.2 Hz on 29,485 Hz is Δf/f = −1.1×10⁻⁴, which the decks' chain reads as ΔEY/EY ≈ −2.2×10⁻⁴. Follows from (14b.1). On the formula sheet as (14b.6).

The two components carry different information. The temporary shift rises with the flux while the beam is on, steeply in these two runs, from about 0.1–0.2 Hz at 2.8×10⁸ to 5.5 Hz at 9.1×10⁸ cm⁻² s⁻¹, and the permanent shift records the fluence accumulated, so one resonator could serve, once calibrated, both as a flux monitor and as a record of past exposure (Lecture 11).

What the literature says about the permanent shift

The decks attribute the persistent shift to displacement damage lowering Young's modulus, and that is the course's account. Published measurements point elsewhere. Silicon resonators exposed to 10 MeV protons showed no change of elasticity up to 10¹³ cm⁻² (Bandi et al. 2013): eight times the course's high fluence, and, because a 10 MeV proton displaces several times fewer atoms per unit path than a 1.8 MeV one, a displacement damage dose about twice the course's, with a total ionizing dose above 5.5 Mrad. Single-crystal silicon micromirrors exposed to 1–4 MeV protons at about 100 Mrad(Si) showed Young's modulus rising, by up to 0.05 % (Gomes and Shea 2011). In the course's device the displacement damage is small: fewer than one atom in 10⁷ displaced (§5), against a relative stiffness change of 2×10⁻⁴.

The mechanism the review literature identifies as dominant in electrostatic MEMS is charge trapped in dielectric layers, which persists for hours to days (Shea 2009). Charge trapped in the oxide that anchors the structure, or in the native oxide on its surfaces — the nanometre or two of SiO₂ that forms on any silicon surface exposed to air — is ionization damage that does not drain through the electronics, and its slow decay would match the gradual recovery after the beam stopped. The decks' summary that MEMS typically degrade less than electronic devices (Deck 11 p. 48) holds for silicon as a structural material, not for an electrostatic device's sensitivity to trapped charge.

7 · Reversing the shift

Neither component of the shift has to be final. The temporary one reverses by itself; the persistent one can be reduced by annealing, compensated electrically, or prevented by design.

The electrostatic shift that lasts only while charge is generated reverses as fast as the external circuit removes the charge, which in the experiment was faster than the interval between measurements (Lecture 11; Deck 11 p. 44).

Annealing reverses radiation damage by giving defects and trapped charge the energy to return to a lower-energy state. Displacement damage in silicon partly anneals at room temperature over minutes to hours as vacancies and interstitials migrate and recombine, and heating accelerates the process (M11 §8). Charge trapped in an oxide is neutralized by tunnelling from the silicon and by thermal emission; both recover linearly in the logarithm of time, and thermal emission rises steeply with temperature, as exp(−Et/kT) with Et the depth of the trap (M14 §6). The slow upward drift of frequency after the high-flux exposure (§4), beyond the drift the frequency already showed before the beam, is consistent with room-temperature recovery of one or both kinds.

A MEMS structure can carry its own heater. A released structure is thermally isolated from the substrate by its suspension, so a current through a resistive element on it raises its temperature with little power. The course's research slides show MEMS devices ovenized to about 1000 °C (Deck 1 p. 18), and a micro-heater that anneals a radiation-damaged superconducting nanowire detector of single photons in place, at 1000 °C for 2 mW (Deck 1 p. 21); the same principle would let a resonator anneal itself.

A shift can also be compensated rather than repaired. In a device with a significant electrostatic spring, the frequency depends on the bias through (14b.3), so changing VDC changes kel and returns the frequency to its target, by the same dependence through which a drift of the effective voltage produced the shift.

Design prevents most of the persistent sensitivity of electrostatic devices (Shea 2009). It removes dielectric from between and beneath moving surfaces, or minimizes the dielectric area left exposed; covers exposed dielectric with a conductor held at a defined potential; chooses dielectrics with fewer traps, or adds a charge-dissipation layer, a weakly conducting film over the dielectric that bleeds trapped charge to a defined potential; stiffens the restoring springs, which reduces the displacement a given charge produces; and holds every conductor at a defined potential rather than letting it float. Accelerometers with a conducting polysilicon shield over the dielectric beneath their proof mass showed no degradation up to 3 Mrad, while unshielded versions degraded near 50 krad (Shea 2009).

Summary

  • MEMS are micromachined mechanical structures, most often of silicon, driven and sensed electrically. Mechanical waves are 10⁴–10⁶ times slower than electrical ones, so a mechanical resonator is that much smaller; a lumped resonator has f = (1/2π)√(k/m).
  • Comb drive: F = ½(dC0/dx)V²; the bias VDC makes the drive linear; the motional branch RmLmCm models the mechanics. The course's device: 5 µm SOI, 29.485 kHz, Q ≈ 23,000.
  • Electrostatic softening: keff = km − kel, kel = ½V² d²C/dx² (ε0AV²/g³ for parallel plates, zero for ideal comb fingers). Charge near the electrodes changes the effective voltage and therefore f.
  • 1.8 MeV protons, Φ = φt: 3.00×10¹¹ and 1.24×10¹² cm⁻². High flux: −5.5 Hz at beam-on, −3.3 Hz during, +5.6 Hz at beam-off, 3.2 Hz persistent. Noise 0.05 Hz, S/N ≈ 14, φmin ≈ 2×10⁷ cm⁻² s⁻¹ if the shift scales with flux.
  • TRIM: the protons stop near 45 µm in the substrate; the device layer sees ≈ 28 eV/nm and about one vacancy per proton; 2.4 Mrad(Si) at the high fluence.
  • The course's mechanism: ionization → charge → electrostatic softening → temporary f↓, removed as the charge drains; displacement → lattice damage → Young's modulus ↓ → permanent f↓. Δf/f = ½Δkeff/keff. The literature finds silicon's modulus unchanged at space doses and trapped dielectric charge the dominant persistent effect in electrostatic MEMS.
  • Recovery: charge drains when the beam stops; annealing at room temperature or by heating, which a released structure can do with an on-chip heater; compensation by retuning VDC. Prevention: no dielectric near moving parts, shields, dissipation layers and defined potentials.
