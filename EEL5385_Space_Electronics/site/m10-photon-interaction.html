Module 10

Photon Interaction with Matter

How X-rays and gamma rays deposit energy in a solid: the three mechanisms that remove a photon from a beam, which one operates at which energy and atomic number, and the exponential attenuation law that turns a tabulated coefficient into a shielding thickness.

Prerequisites M09, M08, M04, M01 Read time ~45 min Sources Deck 6; Lecture 6; NIST tables

Every mechanism in M08 and M09 began with a charged particle. A charged particle carries a Coulomb field with it, so it interacts continuously along its whole path, gives a little energy to a great many atoms, and comes to rest after a well-defined distance. A photon has no charge and no rest mass. It carries no field ahead of itself, it interacts only in discrete and improbable events, and between those events it travels in a straight line and loses no energy at all. There is no distance at which a photon beam stops. There is only a distance over which its intensity falls by a stated factor.

That difference governs everything this course does with photons. It is why gamma sources deliver a uniform dose through the full thickness of a packaged part, while an alpha source deposits everything in the first few micrometres. It is why a photon shield is specified by an attenuation factor and never by a range. And it is why most photons that cross a silicon die do nothing to it at all — a result this module quantifies, because it is the starting point for total ionizing dose in M14.

The module establishes what a photon carries, separates the low-energy regime in which a solid's band structure controls absorption from the high-energy regime in which the band structure is irrelevant, then treats the three interactions in turn: photoelectric absorption, Compton scattering and pair production. It closes with the attenuation law, the tabulated coefficients that feed it, and two worked comparisons that explain why lead is a shielding material and silicon is not.

1 · What a photon carries

A photon is a quantum of the electromagnetic field. It has no rest mass and no electric charge, and it travels at the speed of light in vacuum. Its energy is fixed entirely by its wavelength.

Eph = hcλ = hf
(10.1)
Eph photon energy (J, or eV after dividing by q) · h Planck constant, 6.626×10⁻³⁴ J·s · c speed of light, 2.998×10⁸ m/s · λ wavelength (m) · f frequency (Hz) · q elementary charge, 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ C, used only to convert J to eV. The product hc = 1239.8 eV·nm is the form worth memorising: it returns E(eV) = 1239.8 ⁄ λ(nm) with no unit conversion at all.

For the green light used as the reference case in Lecture 6, λ = 500 nm gives Eph = 1239.8/500 = 2.48 eV, or 3.97×10⁻¹⁹ J. For the gamma rays this course tests parts with, λ is near 1 pm and the energy is near 1 MeV: six orders of magnitude more energy, from a wavelength six orders of magnitude shorter.

A photon also carries momentum, although momentum for a massive body is the product of mass and velocity and a photon has no mass. The relativistic relation between energy and momentum reduces, for a massless particle, to p = E/c, and substituting equation (10.1) gives a momentum set only by wavelength.

pph = Ephc = hλ
(10.2)
pph photon momentum (kg·m/s) · h Planck constant (J·s) · λ wavelength (m). At λ = 500 nm this is 1.33×10⁻²⁷ kg·m/s. The relation p = E/c holds only for massless particles; an electron or an ion needs the full relativistic expression.

That momentum transfers whenever a photon is absorbed or scattered, so a photon flux exerts a pressure. At Earth's distance from the Sun the solar irradiance is 1361 W/m², giving a radiation pressure of 1361/c = 4.5×10⁻⁶ Pa on a fully absorbing surface. The force is negligible against a spacecraft structure over a second and significant against a dust grain over an orbit, which is why a comet's dust tail is swept away from the Sun. A comet's second tail, the ionised one, is driven instead by the solar wind treated in M05, and the two tails point in measurably different directions.

2 · Two regimes, and why the target is described differently in each

One word, absorption, covers two physically distinct processes, and the quantity that predicts each is different. Separating them is the first thing to do, because a shielding calculation that reasons from a bandgap is wrong by orders of magnitude.

At photon energies of a few electronvolts the wavelength is thousands of times larger than the spacing between atoms, so the photon does not resolve individual atoms. It couples to the collective electronic states of the solid, the bands established in M01. The photon is absorbed if it can promote an electron from a filled valence state to an empty conduction state, which requires Eph ≥ Eg. Silicon has Eg = 1.1 eV, so a 2.48 eV green photon is absorbed and its excess 1.38 eV passes to lattice vibrations as the excited electron relaxes to the band edge. Gallium nitride, with Eg = 3.4 eV, cannot absorb that photon at all, and transmits it. Below its gap a semiconductor is transparent. This is the regime of photodetectors, solar cells and light-emitting diodes, and it returns in M21.

At X-ray and gamma energies the wavelength falls below the interatomic spacing — 0.235 nm between nearest neighbours in silicon — and the collective description fails. A 1 MeV photon has a wavelength of 1.2 pm, roughly two hundred times smaller than the atomic spacing. It interacts with one atomic electron, or with the field of one nucleus, as an isolated target. The band structure does not enter: the 1.1 eV silicon gap lies six orders of magnitude below the photon energy and is invisible to it. What determines the interaction is the atomic number Z of the atoms present and how many of them lie in the path.

The quantity that predicts high-energy photon absorption

For X-rays and gamma rays a material is characterised by its atomic number and by the mass it presents per unit area, not by its bandgap, its crystal structure or its doping. Amorphous silicon dioxide and crystalline quartz of equal density attenuate identically, and so do a silicon wafer and the same mass of powdered silicon. Concrete stops gamma rays because it is thick and dense, not because of any electronic property of concrete.

Crystal orientation is irrelevant at these energies for the same reason. It matters a great deal for low-energy ions, where a beam aligned with an open crystallographic direction can channel and penetrate far deeper than the same ion arriving in a random direction — an effect M13 returns to, since it is one of the things a binary-collision simulation must be told about. Doping is negligible in both regimes: a heavy doping level of 10¹⁸ cm⁻³ sits more than four orders of magnitude below the 5.0×10²² cm⁻³ atomic density of silicon, so dopant atoms are far too few to contribute measurably to the total cross-section.

A photon crossing a solid can therefore pass many atoms without interacting, and usually does. The atom is mostly empty space, and the chance of an interaction is described by a cross-section: an effective target area, per atom, that the photon must strike. Section 7 converts that cross-section into a mean free path, and shows that for a 1 MeV photon in silicon it is 6.7 cm — roughly ninety times the thickness of a standard 200 mm wafer.

3 · Photoelectric absorption

In photoelectric absorption the photon is destroyed. All of its energy passes to a single bound atomic electron, which is ejected from the atom with whatever kinetic energy remains after the binding energy is paid. Nothing of the photon survives, which distinguishes this process from Compton scattering. Pair production, treated in §5, also destroys the photon; what separates it from photoelectric absorption is that the energy creates a particle–antiparticle pair rather than freeing one bound electron.

Te = Eph − Eb
(10.3)
Te kinetic energy of the ejected photoelectron (eV) · Eph incident photon energy (eV) · Eb binding energy of the electron in its shell (eV). The process is possible only when Eph ≥ Eb, and the recoil of the whole atom absorbs the momentum imbalance.

The electron must be bound for the process to occur at all. A photon cannot be absorbed by a free electron, because energy and momentum cannot both be conserved in that two-body outcome. The nucleus, bound to the electron, takes up the excess momentum; its share of the energy is negligible because it is thousands of times more massive. For this reason photoelectric absorption is strongly favoured by the most tightly bound electrons available — the K shell supplies most photoelectric events once the photon energy exceeds the K binding energy — typically about 80 % in heavy elements and around 90 % in silicon, a fraction that can be read directly off the size of the absorption-edge step described below.

Lecture 6 takes the silicon binding energy as 99.4 eV, so a photon above about 100 eV, corresponding to λ = 12.4 nm in the extreme ultraviolet, can already eject an electron. That figure is the Si 2p3/2 level, and it is the correct one to use here: it is also silicon's L₃ absorption edge, tabulated at 99.42 eV. Its spin–orbit partner L₂ (2p1/2) sits 0.4 eV higher at 99.82 eV, so the L₂,₃ edge is a closely spaced doublet rather than a single step, and the lower member sets the onset. It is worth being explicit that the same 99.4 eV appears in M08 in a role it does not fill, where the quantity needed is the mean electron–hole pair creation energy of 3.6 eV. Binding energy sets which photons can be absorbed; pair creation energy sets how much charge the deposited energy liberates.

Absorption edge

An absorption edge is the photon energy at which a new shell becomes accessible, and it appears in tabulated data as a discontinuous jump. Silicon's K edge is at 1.839 keV: just below it the mass attenuation coefficient is 309 cm²/g, and just above it 3192 cm²/g, a factor of 10.3 across zero change in energy. The jump occurs because the two K electrons, previously inaccessible, become available targets. This is why a NIST table lists the same energy on two successive rows, once below the edge and once above.

The vacancy left behind does not persist. An electron from a higher shell falls into it, and the energy difference leaves either as a characteristic X-ray of the element — the same line spectrum produced by an X-ray tube anode in M09 — or as an Auger electron, in which the energy is given instead to another bound electron that is ejected. Low-Z materials such as silicon favour Auger emission; high-Z materials favour fluorescence. Either way, a single photoelectric absorption in silicon deposits essentially all of the photon energy locally, because the photoelectron range stays well below a die thickness: under 1 µm at the 10 keV energies used for X-ray dose testing, and about 20 µm even at the top of silicon's photoelectric range near 50 keV. A silicon Auger electron, at roughly 1.6 keV, travels under 0.1 µm.

The cross-section for photoelectric absorption per atom scales approximately as Z⁴ to Z⁵ and as Eph⁻³. Both exponents are large, and together they confine the process to low photon energies and favour heavy elements. The Z dependence follows from the requirement that the electron be tightly bound, which is a stronger condition in a heavy atom; the energy dependence follows from the growing mismatch between the photon momentum and the momentum the bound electron can supply as the photon energy rises.

4 · Compton scattering

In Compton scattering the photon is not destroyed. It collides with an atomic electron that is weakly enough bound to be treated as free, gives up part of its energy and momentum, and continues in a new direction with lower energy and therefore longer wavelength. Two particles leave the interaction: a recoil electron and a scattered photon.

Requiring energy and momentum to be conserved in the collision of a photon with a stationary free electron fixes the wavelength change completely, and it depends only on the scattering angle — not on the incident wavelength, and not on the material.

Δλ = λ′ − λ = hmec (1 − cos θ)
(10.4)
Δλ wavelength increase of the scattered photon (m) · λ, λ′ incident and scattered wavelength (m) · θ scattering angle of the photon measured from its original direction (rad or deg) · me electron rest mass, 9.109×10⁻³¹ kg. The group h/mec = 2.426 pm is the Compton wavelength of the electron, and it is the shift obtained at θ = 90°. Δλ grows monotonically with θ, so the largest possible shift is at θ = 180° and is twice that, 4.853 pm.

Written in energy rather than wavelength, the same result gives the energy of the scattered photon directly, which is the more useful form for dose calculations.

E′ = Eph1 + (Eph ⁄ mec²)(1 − cos θ)
(10.5)
E′ energy of the scattered photon (MeV) · Eph incident photon energy (MeV) · θ photon scattering angle · mec² electron rest energy, 0.511 MeV — both energies must be in the same unit. The recoil electron takes Eph − E′. Valid for a free electron at rest; binding matters only when Eph approaches the shell binding energy.

The electron energy is largest for a backscattered photon, θ = 180°, and this maximum is called the Compton edge. For the 1.25 MeV average of a cobalt-60 source, equation (10.5) gives a backscattered photon of 0.212 MeV and a recoil electron of 1.04 MeV. The photon can never transfer all of its energy in a single Compton event: some always leaves with the scattered photon. That surviving photon is the reason a shield designed from attenuation alone underestimates what arrives at the far side, treated in §7.

Compton scattering happens on individual electrons, so the cross-section per atom is proportional to the number of electrons in the atom, which is Z. Expressed per unit mass rather than per atom, that proportionality becomes Z/A, the ratio of atomic number to atomic weight. For every element except hydrogen, Z/A lies between about 0.39 and 0.50. It is close to 0.50 for the light elements — silicon gives 14/28.09 = 0.498 and oxygen 8/16.00 = 0.500 — and falls slowly to about 0.40 for the heaviest, lead being 0.396 and uranium 0.387.

Why almost all light materials attenuate 1 MeV photons equally

Where Compton scattering dominates, the mass attenuation coefficient is nearly independent of which element is present, because it follows Z/A and that ratio barely varies. At 1 MeV the tabulated coefficients are 6.361×10⁻² cm²/g for silicon and 6.372×10⁻² cm²/g for oxygen, differing by 0.2 %. Per gram, silicon, oxygen, carbon and aluminium are the same shield at this energy to within about 3 %, and only density and thickness distinguish them — which is exactly what §8 finds when silicon is compared with lead. Water is the instructive exception: it is 11 % hydrogen by mass, which lifts its effective Z/A to 0.555 and its coefficient to 7.072 × 10⁻² cm²/g, 11 % above silicon's. The hydrogen exception applies to hydrogen-bearing compounds, not only to elemental hydrogen.

5 · Pair production

Above a threshold energy a photon can convert entirely into matter, creating an electron and a positron. The positron is the antiparticle of the electron: identical rest mass, opposite charge. The process destroys the photon and produces two charged particles that then behave as the charged particles of M08 and M09 do.

Three conservation laws fix the conditions. Charge conservation requires that the created particles carry zero net charge, so an electron of charge −q must be accompanied by a partner of charge +q. Energy conservation requires the photon to supply at least the rest energy of both particles. Applying E = mc² to the electron gives mec² = 9.109×10⁻³¹ × (2.998×10⁸)² = 8.19×10⁻¹⁴ J, which is 0.511 MeV, and the positron has the same rest mass and therefore the same rest energy.

Eph ≥ 2mec² = 1.022 MeV
(10.6)
Eph incident photon energy (MeV) · mec² electron rest energy, 0.511 MeV. A photon of exactly 1.022 MeV has λ = 1.21 pm. Any energy above the threshold appears as kinetic energy shared between the electron and the positron. This threshold applies to pair production in the field of a nucleus; the same process in the field of an atomic electron, called triplet production, requires 4mec² = 2.044 MeV and is much rarer.

Momentum conservation is what forces the interaction to occur near a nucleus rather than in free space. A photon of energy E carries momentum E/c, whereas an electron–positron pair of the same total energy carries less than that, because part of their energy is locked in rest mass. The difference must go somewhere, and it goes to the nucleus, which recoils. The nucleus is thousands of times more massive than the pair, so it absorbs the momentum while taking almost none of the energy — but without it, the process cannot occur at all. The available kinetic energy is shared between the electron and positron in proportions that vary from event to event; an equal split is the average outcome, not a requirement.

Where the course's momentum argument stops holding

Deck 6 p. 14 argues from p = mv that the electron and positron must leave with the same velocity. The conclusion does not follow. The pair shares momentum with the recoiling nucleus, so the two particles' momenta are not equal in magnitude, and the kinetic energy is divided differently in every event — an equal split is the average, not the rule. Both particles are in fact emitted close to the incident photon's direction, within an angle of order mec²/E, so their momenta are nearly parallel rather than opposed. Switching to the relativistic p = γm0v neither rescues nor refutes the argument, because equal rest masses carrying equal-magnitude momenta would still give equal speeds; what breaks it is that the momenta are unequal. The conclusion the course draws — that a partner particle of equal mass and opposite charge must exist — is correct and rests on charge and energy conservation, which are not affected.

The positron survives only until it meets an electron, which in a solid takes a few hundred picoseconds after it slows down — about 220 ps in bulk silicon. The two annihilate, and their combined rest energy leaves as two photons of 0.511 MeV emitted in opposite directions. Those annihilation photons are themselves penetrating and go on to interact by the mechanisms of §3 and §4, so a single high-energy photon can seed a small cascade. The cross-section for pair production per atom scales as Z², because the process depends on the Coulomb field of the nucleus, and it rises slowly with energy above threshold rather than falling like the other two.

6 · Which interaction dominates

All three processes are always available to a photon above the relevant thresholds, and the total probability of an interaction is the sum of the three. Which one accounts for most of that sum depends on photon energy and on the atomic number of the target, and the dependences of the previous three sections determine the pattern completely.

Photoelectric absorption carries the strongest Z dependence, Z⁴ to Z⁵, and the strongest falling energy dependence, E⁻³, so it owns the low-energy corner and extends to much higher energies in heavy elements — to 56.9 keV in silicon but to 521 keV in gold. Compton scattering scales only as Z per atom and falls slowly with energy, so it occupies the middle band across all elements. Pair production scales as Z², begins at 1.022 MeV and grows with energy, so it owns the high-energy corner and reaches down to lower energies in heavy elements.

Two consequences follow for this course. In silicon, every photon energy used for testing electronics falls in the Compton band: the gamma rays of cobalt-60 at 1.17 and 1.33 MeV are far above the photoelectric crossover at 56.9 keV and far below the pair crossover at 14.3 MeV. Photoelectric absorption contributes 0.04 % of silicon's total cross-section at 1.25 MeV, and pair production 0.06 %; Compton scattering accounts for essentially all of it. Lead and gold are Compton-dominated at the same energies, but not so completely: about 20 % of their attenuation is residual photoelectric absorption, because their crossover sits at 521 keV for gold and 556 keV for lead rather than at 57 keV. That residual photoelectric term is what makes a high-Z shield behave differently from the silicon it protects. Pair production contributes 0.6 % even in lead at these energies and explains nothing.

7 · Attenuation, and reading the coefficient tables

Because a photon either interacts or does not, and because the chance of interacting in a thin layer is the same wherever that layer sits, the surviving intensity falls exponentially with thickness. Each atom presents a cross-section σ, there are n atoms per unit volume, and the product nσ is the probability of interaction per unit path length.

I(x) = I0 e−µx,   µ = nσ = (µρ) ρ
(10.7)
I intensity of photons that have not interacted (any consistent unit) · I0 incident intensity · x thickness (cm) · µ linear attenuation coefficient (cm⁻¹) · n atoms per unit volume (cm⁻³) · σ total cross-section per atom (cm²) · µ/ρ mass attenuation coefficient (cm²/g) · ρ density (g/cm³). The course works in centimetres throughout this equation, so a thickness given in micrometres must be converted: 1 µm = 10⁻⁴ cm.

Dividing by density is what makes tabulation practical. The linear coefficient µ changes if the same material is compressed; the mass coefficient µ/ρ does not, because it counts interactions per gram of material rather than per centimetre. A single table of µ/ρ against energy therefore serves every physical form of an element. The National Institute of Standards and Technology publishes these tables for every element and for many compounds, and they are the source Lecture 6 uses.

The probability that a given photon does interact in thickness x is the complement of the surviving fraction.

P = 1 − e−(µ/ρ)ρx
(10.8)
P probability that one photon interacts somewhere in the thickness (dimensionless, 0 to 1) · µ/ρ mass attenuation coefficient (cm²/g) · ρ density (g/cm³) · x thickness (cm). The exponent (µ/ρ)ρx is dimensionless. When the exponent is much smaller than 1 the probability is approximately equal to the exponent itself, but the two diverge as soon as the exponent approaches 0.05, so the exponential form should be evaluated rather than assumed small.

For a compound or mixture, the mass coefficient is the weighted sum of the elemental coefficients, with each element weighted by the fraction of the total mass it contributes. This is the Bragg additivity rule, and it holds because at these energies each atom interacts independently of the chemical bonds around it.

(µρ) = Σ wi (µρ)i
(10.9)
wi mass fraction of element i in the compound (dimensionless, summing to 1) · (µ/ρ)i mass attenuation coefficient of pure element i at the same photon energy (cm²/g). Mass fractions are computed from atomic weights, not from the number of atoms: for SiO₂, wSi = 28.09/(28.09 + 2×16.00) = 0.467 and wO = 32.00/60.09 = 0.533.

Applying this to the gate and field oxide of an integrated circuit at 1 MeV gives (µ/ρ) = 0.467 × 6.361×10⁻² + 0.533 × 6.372×10⁻² = 6.367×10⁻² cm²/g. The two elemental coefficients are almost identical, so the weighting changes nothing; that is the Z/A result of §4 appearing again. A compound only shows a significantly different coefficient from its major constituent when one of its elements is much heavier, or when the photon energy is low enough for the Z⁴–Z⁵ photoelectric term to dominate.

The reciprocal of the linear coefficient is the mean free path, the average distance a photon travels between interactions.

λmfp = 1µ
(10.10)
λmfp mean free path (cm) · µ linear attenuation coefficient (cm⁻¹). After one mean free path the uncollided intensity has fallen to 1/e = 37 % of its initial value. Do not confuse this with the range of a charged particle: a photon beam has no maximum depth, only a characteristic one.

The two coefficient columns, and the duplicated edge rows

A NIST table lists two coefficients at each energy. µ/ρ is the mass attenuation coefficient: it counts every event that removes a photon from the beam, including a Compton scatter that merely deflects it. µtr/ρ, the mass energy-transfer coefficient, counts the energy handed to secondary electrons as kinetic energy. µen/ρ, the mass energy-absorption coefficient, is that quantity reduced by the fraction those electrons go on to radiate away as bremsstrahlung, µen/ρ = (1 − g)µtr/ρ, and it is the one that gives absorbed dose. In light elements at MeV energies g is a few tenths of a percent and the two are interchangeable; in lead at 10 MeV g exceeds 20 %. All three differ wherever scattering carries energy out of the volume: in silicon at 1 MeV, µ/ρ and µen/ρ are 6.361×10⁻² and 2.778×10⁻² cm²/g, so under half of the energy removed from the beam is deposited locally.

Two rows at the same energy, with the second marked K, straddle an absorption edge as described in §3. Use the lower value for photons below the edge and the upper for photons above it. Attenuation problems use µ/ρ; dose problems use µen/ρ.

Equation (10.7) describes photons that have not interacted at all. In a real shield of appreciable thickness, photons that scattered once and survived still emerge, and they still carry energy. Accounting for them requires a buildup factor, an empirical multiplier greater than 1 that depends on the material, the photon energy and the shield thickness in mean free paths.

Itotal = B · I0 e−µx
(10.11)
Itotal total intensity arriving, including scattered photons · B buildup factor (dimensionless, ≥ 1) · other symbols as in (10.7). B = 1 for a narrow, well-collimated beam, and grows with shield thickness; for several mean free paths of a low-Z shield it reaches values of several units. The uncollided form is a lower bound on what gets through.

Geometry enters the exponent through path length rather than through the law itself. A photon crossing a slab of thickness x at angle θ to the normal travels x/cos θ of material, so the exponent grows and the transmitted fraction falls. The exponential form is unchanged, because it follows from a constant interaction probability per unit path length and not from any assumption about the shape of the absorber.

8 · Silicon and lead compared

The two worked cases of Lecture 6 apply equation (10.8) to a 600 µm thickness — the figure the deck adopts as a representative die, a little below the 725 µm to which a 200 mm wafer is actually polished — for a 1 MeV photon. Together they answer why shielding is built from lead and why a chip does not shield itself.

For silicon, µ/ρ = 6.361×10⁻² cm²/g and ρ = 2.33 g/cm³, giving µ = 0.1482 cm⁻¹. With x = 600 µm = 0.06 cm the exponent is 8.89×10⁻³ and P = 1 − e−0.00889 = 8.85×10⁻³. Fewer than nine photons in a thousand interact anywhere in the die. The mean free path from equation (10.10) is 6.7 cm, a hundred times the wafer thickness.

For lead, µ/ρ = 7.102×10⁻² cm²/g and ρ = 11.35 g/cm³, giving µ = 0.806 cm⁻¹, an exponent of 4.84×10⁻² and P = 4.72×10⁻². The same 600 µm of lead interacts with 4.7 % of the beam, 5.3 times as many photons as silicon does.

Two arithmetic points in the worked slides

Deck 6 p. 24 prints 8.63×10⁻³ for silicon; evaluating the expression written on the slide gives 8.85×10⁻³. Deck 6 p. 25 prints 4.84×10⁻² for lead, which is the value of the exponent (µ/ρ)ρx rather than of 1 − e−(µ/ρ)ρx; the probability is 4.72×10⁻². The slide also states that lead interacts 20 times more than silicon, and Lecture 6 states about ten times. The ratio of the two results is 5.3. Neither the tabulated coefficients nor the conclusion is affected — lead is decisively the better absorber — but the size of its advantage at 1 MeV is a factor of about five, and the reason is worth stating precisely.

Lead's advantage at 1 MeV is almost entirely density. The mass coefficients differ by only 12 %, 7.102×10⁻² against 6.361×10⁻² cm²/g, and the density ratio, 11.35/2.33 = 4.87, supplies the rest. Per gram of material, lead at 1 MeV is scarcely better than silicon. The near-equality of the two mass coefficients is a cancellation rather than a similarity: lead's Z/A is 0.396 against silicon's 0.498, so on Compton scattering alone lead would be 21 % weaker per gram, and the residual photoelectric term — a fifth of lead's attenuation at this energy against a twentieth of a percent of silicon's — more than makes up the difference. The Z/A argument of §4 holds between light elements; it does not reach from silicon to lead.

The picture changes completely at lower photon energy, where photoelectric absorption and its Z⁴–Z⁵ dependence take over in lead but not in silicon. At 100 keV, µ/ρ is 0.1835 cm²/g for silicon and 5.549 cm²/g for lead, a ratio of 30 per gram and 147 per centimetre. The same 600 µm of lead now removes 98 % of the beam, against 2.5 % for silicon. That is the regime in which lead earns its reputation: a few millimetres of it line an X-ray cabinet. At cobalt-60 energies lead is still the most thickness-efficient shield available — its tenth-value layer is about 4 cm against 21 cm for ordinary concrete — but its per-gram advantage has gone, so shielding a cobalt-60 source is a mass problem whichever material is chosen. That is why such a cell is built from tens of centimetres of lead, or from metres of concrete where mass is cheap and structural.

9 · Cobalt-60 and the choice of test energy

Cobalt-60 is the laboratory gamma standard introduced in M04, and Lecture 6 returns to it to show where its gamma rays come from. The nuclide has 27 protons and 33 neutrons. It is unstable because that ratio is neutron-rich for its mass, and it decays by beta-minus emission with a half-life of 5.27 years: a neutron in the nucleus converts to a proton, emitting an electron and an antineutrino, and the nucleus becomes nickel-60 with 28 protons.

A slip in the deck's nucleon count

Deck 6 p. 16 lists cobalt-60 as having 28 protons and 33 neutrons, which totals 61. Cobalt is element 27, and the decay scheme printed on that same slide labels the parent correctly as 6027Co. The daughter, nickel, is element 28. Lecture 6 gave 28 protons and 32 neutrons, which totals 60 but assigns the proton number of the daughter to the parent.

The beta decay does not leave nickel-60 in its ground state. It populates an excited nuclear level, and the nucleus then drops through two lower levels in a cascade, emitting one gamma ray at each step: 1.173 MeV followed by 1.333 MeV. These two gamma rays, not the beta particle, are what a cobalt-60 irradiator delivers to a part under test, because the beta particle is stopped by the stainless-steel source capsule while the gamma rays pass through it.

The energy bookkeeping closes exactly. The total energy released per decay is 2.823 MeV, and it divides into the beta endpoint energy of 0.317 MeV, shared between the electron and the antineutrino, plus the two gamma rays: 0.317 + 1.173 + 1.333 = 2.823 MeV. The mass-difference argument of Deck 6 p. 16 points in the right direction — the energy does come from a mass deficit — but the specific subtraction it performs, mn − me − mp = 0.000840 u, is 0.782 MeV, which is the decay energy of a free neutron and is neither 1.173 nor 1.333 MeV. The gamma energies are set by the spacing of nuclear energy levels in nickel-60, in the same way that characteristic X-ray energies are set by atomic level spacings.

Pair production is open at cobalt-60 energies but does not occur

Deck 6 p. 16 marks the 1.173 and 1.333 MeV gamma rays as leading to pair production, and Lecture 6 states that a cobalt-60 source produces many pairs in a part under test. Both energies do exceed the 1.022 MeV threshold of equation (10.6), so the process is not forbidden. But the cross-section immediately above threshold is negligible, and Figure 7 places these energies deep inside silicon's Compton band, more than a decade below the energy at which pair production overtakes Compton scattering in silicon. Essentially every cobalt-60 interaction in silicon is a Compton scatter. Cobalt-60 is the standard total-dose source because its gamma rays are penetrating enough to dose a packaged die uniformly, and because the isotope is cheap, long-lived and available as a reactor by-product — not because of pair production.

10 · What this means for a part in orbit

Sections 7 and 8 establish that a photon crossing a die almost always crosses it untouched. That is the fact that shapes photon effects on electronics. Damage does not come from photons directly; it comes from the secondary electrons that the rare interaction sets in motion. A photoelectric absorption releases one energetic electron, a Compton scatter releases one and degrades the photon, and a pair production releases two. Those electrons are charged, and from that point onward the mechanisms of M08 apply: they ionise along their tracks, generating electron–hole pairs at 3.6 eV each in silicon and 17 eV each in silicon dioxide.

The low interaction probability is therefore a feature of a test source rather than a limitation. Because a 1 MeV photon has a 6.7 cm mean free path in silicon, the generation rate is very nearly uniform through a packaged part: the front of the die and the back of the die receive the same dose. An alpha or low-energy proton source cannot do that, because its range is comparable to the die thickness and the dose falls steeply with depth. Uniformity is why cobalt-60 became the total-dose standard, and total ionizing dose is the subject of M14.

Interfaces are the exception to uniformity. Where a low-Z material meets a high-Z one — silicon against a gold bond pad, or a die against a Kovar or ceramic package — the high-Z material generates more secondary electrons per unit mass, and those electrons cross into the neighbouring low-Z material. The dose within a few micrometres of the interface can exceed the bulk value by a substantial factor. This dose enhancement is strongest at photon energies where the Z dependence is strongest, which is the photoelectric region, and it is one reason a part tested in a low-energy X-ray cabinet does not always behave like the same part tested at cobalt-60 energies.

An aluminium shield 2 mm thick is placed in front of a silicon part in a 1 MeV gamma field. Aluminium has µ/ρ = 6.146×10⁻² cm²/g and ρ = 2.70 g/cm³. What fraction of the beam does it remove, and what does that suggest about shielding against gamma rays with structural aluminium?

The exponent is (6.146×10⁻²)(2.70)(0.20) = 3.32×10⁻², so the interacting fraction is 1 − e−0.0332 = 3.3×10⁻², about 3 %. The mean free path is 1/(6.146×10⁻² × 2.70) = 6.0 cm, so 2 mm is one thirtieth of a mean free path.

Structural aluminium of the thickness a spacecraft actually carries does almost nothing against 1 MeV gamma rays. Reducing the uncollided beam by a factor of ten requires ln(10) = 2.3 mean free paths, which is 14 cm of aluminium — a mass no mission would fly. The real reduction is worse than that, because at 2.3 mean free paths in a low-Z material the buildup factor of equation (10.11) is several units, so the dose behind the shield falls by a factor of two to four rather than ten. Spacecraft shielding is effective against electrons and low-energy protons, whose behaviour is governed by range rather than by attenuation, and it is largely ineffective against penetrating gamma rays. The same reasoning recurs in M16.

Summary

  • A photon carries E = hc/λ and momentum h/λ. The shortcut hc = 1239.8 eV·nm converts either way in one step.
  • Below a few electronvolts, absorption is decided by the bandgap of the solid. Above about 100 eV it is decided by the atomic number and the mass per unit area, and the bandgap is irrelevant.
  • Photoelectric absorption destroys the photon and ejects a bound electron; σ ∝ Z⁴–Z⁵ and E⁻³. Absorption edges appear where a new shell opens; silicon's K edge is 1.839 keV.
  • Compton scattering transfers part of the energy to an electron and the photon survives with longer wavelength, Δλ = 2.426 pm × (1 − cos θ). Per atom σ ∝ Z, so per gram the coefficient follows Z/A ≈ 0.5 and barely varies between light elements.
  • Pair production needs 1.022 MeV and a nearby nucleus to absorb momentum; σ ∝ Z² and grows with energy. The positron annihilates into two 0.511 MeV photons.
  • In silicon, photoelectric gives way to Compton at 56.9 keV and Compton to pair production at 14.3 MeV. Cobalt-60 gamma rays at 1.173 and 1.333 MeV are Compton-dominated. In gold and lead the photoelectric crossover is near 520–556 keV, so a fifth of their attenuation at Co-60 energies is still photoelectric.
  • I = I0e−µx with µ = (µ/ρ)ρ; interaction probability is 1 − e−µx; mean free path is 1/µ. Compounds use mass-weighted sums of elemental coefficients.
  • Use µ/ρ for attenuation and µen/ρ for dose; they differ by more than a factor of two in silicon at 1 MeV. A thick shield also needs a buildup factor, and the plain exponential is a lower bound on what gets through.
  • Only 0.89 % of a 1 MeV beam interacts in a 600 µm silicon die, against 4.7 % in the same thickness of lead. At 100 keV the lead figure rises to 98 % while silicon reaches only 2.5 %: lead's advantage is photoelectric, and it collapses at MeV energies where only its density helps.
